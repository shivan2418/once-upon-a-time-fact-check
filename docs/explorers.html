Episode 1

The First Navigators

10 issues

The episode surveys the earliest seafaring, from the first sailing boats in Egypt and Mesopotamia through Minoan Crete, the Thera eruption and the Phoenician–Mycenaean rivalry. It then follows three ancient voyages: Pharaoh Necho II's Phoenician circumnavigation of Africa, Hanno of Carthage's trip down the West African coast, and Pytheas of Massalia's journey north to Thule. It closes with the Greek discovery that the Earth is a sphere, from Pythagoras to Eratosthenes, and uses this to show how limited ancient geography was.

  1. OutdatedE01 · 0:46medium confidence
    "In Egypt, the oldest examples of sailing craft go back 3,500 years before the common era."

    TodayEgyptian pictures of sailing boats from about 3500–3200 BCE (Naqada II pottery) were the textbook "first sail" in 1996, though an Ubaid-period model boat from Eridu with a possible mast socket was already known. Excavations at H3 (As-Sabiyah, Kuwait) from 1998 onward, published by Robert Carter in 2002–06, found Ubaid-period boat remains and a painted disc showing a two-masted sailing boat from about 5500–5000 BCE. The oldest evidence for sail is now from the Gulf and Mesopotamia, about 1,500–2,000 years earlier than the date given. The next line, "That was in Mesopotamia", suggests the script pointed that way too.

  2. Wrong in its dayE01 · 2:59medium confidence
    "The Phoenicians, in what is now Lebanon, and the Mycenaeans, in Greece."

    TodayMinoan Crete's naval peak was about 2000–1450 BCE. The Phoenicians, as a distinct seafaring power in Tyre and Sidon, only appeared after the Bronze Age collapse, around 1200–1100 BCE, by which point Minoan Crete had long since passed to the Mycenaeans. Canaanite ports such as Byblos and Ugarit did trade with Crete and Egypt, so "Phoenician" can be read loosely as Bronze Age Canaanite. But showing Phoenicians fighting Crete for the seas "for centuries" is anachronistic.

  3. Wrong in its dayE01 · 3:12medium confidence
    "It took place on Thera, one of the islands of Crete."

    TodayThera (Santorini) is a separate island in the Cyclades, about 110 km north of Crete. Its town at Akrotiri was heavily influenced by Minoan culture but did not belong to Crete. The wording may be a dub slip for "near Crete".

  4. Common belief thenE01 · 4:27high confidence
    "This was the end of the great Minoan civilization of Crete."

    TodayThe Thera eruption did not end Minoan civilization. Archaeologists had known since the 1980s that it happened in Late Minoan IA, while the Cretan palaces carried on and were destroyed about a century or more later (Late Minoan IB, c. 1450 BCE). Mycenaean Greeks then took over Knossos. Since 1996, radiocarbon dates, especially the buried olive branch from Thera (Friedrich et al., 2006), put the eruption around 1600 BCE, which makes the gap even longer. The eruption probably hurt Minoan trade and coastal sites, but it was not the knockout blow.

    Why it was believedSpyridon Marinatos's 1939 theory that the eruption destroyed Minoan Crete, together with the Atlantis link, dominated popular books and TV documentaries for decades.

  5. Wrong in its dayE01 · 5:37high confidence
    "It's those Mycenaeans. They're better armed than we are."

    TodayHanno and Himilco sailed around the 5th century BCE (perhaps late 6th). Mycenaean civilization had disappeared about 600–700 years earlier, around 1100 BCE. Carthage's sea rivals then were the western Greeks (Phocaeans and Massalia, later Syracuse) and the Etruscans.

  6. OutdatedE01 · 14:45medium confidence
    "The Caramantes, no-bads, who roamed over North Africa."

    TodayThe Garamantes ("Caramantes" is a mishearing) were not roaming nomads. They ran a settled, urban, irrigated farming state in the Fezzan (Libya), with its capital at Garama (Germa) and underground water channels (foggaras). Charles Daniels' excavations had begun to show this, but David Mattingly's Fezzan Project (1997–2001) and later work firmly overturned the old nomad picture. Herodotus says they hunted the "cave-dwelling Ethiopians" in four-horse chariots, so "fought against another people" is fair.

  7. Common belief thenE01 · 16:58medium confidence
    "We're gonna be Pygmy's lunch, they'll chop us into the little chest."

    TodayHanno's Periplus does not mention Pygmies, and certainly not man-eating ones. It describes hostile people in animal skins who threw stones, and "hairy people" the interpreters called Gorillai, three of whose women the Carthaginians killed and skinned. Central African forest peoples (Mbuti, Aka, Baka and others) were never cannibals, and they lived far from the coast Hanno probably reached. The joke recycles the colonial "cannibal savages" cliché. It is a character speaking, not the narrator.

    Why it was believedAdventure stories, comics and colonial-era writing routinely showed Africans in general, and "Pygmies" in particular, as cannibals.

  8. Wrong in its dayE01 · 18:47medium confidence
    "Only this time, it was to the north. He encountered the Hyperboreans."

    TodayThe Hyperboreans were a mythical people "beyond the North Wind", linked to Apollo. Pytheas did not claim to have met them. In the fragments that survive, he describes the tin trade of Cornwall, the Britons and their grain and mead, and Thule six days' sail north of Britain. The narrator's explanation treats Hyperboreans as a real people. Herodotus himself doubted they existed (4.36).

  9. Wrong in its dayE01 · 22:21medium confidence
    "He says they're a night six months long!"

    TodayA six-month night happens only at the poles. Wherever Thule was (Iceland, the Norwegian coast or Shetland, at about 60–66°N), the winter night lasts at most about a day around the solstice, and in the far north of Norway a few weeks. The "six months" claim comes from Pliny (Natural History 4.104) and is not from Pytheas. The fragment Geminus quotes has Pytheas saying only that the summer nights there were two or three hours long. The show does explain correctly that the midnight sun is caused by the Earth's shape.

  10. Wrong in its dayE01 · 22:47high confidence
    "The Earth is round and surrounded by oceans, but Athens is here, right at the center."

    TodayThis turns Herodotus upside down. He mocked the Ionian maps that drew the Earth "round as if by a compass" with Ocean flowing all around it (4.36), and he said nobody knew whether Europe was bounded by sea to the north and east (4.45). He came from Halicarnassus, and Greeks put the world's centre at Delphi (the omphalos), not Athens. He also doubted the Hyperboreans and knew nothing of the Urals, though he did mention elephants and small men in Libya.

Simplified but fine (15)
  • The wheel (about 3500 BCE), the sail and writing (about 3200 BCE) did appear in Egypt and Mesopotamia within a few centuries of one another.
  • "Crete... rules the seas": the idea of a Minoan thalassocracy goes back to Thucydides. It is still debated but was a mainstream view.
  • Ulysses' ten-year voyage matches the Odyssey, which the show rightly presents as a story.
  • Necho II (reigned 610–595 BCE) sending Phoenicians around Africa from the Red Sea, with the voyage taking three years, follows Herodotus 4.42. He names no single captain, and historians still argue over whether it happened. About 25,000 km is a reasonable figure for the route.
  • Hanno of Carthage sailed about a century after Necho (c. 500 BCE, though the date is uncertain). The Periplus gives him 60 ships and colonists, a much bigger venture than the show's small crew. Himilco led a separate voyage north rather than serving as Hanno's captain.
  • Carthage sought West African gold (Herodotus describes the "silent trade") and closed the Strait of Gibraltar (the Pillars of Hercules) to Greek shipping.
  • The sailors' fears of a flat Earth with an edge, boiling water and sea monsters fit early Greek (Homeric) cosmology and the "torrid zone" idea. These were ancient sailors, not Columbus's crew, so this is not the Columbus flat-Earth myth.
  • Pythagoras is traditionally credited with a spherical Earth (Parmenides is the other candidate). "Nobody would believe him" overstates it, since educated Greeks generally accepted the sphere by Plato's and Aristotle's time.
  • Zephyros (the west wind) and Euros (the east wind) are named correctly.
  • The Sahara was green and pastoral in the African Humid Period (c. 9000–3000 BCE), well before Hanno's time.
  • The mutiny against Hanno is invented. In the Periplus the expedition turned back for lack of food. The crocodile-filled river and Black Africans (probably the Senegal region) are in the text.
  • Pytheas of Massalia, an astronomer who measured Marseille's latitude accurately with a gnomon, sailed about 325 BCE, closer to 150–200 years after Hanno than "a century later". His latitude work was precise, but his distances (such as Britain's circumference) were rough.
  • The midnight sun and polar winter explained by a spherical Earth is correct and well presented.
  • Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference about 240 BCE, roughly a century after Pytheas.
  • Thera's ash fell over a wide area, reaching Crete, Rhodes, Anatolia and the Nile delta. "Thousands of miles" overstates the distance (it reached about 1,000 km), but the area covered was vast.
Episode 2

Alexander the Great

5 issues

The episode follows Alexander from taming Bucephalus and his lessons with Aristotle, through his quarrel with Philip, to his campaign against Persia. It covers the Granicus, the Gordian knot, Issus, Tyre and Gaza, Egypt, Siwa and Alexandria, then Gaugamela, Babylon and Susa. It ends with his growing excesses, the killing of Philotas and Parmenio, and his death at 33, and presents him as a spreader of Greek thought who was also ruined by his passions.

  1. Wrong in its dayE02 · 11:51high confidence
    "It extended right to India, China, Russia, all the way to Egypt."

    TodayThe Achaemenid empire reached the Indus, Central Asia (Sogdiana) and Egypt. It never bordered or included China, and apart from a foothold north of the Caucasus it did not reach what is now Russia. The list of modern countries that follows (Afghanistan, Pakistan, Iran, Iraq, Syria, Israel, Lebanon, Turkey) is correct.

  2. Common belief thenE02 · 12:03medium confidence
    "The Persians could raise an army of a million men."

    Today"A million men" comes from ancient writers: Herodotus on Xerxes, and Arrian on Gaugamela. Modern historians estimate the largest Persian field armies of Alexander's time at roughly 50,000–120,000. The episode repeats the figure later ("He said he has a million soldiers"). The river-drinking story is Herodotus' and is fairly framed with "it was said".

    Why it was believedPopular retellings followed the ancient numbers, even though scholars had long rejected them.

  3. Wrong in its dayE02 · 14:27medium confidence
    "Ptolemy, you will take the right."

    TodayAt the Granicus (334 BC), and in all his major battles, Alexander led the Companion cavalry on the right wing himself, and Parmenio held the left. Ptolemy, son of Lagus, was not yet a senior commander. The sun-in-their-eyes reasoning is invented. The real story is that Parmenio advised waiting until dawn and Alexander attacked across the river at once, late in the day.

  4. Wrong in its dayE02 · 19:39high confidence
    "gaza was another hard nut to crack greek fire it was a novelty"

    Today"Greek fire" was invented about 1,000 years later, around AD 672, by the Byzantines (traditionally by Kallinikos). The defenders of Tyre in 332 BC did use fire ships and heated sand, but not Greek fire.

  5. Wrong in its dayE02 · 22:35medium confidence
    "And the faithful Parmenio will take the last enemy stronghold on the way to the fabulous Persepolis."

    TodayThe last obstacle before Persepolis was the Persian Gates, held by Ariobarzanes. Alexander took the pass himself in early 330 BC by outflanking it on a mountain path. Parmenio led the baggage and heavy troops by the slower main road.

Simplified but fine (17)
  • Bucephalus ("Cephalus") was afraid of his own shadow. This is Plutarch's anecdote.
  • Macedonia lay just north of the Greek city-states.
  • Philip hired Aristotle as tutor in 343 BC. The curved shadow of the Earth on the Moon in a lunar eclipse is Aristotle's real argument for a round Earth (On the Heavens).
  • Alexander claimed descent from Achilles through his mother and from Heracles through his father. Achilles was a mortal hero, not a god, but the line is the boy's boast and Aristotle corrects him.
  • Philip married Cleopatra Eurydice (337 BC), Attalus insulted Alexander, Philip drew his sword and fell "passing from couch to couch", and Alexander fled. All of this follows Plutarch.
  • Olympias' story that Zeus was Alexander's father, and the Diogenes "you're blocking the sun" exchange, are ancient anecdotes.
  • Sparta refused to join the League of Corinth ("not us Spartans").
  • Alexander crossed to Asia with about 32,000 infantry and 5,000 cavalry, close to the show's 30,000. The 100,000 Persians at the Granicus is Diodorus' figure.
  • Apelles (painter) and Lysippus (sculptor) were Alexander's official portraitists.
  • Gordion, Midas, Gordius' ox-cart, the oracle that whoever undid the knot would rule Asia, and Alexander cutting it are all in the ancient sources.
  • Issus (333 BC) followed Darius ("Sidereus") marching behind Alexander. The Damascus haul is close to Parmenio's list in Athenaeus: 329 concubine-musicians, 277 cooks and 46 garland-weavers.
  • Tyre and Gaza (332 BC), Alexandria (331 BC), the oracle of Zeus-Ammon at Siwa ("Sionat-Amon"), Gaugamela ("Abridis", that is, Arbela, 331 BC), the welcome at Babylon, and the surrender of Susa ("Caesar").
  • Alexander did see naphtha (oil) seeps in Babylonia, as Plutarch reports. The "black gold" line is a joke.
  • Philotas was executed and Parmenio assassinated (330 BC). The "you're not a god" scene suggests Cleitus (328 BC) or the proskynesis quarrel with Callisthenes.
  • Alexander died at Babylon in June 323 BC, a few weeks short of 33. The cause (fever such as malaria, or poison) is still unknown.
  • "Greatest empire ... ever known": Alexander's empire roughly matched the Achaemenid empire it took over.
  • Greek thought spread across the Hellenistic East and later shaped both Rome and Arabic philosophy. Rome itself grew outside Alexander's conquests.
Episode 3

Erik the Red

4 issues

The episode follows the Norse Atlantic voyages as told in the Icelandic sagas. Erik the Red is outlawed from Iceland, finds and names Greenland, and leads settlers there. Bjarni sights unknown land to the west, and Leif Eriksson then sails past Helluland and Markland to winter in Vinland. Thorvald is killed by natives, and Thorfinn Karlsefni's colonising expedition (with Freydís) fails after fighting with the "Indians". The lesson is that Norsemen reached America about five centuries before Columbus, though the show also calls them plunderers.

  1. Wrong in its dayE03 · 1:32medium confidence
    "But the eastern side of Iceland was completely unknown."

    TodayThe unknown lay to the west of Iceland, which is where Erik sails next. This is probably a dubbing or translation slip.

  2. Wrong in its dayE03 · 1:43medium confidence
    "In the year 964, Erik the Red was banished from Norway for murder"

    TodayAccording to the sagas, Erik's father Thorvald Asvaldsson was exiled from Norway for manslaughter around 960, and took his young son (born c. 950) with him to Iceland. Erik's own killings, and his outlawry, came later in Iceland. The episode gets that part right.

  3. Common belief thenE03 · 14:17medium confidence
    "This whole story about wild grapes is false,"

    TodayThe Master's claim that Old Norse vin means "grassy land" (meadow), so that Vinland was "pasture land" and not "wine land", is Helge Ingstad's theory. Philologists have largely rejected it. Vin (short vowel, "meadow") was an old place-name element that had stopped being used to form new names long before 1000, and the sagas and Adam of Bremen (c. 1075) both explain the name by wild grapes (vín, long vowel). Butternuts found in the Norse layers at L'Anse aux Meadows grow only further south, in the same areas as wild grapes, so the Norse did reach grape country around the Gulf of St Lawrence. The grape story is most likely genuine, and the show's "completely false" is the wrong way round.

    Why it was believedIngstad, who found L'Anse aux Meadows in 1960, popularised the meadow reading in widely read books, and many popular accounts repeated it.

  4. Wrong in its dayE03 · 20:37medium confidence
    "So Torgal and eight of his men set sail for Greenland,"

    TodayIn Erik the Red's Saga, Thorhall the Hunter left with nine men because of a quarrel over which way to search for Vinland. He left before the clashes with the natives, not out of fear of them. Westerly gales drove his ship to Ireland, where the crew were reportedly enslaved and killed. They did not land in Iceland. ("Iceland" may be a mishearing of "Ireland".)

Simplified but fine (14)
  • The Viking Age (late 8th to 11th century) included raids and trade down the Russian rivers and as far as Spain and Morocco (Nekor, 859).
  • By Erik's time the good land in Iceland had been claimed, but "overcrowded" overstates it. Around 982 he was outlawed for three years at the Thorsnes Thing and sailed west.
  • The earlier sailor blown west "over 50 years ago" is Gunnbjörn Ulfsson (c. 900–930), who sighted Greenland.
  • Erik rounded the southern tip of Greenland and explored the green fjords on the west coast. The sagas themselves say he chose the name "Greenland" to attract settlers.
  • 25 ships set out around 985/986 and 14 arrived. The sagas give no headcount, so "about 600" is an estimate ("400 ships" is probably a mishearing of "400 people").
  • The Greenland Norse lived mainly on livestock and seals rather than "plenty of fish".
  • Bjarni Herjólfsson ("Birni"/"Bernarni") was blown off course on his way from Iceland to his father in Greenland. He saw forested lands without going ashore and about 15 years later sold his ship to Leif ("Le"/"Lee"/"Lay").
  • In the saga Erik stayed home because he fell from his horse, not because of age.
  • The sunstone is mentioned in Norse texts. Its use for navigation is a plausible hypothesis that later experiments support, but it is not proven.
  • Helluland ("Ulan", flat-stone land) is usually identified with Baffin Island and Markland with Labrador. Vinland was around the Gulf of St Lawrence, with the base camp probably at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland. Tree rings later dated Norse woodcutting there to 1021.
  • Thorvald ("Zorbal") Eriksson was killed by an arrow in a clash with natives (skrælings).
  • Thorfinn Karlsefni ("Torfinn Alfni") married Gudrid, the widow of Leif's brother Thorstein. He led the largest colonising attempt, with livestock. The sagas mention quarrels over women, a first peaceful trade in cloth and milk, and later fighting. Freydís shaming the fleeing men is from the saga.
  • Karlsefni's attempt was the last serious colonisation, though Freydís made one more voyage and Greenlanders still fetched timber from Markland as late as 1347.
  • The show deserves credit for naming the Norse, not Columbus, and for its line about the "savages" being spoken by characters. It still frames "discovery" from the European side, even though the natives were already there.
Episode 4

Genghis Khan

1 issue

The episode tells how Temüjin, abandoned after his father Yesügei's death, recovers stolen horses with Bo'orchu, marries Börte, wins her back from the Merkits with Toghrul's help, and unites the steppe tribes to be proclaimed Genghis Khan. A fast chronology covers his conquests from Siberia to northern China, Khwarazm and the Caucasus, and it ends with the empire passing to Ögedei and a blessing for the young Kublai. It teaches how a poor nomad built one of history's largest empires through cunning, loyalty and military organisation.

  1. Wrong in its dayE04 · 23:57medium confidence
    "1209, the conquest of northern Tibet."

    TodayThe 1209 campaign was against Western Xia (Xi Xia), the Tangut kingdom in present-day Ningxia and Gansu. The Tangut spoke a Tibeto-Burman language, but their state was not Tibet, and Western Xia was not conquered until 1227. The Uyghurs also submitted in 1209. Tibet itself first came under Mongol control in 1240, under Ögedei's son Köten.

Simplified but fine (13)
  • Tatars poisoned Yesügei when Temüjin was about nine. Targutai ("Tagutai") of the Tayichiud then abandoned the family, which was left almost destitute. "Kinjit" is probably a mishearing of Borjigin, and "Kia" of Kiyad.
  • The family's eight horses were stolen and Temüjin recovered them with Bo'orchu ("Borchu"/"Boichu") of the Arulat ("Ariolettes"). This is the Secret History episode.
  • Temüjin was betrothed to Börte, daughter of Dei Sechen ("D.A. Session"), as a boy and married her at about 16. The sable coat (her dowry) went to Toghrul as a gift.
  • Toghrul ("Togra"/"Targrill"), Ong Khan of the Kereyids, was his father's sworn brother (anda) and helped him win Börte back from the Merkits ("Miguan"). The Kereyids were Nestorian Christians, which is a nice touch. Jamukha's role is left out.
  • He fought the Naimans ("Naaman"). The feigned retreat was a real Mongol tactic.
  • He was proclaimed Genghis Khan at the 1206 kurultai. Tengri, the Eternal Blue Sky, was the supreme deity.
  • He organised the army in decimal units, with about 95 mingghans, roughly 100,000 men. Discipline was harsh.
  • Bouvines (1214), where the French had about 1,300 knights, was indeed contemporary.
  • The forest peoples of southern Siberia submitted in 1207. The war against the Jin began in 1211, and Zhongdu (Beijing) fell in 1215. Only northern China (the Jin) was conquered, not the Song.
  • 1218 was Jebe's conquest of Qara Khitai (parts of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Xinjiang).
  • The Khwarazm war ("Kwanzaan") began in 1219, with Bukhara and Samarkand falling in 1220. Mongol numbers are uncertain (about 100,000–200,000) and Khwarazm had more men on paper.
  • Jebe and Subutai's raid through the Caucasus to the Kalka (1221–23).
  • Ögedei was named successor. Genghis reportedly took notice of the boy Kublai (Rashid al-Din).
Episode 5

Ibn Battuta

8 issues

This transcript (French) does not match the official title. Ibn Battuta never appears. After a short recap of Genghis Khan's conquests, it follows Niccolò, Maffeo and Marco Polo from Acre in 1271 across Persia, the Pamirs and the Gobi to Kublai Khan's court. It covers Marco's 17 years in Kublai's service, the return by sea escorting a princess to Persia, and the dictation of his book after his capture by the Genoese. Much of the dialogue quotes or paraphrases Le Devisement du monde directly.

  1. Wrong in its dayE05 · 4:06medium confidence
    "le Tibet en 1209" [Tibet in 1209]

    TodayThe 1209 campaign was against the Tangut kingdom of Western Xia, not Tibet. Tibet came under Mongol control only in 1240.

  2. Wrong in its dayE05 · 4:25medium confidence
    "En 1220, c'est la guerre contre le Khorasan qui a fait offense aux Mongols et refuse réparation." [In 1220 came the war against Khorasan, which had offended the Mongols and refused reparation.]

    TodayThe enemy was the Khwarazmian empire of Shah Muhammad II. Khorasan was one of its provinces. The offence was the massacre of a Mongol caravan at Otrar in 1218 and the killing of Genghis' envoy, and the war began in 1219.

  3. Wrong in its dayE05 · 5:30high confidence
    "Et si le pape veut bien m'envoyer six hommes sages de votre foi chrétienne," [And if the pope will send me six wise men of your Christian faith,]

    TodayIn Polo's text Kublai asks for "up to a hundred" (jusqu'à cent) learned Christians skilled in the seven arts. The episode lowers the number to six and builds the bargaining scene with the pope around it. The pope did in fact send only two friars, Nicholas of Vicenza and William of Tripoli, and they turned back in Armenia for fear of Baybars' Mamluk invasion. The episode gets that part right.

  4. Wrong in its dayE05 · 5:57medium confidence
    "quand ils l'ont quitté voilà deux ans." [when they left him two years ago.]

    TodayNiccolò and Maffeo left Kublai around 1266, reached Acre in 1269, and set out again in 1271 after the long papal vacancy, so they had left him about five years earlier. (It was the stay in Venice that lasted about two years.)

  5. Common belief thenE05 · 20:01high confidence
    "Il me semble qu'on pourrait essayer ces fameuses catapultes dont on nous avait parlé." [It seems to me we could try those famous catapults we were told about.]

    TodayPolo's book claims the Polos built the siege engines that took "Saianfu", which is Xiangyang (misheard "Shang-Fu"). The siege ended in early 1273, about two years before the Polos reached China. Chinese and Persian sources (Rashid al-Din, the Yuan Shi) credit two Muslim engineers sent by the Ilkhan Abaqa, Ismail and Ala al-Din.

    Why it was believedThe story comes from Polo's own text. Henry Yule had already shown the problem with the dates in 1871, but retellings kept the heroic version.

  6. Wrong in its dayE05 · 21:56medium confidence
    "et eu treize bateaux pour les mener à bon port." [and thirteen ships to bring them safely to port.]

    TodayPolo says Kublai fitted out fourteen four-masted ships for the voyage escorting the princess Kököchin to the Ilkhan Arghun (1291–93).

  7. Wrong in its dayE05 · 24:59medium confidence
    "Et à Angcheu, la capitale qu'il traverse, on peut admirer douze mille ponts de pierre," [And at Angcheu, the capital it flows through, you can admire twelve thousand stone bridges,]

    TodayPolo's "12,000 bridges" belongs to Quinsai (Hangzhou), the old Song capital, which stands on West Lake and the Grand Canal, not on the Yangtze. The episode joins his separate Yangtze passage ("the greatest river in the world", with its huge traffic of boats) to Hangzhou.

  8. Common belief thenE05 · 25:25medium confidence
    "Près de l'île de Curzola, sur un bateau véhicule." [Near the island of Curzola, on a ... ship.]

    TodayPolo's capture at the battle of Curzola (1298) comes from Ramusio's edition (1559), nearly 250 years later. Polo's book only says he was a prisoner in Genoa in 1298. Many historians think he was taken in a smaller clash, perhaps off Laiazzo (Ayas) in 1296.

    Why it was believedRamusio's account was the standard biography and Curzola made a better story.

Simplified but fine (15)
  • "1990" at the start is almost certainly a mishearing of 1190 (the Third Crusade era), and "Mme Kahn en 1200" a mishearing of the 1206 proclamation.
  • Dried dung as the steppe's only fuel is accurate.
  • Bouvines (1214) with about 1,000 French knights: the usual figure is about 1,300.
  • The "greatest joy is to cut enemies to pieces" saying is attributed to Genghis by Rashid al-Din. Its authenticity is uncertain, but the show presents it as his creed.
  • Genghis died in 1227. His birth year is uncertain (1155–1167), so "65" is one possible figure, and the fall from a horse is the Secret History's account, hedged here with "dit-on". Kublai was about 12 at the time.
  • Teobaldo Visconti of Piacenza was elected Gregory X while at Acre in 1271. The holy oil from the lamp of the Holy Sepulchre was requested by Kublai.
  • Polo describes oil near Baku in "Greater Armenia" rather than Mosul. The sea leg to Hormuz is a loose reading of an uncertain route.
  • The Pamirs ("l'un de nos couches"), the Gobi, and Kamul's (Hami, "Chamoule") hospitality all come from Polo.
  • The Mongol theft law (ninefold repayment or 7–107 blows, death by being cut in two for horse theft) is Polo's description.
  • The journey took about 3½ years. The Polos actually first met Kublai at Shangdu (Xanadu) in 1275, not Beijing.

Episode 18

Alexander von Humboldt

5 issues

The episode follows Alexander von Humboldt and Aimé Bonpland from their meeting in Paris in 1798 and Carlos IV's permission. It covers their Venezuelan journey (electric eels, the cow tree, the Apure, the Orinoco and the Casiquiare, Bonpland's fever), the Andes and the attempt on Chimborazo, the cold Pacific current, and the meeting with Jefferson in 1804. Throughout, it stresses Humboldt as a holistic "ecologist" and an outspoken opponent of slavery, ending with Kosmos and the Berlin revolution of 1848.

  1. Wrong in its dayE18 · 4:17medium confidence

  2. Wrong in its dayE18 · 13:58high confidence

    TodayThis is anachronistic in 1800. Ronald Ross showed that mosquitoes spread malaria in 1897. Humboldt's era blamed "miasma" (bad air) from swamps.

  3. Common belief thenE18 · 21:46medium confidence
    "Nobody has ever climbed higher."

    TodayHumboldt's party reached about 5,875 m on Chimborazo on 23 June 1802. That was the highest recorded climb by a European, and it stood for about 30 years. Centuries earlier, however, the Incas had climbed several Andean peaks over 6,000 m and built shrines on top: Llullaillaco (6,739 m), with summit ruins found in 1952, and Ampato, where the "Juanita" mummy was found in 1995. The "record" counts European climbers only.

    Why it was believedMountaineering history was written as a list of European "firsts", and Inca high-altitude archaeology was little known outside specialists.

  4. Wrong in its dayE18 · 23:30medium confidence
    "The human air rises over the mountains, the Andes going to the east."

    TodayThe explanation has the weather running the wrong way. The coastal desert of Peru exists because the cold Humboldt Current cools the air above it. That cool air sits stable under warmer air (an inversion), so it gives fog (garúa) but almost no rain. Moisture over the Andes comes from the east: trade winds carry it from the Atlantic and the Amazon, and it rains on the eastern slopes. Pacific air does not cross the Andes and rain on the other side. The coast lies in the Andes' rain shadow. ("human" is a mishearing of "humid".)

  5. Wrong in its dayE18 · 24:52high confidence
    "At the age of 90, Humboldt completed the last volume of his great work, Cosmos."

    TodayHumboldt died on 6 May 1859 at 89, before finishing Kosmos. Volume 4 appeared in 1858, and volume 5 was put together from his papers and published after his death (1862).
