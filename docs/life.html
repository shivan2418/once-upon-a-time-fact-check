Episode 1

The Cell Planet

7 issues

The episode tells the story of how the first cells formed in the "primal soup" and shows the main parts of a cell as a factory: the membrane, mitochondria that make energy, ribosome "assembly lines," lysosomes, and a nucleus whose chromosomes send RNA messengers with production orders. A skin injury sets off an urgent order for collagen, and a careless worker nearly sends out the master DNA instead of an RNA copy.

  1. Wrong in its dayE01 · 1:01high confidence
    "Approximately 5,000 million years ago, single-celled, the cell developed."

    TodayEarth is about 4,540 million years old, so cells cannot be 5,000 million years old. The oldest signs of life are roughly 3,500 to 3,800 million years old. Earth's age had been known since 1956.

  2. Common belief thenE01 · 3:10medium confidence
    "the liquid in which our cells bathe is almost identical to the seawater from which we originally come."

    TodayThe fluid around our cells is only about one third as salty as seawater (about 0.9% versus 3.5%), and its mix of minerals is also different. This was an old idea that had already been abandoned by 1987.

    Why it was believedArchibald Macallum's 1926 theory that body fluids preserve the saltiness of the ancient ocean was repeated for decades in biology textbooks and popular science as "we carry the sea inside us."

  3. OutdatedE01 · 3:38high confidence
    "Take the brain with its three layers. The first layer we inherited from the reptiles. The second, the Paleocortex, from the mammals. And the third, the Neocortex"

    TodayThis is Paul MacLean's "triune brain" idea. Brain scientists have rejected it. The brain did not grow by adding new layers on top of old ones. Reptiles and birds have structures that correspond to parts of our cortex, and all brain parts evolved together (comparative neuroanatomy from the 1990s onward, summarized by Cesario et al., 2020).

  4. Wrong in its dayE01 · 3:55high confidence
    "And the third, the Neocortex, the most important, developed very rapidly. It only took 500,000 years to form."

    TodayAll mammals have a neocortex, and it is well over 100 million years old. It did not appear in human evolution, and it did not form in 500,000 years.

  5. OutdatedE01 · 7:11high confidence
    "the human body has 60,000,000 million of them" (later "60,000,000,000", probably a dubbing or Whisper slip)

    TodayCareful counts give about 30 to 37 trillion human cells (Bianconi 2013; Sender, Fuchs and Milo 2016). That is about half the old 60 trillion estimate, which is exactly what the episode gives for 1987. ("60,000,000 million" is 60 trillion written in a way that cannot be misread whether "billion" means a thousand million or, in older British and French usage, a million million.)

  6. OutdatedE01 · 13:11medium confidence
    "The centriole, the command post that controls the unity or the division of the cell."

    TodayCentrioles help organize the machinery that pulls chromosomes apart. They do not decide when a cell divides. That decision is made by cell-cycle control proteins (cyclins and CDKs; Nobel Prize 2001). Some cells divide without any centrioles (plants, and fruit flies engineered without them, Basto 2006).

  7. Wrong in its dayE01 · 23:20medium confidence
    "every day 500,000 million of them vanish and are replaced at the rate of 50 million each second."

    TodayThe two numbers contradict each other: 50 million per second adds up to about 4,300 billion per day, not 500 billion. Measurements put turnover at about 330 billion cells a day (Sender and Milo 2021), which is about 4 million per second. The per-second figure is roughly 13 times too high.

Simplified but fine (7)
  • "Mitochondria, which are probably ancient bacteria": correct. This is now confirmed beyond reasonable doubt.
  • "Fingers used to be fish fins": correct in broad terms (see fossil fish-to-limb transitions such as Tiktaalik).
  • The tailbone ("Six" is a Whisper error for "coccyx") as "what remains of our monkey's tail": the tailbone really is what is left of a tail. The ancestor was an early primate, not a modern monkey.
  • Red blood cells "lost our nucleus so we can't reproduce": correct.
  • RNA messengers carrying copies of the instructions, with the master DNA staying in the nucleus: correct.
  • Proteins making up "half our body substance": roughly true for the body's dry weight, though not if water is counted.
  • Cell size of "one hundredth of a millimeter": a typical size.
Episode 2

Birth

1 issue

Hundreds of millions of sperm set out, and only a few reach the egg. The fertilized egg divides, implants in the womb, and becomes an embryo fed by the placenta. Newly made cells are sent off by type (bone, liver, muscle, eye and so on) to build the body, and the episode tracks the fetus's growth up to a 50 cm, 3.2 kg baby at nine months.

  1. OutdatedE02 · 11:01medium confidence
    "There are 200 different kinds of you"

    Today"About 200 cell types" was the standard textbook number. Since the 2010s, single-cell studies such as the Human Cell Atlas have found several hundred main types and thousands of subtypes. The real number depends on how finely you split them.

Simplified but fine (7)
  • Only a small share of sperm get past the cervix, many die on the way, and few reach the egg: correct.
  • "The neck of the womb closes up again to preserve the fertilized egg": the cervix does become sealed by a thicker mucus plug, driven by the hormone progesterone. The timing is compressed, but this is not an explicit falsehood.
  • Implantation within a few days, and the placenta forming from the outer membrane: correct.
  • Embryo sizes (6 mm at day 35, 12 mm at day 42, 20 mm at day 50): roughly right. Later growth figures in the transcript are garbled by Whisper.
  • "Collagen and ovalbumin right away": ovalbumin is egg-white protein, which humans do not make. This is probably a dubbing slip for "albumin," so it is not counted as an error.
  • "I need a U": RNA does use U (uracil) in place of DNA's T. Correct.
  • 50 cm and 3.2 kg at birth: typical.
Episode 3

The Body's Sentinels

4 issues

Young white blood cells go to "school" in the thymus. There they learn to tell enemies (viruses and bacteria) from friends (red cells, platelets, helpful gut bacteria), and to check the HLA "identity card" that marks the body's own cells. The episode introduces B cells that release antibodies, killer T cells, phagocytes, macrophages, basophils and interferon. It ends with a T-cell patrol fighting off a virus in the lung.

  1. Wrong in its dayE03 · 2:39medium confidence
    "every day 15,000 million white corpuscles come into the world"

    TodayThe bone marrow makes about 50 to 100 billion neutrophils a day, plus the other white cells. That is several times more than 15 billion, and estimates from the 1970s already showed this.

  2. Wrong in its dayE03 · 3:27medium confidence
    "Thymus, the school for white cells."

    TodayOnly T lymphocytes are trained in the thymus. B cells mature in the bone marrow, and neutrophils (the "polynuclears") and macrophages never go to the thymus. The episode shows all these cell types as pupils in the same school.

  3. Wrong in its dayE03 · 5:54high confidence
    "These are bacteria. ... They cause chickenpox, diphtheria, meningitis, and many other illnesses."

    TodayA virus (varicella-zoster) causes chickenpox, not a bacterium. This was known well before 1987. Diphtheria is bacterial. Meningitis can be caused by either bacteria or viruses.

  4. Wrong in its dayE03 · 13:57medium confidence
    "Those are basophiles. They fight allergies."

    TodayBasophils cause allergic symptoms rather than fight them. They release histamine, which drives allergic reactions.

Simplified but fine (9)
  • "We drop 2,000 a second" (antibodies per cell): this is the classic textbook figure for a plasma cell, and it is still quoted.
  • Each antibody fits only one kind of enemy, and the cells that make it multiply into clones when needed: correct.
  • Smallpox wiped out by vaccination: correct (eradication declared in 1980).
  • Helpful gut bacteria that aid digestion and make vitamins: correct.
  • Interferon as a fast messenger warning neighboring cells about a virus: correct.
  • "all the cells of the human body have an identical HLA mark. No other cell in the world has the same": an exaggeration, since identical twins share HLA and siblings can match (episode 4 relies on this). Red blood cells having no HLA because they lack a nucleus: essentially correct.
  • Plasma making up "half the blood": it is about 55%. Fine.
  • Immune cells destroying cells that turn cancerous ("transforming cells"): this idea, called immune surveillance, is still accepted.
  • The route from the jugular vein to the superior vena cava, right atrium ("oracle" is Whisper for "auricle") and lung: correct.
Episode 4

The Bone Marrow

4 issues

The bone marrow is shown as a factory that turns a "master cell" (stem cell) into red cells, white cells and platelets every day. A programming error in the chromosomes produces too many rogue white cells, and a girl is diagnosed with leukemia. She is treated by destroying her blood-forming cells and then receiving a bone marrow transplant from her HLA-matched brother, whose cells find their own way to her marrow.

  1. Wrong in its dayE04 · 1:03medium confidence
    "Every day, 200 billion red blood corpuscles, 15 billion white blood corpuscles, and 500 billion platelets are produced."

    Today200 billion red cells a day is correct. Platelet production is about 100 to 150 billion a day, so 500 billion is several times too high. (Episode 6's "half a billion" is the same figure in the older British long scale, where a billion is a million million, so the two episodes agree.) White cell production is about 50 to 100 billion a day or more, not 15 billion.

  2. Wrong in its dayE04 · 7:03medium confidence
    "Triction enzymes, ligase enzymes to work immediately. We've got to repair this at once" (Whisper: "Triction" = "Restriction"), and later "Calling restriction enzymes. It's absolutely essential to reduce production."

    TodayRestriction enzymes are bacterial defense tools that people use in the lab. Human cells do not use them to repair DNA or to control how many blood cells are made. Ligase does take part in DNA repair, so that part is correct.

  3. Wrong in its dayE04 · 17:31high confidence
    "The white blood cells attack the population of their own organism. ... Leukemia. The white cells are unbalanced. They've become attackers, not protectors."

    TodayLeukemia is a cancer of blood-forming cells, not white cells attacking the body. Abnormal, immature cells multiply out of control and crowd out normal blood production in the marrow. That crowding is what causes the anemia and low platelets. White cells attacking the body's own tissue is autoimmune disease, which is a different illness. This was well understood in 1987.

  4. Wrong in its dayE04 · 22:46high confidence
    "right ventricle pushes them into the pulmonary aorta"

    TodayThe right ventricle pumps into the pulmonary artery, also called the pulmonary trunk. The aorta leaves the left ventricle. This is a small naming error. (likely a translation/dubbing slip)

Simplified but fine (7)
  • A single "big master cell" giving rise to all blood cells: this is the hematopoietic stem cell. Correct.
  • If immune "programming" goes wrong, its weapons can be turned on the body, and "it's still a mystery": a fair description of autoimmunity, whose causes are still only partly understood.
  • Destroying the diseased marrow before the transplant, and the patient being unable to fight germs afterwards: correct.
  • The brother is a match because he has "the same HLA": siblings match about 25% of the time. Correct.
  • Injected marrow cells travel through the blood and settle in the bone marrow ("homing"): correct.
  • "Just a tiny little prick" for the brother: in 1987, donating bone marrow meant many needle draws from the hip bone under general anesthesia. Today stem cells are usually collected from the blood over several hours. It is understated, but this is a reassuring line to a child rather than a stated fact.
  • "vaccination proved 100% effective": an event in the story, not a general claim. Real flu vaccines are about 40 to 60% effective.
Episode 5

The Blood

3 issues

The episode follows red blood cells carrying oxygen and carbon dioxide through the blood vessels, with white "police" cells clearing out bacteria such as streptococci. It covers how long red cells live, how they are replaced, how cells release stored sugar during a shortage, and an antibody-led fight against a flu virus.

  1. Wrong in its dayE05 · 0:05medium confidence
    "blood vessels of the human body. Their surface area is 700,000 square meters."

    TodayThe inner surface of all the blood vessels (mostly capillaries) is usually estimated at a few hundred to about 1,000 square meters. 700,000 is roughly 1,000 times too large.

  2. Wrong in its dayE05 · 1:05high confidence
    "One cubic millimeter of liquid blood plasma contains five million red corpuscles and 700,000 phagocytes, or white."

    TodayNormal blood has about 4,000 to 11,000 white cells per cubic millimeter, so 700,000 is about 100 times too high. (The red cell figure is right, though the cells are in whole blood, not plasma.) This could be a dubbing slip for 7,000, but the figure as spoken is wrong. (likely a translation/dubbing slip)

  3. Wrong in its dayE05 · 6:28high confidence
    "a signal goes out to the part of the cell where sugar is stored. The Golgai bodies. When the signal is received, sugar is released through the walls of the cell."

    TodayCells store sugar as glycogen, in small grains in the cell's fluid (cytoplasm), not in the Golgi. Only the liver (and to a small extent the kidney) can release that sugar back into the blood. Muscle keeps its glycogen for its own use. The Golgi packages proteins.

Simplified but fine (6)
  • "Every day, the heart beats a hundred thousand times": correct.
  • "more than a hundred thousand kilometers" of vessels: this is the standard popular figure. It is debated, but too uncertain to count as an error.
  • Red cells living "100 days, 120 at the outside," then being removed in the spleen: correct.
  • "Over two million of us per second" (new red cells): correct.
  • The route "4,000 times a day": probably too high, since a full loop of the circulation takes about a minute. The number is unclear, so it is not counted.
  • Macrophages and platelets helping clear blockages, and streptococci as routine invaders: fine as a cartoon.
Episode 6

The Tiny Platelets

3 issues

Young platelets learn their job of patching damaged blood vessel walls, and fibrin "ropes" (made from fibrinogen by thrombin) bind them into clots. An old platelet tells the story of a disease in which antibodies attacked the body's own platelets. When Peter cuts his thumb with a dirty knife, platelets and clotting factors stop the bleeding while white cells fight a staphylococcus infection, and the pus is shown to be dead white cells.

  1. Wrong in its dayE06 · 0:05medium confidence
    "This is the bone marrow, where nearly half a billion blood platelets are produced every day." (repeated at the end: "Your body produces nearly half a billion platelets every day")

    TodayThe dub most likely uses the older British long-scale "billion" (a million million, like the French billion), so "half a billion" means about 500,000 million, the same figure episode 4 gives as "500 billion." That is still about 3 to 5 times too high: the body makes about 100 to 150 thousand million platelets a day, a rate already measured with isotope-labelled platelets in the late 1960s (Harker and Finch, 1969). Platelets live 7 to 10 days and there are about a million million in circulation.

  2. OutdatedE06 · 7:01medium confidence
    "He injected the bloodstream with Trojan horse platelets. They were full of medicine that killed any antibody that touched them."

    TodayThe disease is immune thrombocytopenia (ITP). The experimental "Trojan horse" treatment (platelets loaded with the drug vinblastine, 1970s to 80s) was aimed at the macrophages that eat antibody-coated platelets, not at the antibodies, and it never became standard. Today ITP is treated with steroids, immunoglobulin infusions (IVIG), rituximab, and drugs that boost platelet production (romiplostim and eltrombopag, approved in 2008).

  3. Wrong in its dayE06 · —:—medium confidence
    "I'm calling in the Prostaglandin squad immediately. ... Only Prostaglandin can remove the blockage."

    TodayClots are dissolved by the fibrinolytic system: plasmin, activated by tPA (approved as a clot-busting drug in 1987). Some prostaglandins, such as prostacyclin, stop platelets from clumping, while others, such as thromboxane, make them clump more. None of them dissolves an existing blockage.

Simplified but fine (7)
  • "We lived for only ten days": correct (7 to 10 days).
  • Wall damage caused by "harmful fats, acid, or even adrenaline": broadly in line with how blood vessel linings are thought to be damaged.
  • Thrombin acting on fibrinogen to make fibrin: correct.
  • Antibodies attacking the body's own platelets: a correct description of ITP.
  • Pus as dead white cells that fought germs: correct.
  • "platelets shrink with age": still debated (platelet size mostly reflects how they were produced, but some studies show older platelets lose content). Too uncertain to count.
  • Platelets staying on the wall "Forever, naturally": unclear in context, so not counted. Clots are actually remodeled and cleared.
Episode 7

The Heart

4 issues

The episode opens with the history of how blood circulation was discovered, then follows red blood cells through the heart's four chambers, the tricuspid valve, the short loop through the lungs and the long loop through the body. A flashback shows a smoker with a fatty diet who gets chest pain, is told to change his lifestyle, ignores it, and has a coronary thrombosis.

  1. Common belief thenE07 · 0:58high confidence
    "Before that, the Belgian anatomist, Andreas Vassale, first dared to speak of it in 1543. The result? He was condemned to be burnt at the stake. He was saved from the Inquisition in the nick of time by a powerful friend, Philip II of Spain."

    TodayVesalius's 1543 anatomy book did not describe blood circulation. The story that he was sentenced to death by the Inquisition and saved by Philip II is a legend with no reliable evidence behind it.

    Why it was believedThe tale of a death sentence commuted by Philip II goes back to late-16th-century rumours and was retold as fact in popular histories of medicine, which cast Vesalius as a martyr of anatomy.

  2. Common belief thenE07 · 1:19medium-high confidence
    "Ten years later, Michel Servette, a Spaniard, dared to speak of the circulatory system. But he had no king to protect him, so he died at the stake."

    TodayServetus did describe blood passing through the lungs (in 1553), and he was burned at the stake in Geneva that year. He was executed for his religious views (he denied the Trinity), not for his ideas about blood. Ibn al-Nafis had described the same lung circulation around 1242.

    Why it was believedPopular histories of science told Servetus's execution as martyrdom for scientific truth, because his account of blood passing through the lungs appeared in the same theological book (Christianismi Restitutio, 1553) for which he was burned.

  3. OutdatedE07 · 14:45medium-high confidence
    "You must cut out all tobacco, all alcohol, all salt, all fats, everything. A very strict diet"

    TodayStopping smoking is still the top priority. But guidelines no longer tell heart patients to cut out all fat. They advise replacing saturated fat with healthy unsaturated fats such as olive oil, nuts and fish (the PREDIMED trial in 2013; the US dietary guidelines dropped their limit on total fat in 2015). Salt should be reduced, not eliminated.

  4. Common belief thenE07 · 15:31medium-high confidence
    "Obviously, when the fats built up, the passageways became completely blocked. ... That's what a thrombosis is."

    TodayA thrombosis is a blood clot, not a fatty blockage. Most heart attacks happen when a fatty plaque in the artery wall cracks and a clot forms on it suddenly (shown in the early 1980s by Falk and by Davies and Thomas). It is not usually fat slowly filling the artery until it closes.

    Why it was believedThe "clogged pipe" picture, with fatty deposits slowly narrowing an artery until it closes, was how 1980s textbooks and health campaigns explained heart attacks; the role of plaque rupture and clotting was only being established in specialist journals in the early 1980s.

Simplified but fine (6)
  • Harvey (1628) being mocked, and Guy Patin defending Galen: broadly correct.
  • The heart pumping about 5 liters a minute, about 8,000 liters a day and about 200 million liters in a lifetime; weighing about 300 g; about the size of a fist; about 3 billion beats in a lifetime: all reasonable.
  • "the left ventricle is seven times more powerful than the right": the left side generates about 5 to 6 times the pressure. Close enough.
  • The tricuspid valve, the right and left chambers, and the short (lung) versus long (body) loops: correct.
  • Chest pain spreading to the left arm and breathlessness as warning signs: correct. (Angina is more often a squeezing feeling than "sharp needles.")
  • "made for emotions, feelings, love": poetry, not a claim.
Episode 8

Breathing

1 issue

The episode introduces the alveoli, where blood gives off carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen. A gang of bacteria and viruses tries to get in through the mouth. The defenders in the tonsils wipe out most of them, and the survivors try the lungs, where infected cells send interferon alarms and order antibodies. It also shows dust and pollution building up in the lungs.

  1. Common belief thenE08 · 12:34high confidence
    "That's where oxygen is transformed into carbon dioxide."

    TodayThe oxygen we breathe ends up as water. The carbon dioxide we breathe out is made from the carbon in food (sugars and fats), which is broken down in the mitochondria of cells all over the body. This was shown with isotope tracer experiments decades before 1987. (The line also seems to place this "real breathing" in the lungs, which is wrong too, though the transcript is unclear there.)

    Why it was believedSchool textbooks summed up respiration as "glucose + oxygen gives carbon dioxide + water" and "we breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide," which reads naturally as oxygen turning into CO2; that inhaled oxygen ends up in water was known mainly from isotope tracer studies.

Simplified but fine (6)
  • "300 million, 600 or 800 million? Opinions differ." (number of alveoli): the modern estimate is about 480 million (Ochs, 2004). The episode admits the uncertainty, so this is not an error.
  • Alveolar surface of "70 to 90 square meters": within the range of current estimates.
  • The nose filtering and warming air better than the mouth: correct.
  • Tonsils acting as a front-line immune checkpoint: correct.
  • Interferon alarms between cells, and the call to produce antibodies after a virus is identified: correct.
  • Dust and pollution accumulating in the lungs: correct.
Episode 9

The Brain

5 issues

The episode presents the brain as three evolutionary layers: a reptilian brain, a primitive mammalian brain and a modern neocortex. It explains the brain's large need for oxygen and sugar, how neurons pass messages through synapses, and how the medulla, hypothalamus and cerebellum handle automatic jobs such as breathing, thirst and balance. It also explains memory as storing sensory information (a rose's color and thorns), and the body map in the brain where more sensitive parts get larger areas.

  1. OutdatedE09 · 1:00high confidence
    "Others say it probably has more than a hundred billion." / "Brain contains a hundred thousand million cells."

    TodayThe human brain has about 86 billion neurons (Azevedo and Herculano-Houzel, 2009), plus a similar number of support cells (glia). The old "100 billion neurons" figure was an estimate that had never been measured. The episode also says "the brain has 10 billion nerve cells," which is too low.

  2. Wrong in its dayE09 · 3:04high confidence
    "Approximately a hundred thousand years ago, the neocortex developed."

    TodayAll mammals have a neocortex, so it is well over 100 million years old. Only its large expansion in humans is recent, and even that took millions of years.

  3. OutdatedE09 · 4:02high confidence
    "The reptilian brain, the paleocortex or primitive mammalian brain, and the neocortex or modern man's brain. Unfortunately, communication between them is not always perfect." (and "the most primitive part always tries to dominate")

    TodayThe "triune brain" model has been abandoned by neuroscientists. Brains did not evolve by stacking new layers on top of old ones, and there is no separate "reptile brain" fighting the "thinking brain" (see for example Cesario et al., 2020).

  4. Wrong in its dayE09 · 5:33medium confidence
    "It uses 2,000 liters of blood a day ... This is 20% of the body's oxygen requirements."

    TodayThe 20% share of the body's oxygen is right. But blood flow to the brain is about 750 ml a minute, which is about 1,000 to 1,100 liters a day, so 2,000 is roughly double.

  5. Wrong in its dayE09 · 10:31medium confidence
    "Nerve cell has 100,000 synaptic connections."

    TodayA typical neuron has a few thousand to about 10,000 synapses. Only special cells, such as the Purkinje cells of the cerebellum, reach about 100,000 or more.

Simplified but fine (9)
  • A few minutes without oxygen causing permanent brain damage: correct.
  • "Neurons are too sensitive to come into direct contact with us" (the blood-brain barrier): correct.
  • The brain also needing sugar: correct.
  • The medulla oblongata controlling breathing and heart rate, the hypothalamus controlling thirst and temperature, the cerebellum controlling balance, and spinal reflexes: correct.
  • Making new proteins being needed to store long-term memories: still accepted. The picture of one protein per fact is a cartoon.
  • More sensitive body parts (face, hands) having larger brain areas: correct (this map is called the homunculus).
  • "Represents 85% of the cerebral mass": close to the modern figure of about 80% of brain mass for the cortex.
  • "The human brain developed approximately three billion years ago": probably a garbled reference to the start of life's evolutionary history, so not counted.
  • "Little chemical messengers now arrive at the nucleus of the neuron": probably means the cell body. Not counted.
Episode 10

The Neurones

2 issues

The episode shows nerve cells as busy offices that receive thousands of messages, "vote" on a response and send runners along nerves. It walks through a cough reflex during a run, the sympathetic nerves speeding up the heart, and ends by saying that thought and personality come from brain chemistry.

  1. OutdatedE10 · 0:05medium confidence
    "they're the cells in the human body that are never renewed"

    TodayMost neurons do last a lifetime, but adults do grow some new neurons, at least in the hippocampus (a memory area). This was first shown in humans in 1998 (Eriksson and colleagues) and backed up by later work, including a 2025 study in Science. How much it happens is still debated.

  2. Wrong in its dayE10 · 5:18medium confidence
    "Coughing is a reflex, so you're entitled to go straight to the command centres bypassing the brain ... Shortcut along reflex road, and straight to the spinal column."

    TodayThe cough reflex is run by the brainstem (the medulla), which is part of the brain. It is not a spinal reflex like the knee-jerk. The episode is right that the thinking part of the brain is skipped, but wrong that the signal goes straight to the spinal cord.

Simplified but fine (5)
  • "a hundred thousand neurons [in a] pinhead" is roughly the right order of magnitude for brain tissue.
  • Neurons "voting" on a response is a fair picture of how a neuron adds up its incoming signals.
  • "The cell continuously receives about 30,000 pieces of information": a single neuron can have thousands to tens of thousands of synapses, so this is a reasonable figure.
  • "most of our errands only take one hundredth of a second": a fair figure for nerve signals over short distances.
  • Calling the mind "the product of some chemistry ... of glucose and amino acids" is a simplification, but not wrong.
Episode 11

The Eye

1 issue

Light is shown as photons that bounce off objects into the eye, where the lens focuses them onto the rods and cones of the retina. The signal travels along the optic nerve to the brain, which does the real "seeing" and interprets images (optical illusions, an orange vs. the sun). The episode also covers tears, which clean the eye and contain germ-killing enzymes (lysozyme).

  1. Wrong in its dayE11 · 12:32high confidence
    "The left brain deals with the signals from the right eye and vice versa."

    TodayEach side of the brain handles the opposite half of what you see, using input from both eyes. About half the fibres from each eye cross over at the optic chiasm. This has been known since the 1800s. A simple crossover like the one described happens in some animals, not in humans.

Simplified but fine (5)
  • "300,000 kilometres per second" and "around the world seven times" in a second (really about 7.5): correct.
  • Vitamin/carotene units helping the eye: correct (vitamin A is needed for night vision).
  • Rods and cones named "from their shapes": correct.
  • Tears with "germ-killing additives" and lysozyme "cleaning enzymes": correct. Lysozyme fights bacteria rather than the "viral conjunctivitis" mentioned, but that is a small slip in a cartoon scene.
  • Rubbing eyes with dirty hands can cause conjunctivitis: still sound advice.
Episode 12

The Ear

1 issue

Sound waves vibrate the eardrum. The three small bones (hammer, anvil, stirrup) pass the vibration to the cochlea, where hair cells turn it into nerve signals, and the brain turns those signals into sound. The episode also covers an ear infection that spreads up the Eustachian tube, noise damage that cannot be undone, and the semicircular canals that help with balance.

  1. Wrong in its dayE12 · 2:35medium confidence
    "polarize the cell, let the sodium ion messengers in" (hair cells of the cochlea)

    TodayThe inner-ear hair cells are activated mainly by potassium (plus some calcium) flowing in from the potassium-rich fluid around them, not by sodium. The unusual potassium-rich fluid had been known since the 1950s, and the potassium-based mechanism was well described by the early 1980s. (Sodium is right for ordinary nerve impulses further along.)

Simplified but fine (7)
  • "amplify the vibrations two times": the middle ear actually raises sound pressure about 20-fold, but the transcript may have misheard "twenty". Not counted as an error.
  • Staphylococcus as the cause of a childhood middle-ear infection: the usual germs are pneumococcus and Haemophilus, but the episode only shows staph as the invader and doesn't claim it is the usual cause.

The Skin

5 issues
