Episode 1

The First Americans

5 issues

A band of Ice Age hunters in Siberia follows the game herds east, generation after generation, across the land bridge that joined Siberia and Alaska when sea levels were low. The episode ends with them waiting thousands of years for a wall of ice to melt before they can move south into the new continent. It gives no dates, but it follows the standard 1991 model: one overland crossing, then a trip south through an opening in the ice sheets.

  1. Common belief thenE01 · 0:30medium confidence
    "A period of cold back then transformed the sea into ice and snow,"

    TodayThe Bering land bridge was not frozen sea. The sea level fell because huge amounts of water were locked up in ice sheets on land. That exposed the shallow seabed as dry land, mostly a cold, dry grassland (the "mammoth steppe") that was largely free of glaciers. Animals and people could live there.

    Why it was believedPopular books pictured an "Ice Age" as ice everywhere, and they mixed up the land bridge with walking across sea ice. Geologists (e.g. Hopkins, The Bering Land Bridge, 1967) had described Beringia as an unglaciated steppe long before 1991.

  2. Common belief thenE01 · 17:25medium confidence
    "the deep cold had frozen it into ice and snow,"

    TodaySame point: the "earth bridge" the show mentions next was exposed seabed, a dry grassland, not a sheet of frozen sea.

    Why it was believedThe same Ice Age imagery as above.

  3. Wrong in its dayE01 · 21:06medium confidence
    "And with the guts, we can make bow strings,"

    TodayIce Age hunters in Beringia and the first Americans hunted with thrusting spears and spear-throwers (atlatls). The bow reached the American Arctic only with the Arctic Small Tool tradition, about 4,500–5,000 years ago, and most of North America adopted it about 2,000–1,500 years ago. This was in standard archaeology textbooks by 1991.

  4. OutdatedE01 · 25:42high confidence
    "Thousands and thousands of years"

    TodayThe episode follows the 1991 standard model: people crossed Beringia and then waited for the "wall of ice" to open into an ice-free corridor down the Canadian interior (the "Clovis-first" scenario, about 13,000 years ago). Later evidence changed this in three ways. Sites older than the corridor are now accepted: Monte Verde in Chile (about 14,500 years, accepted in 1997) and the White Sands footprints in New Mexico (about 21,000–23,000 years, published 2021, confirmed 2023). Studies of plant and animal remains show the corridor could not support travellers until about 12,600 years ago (Pedersen et al., Nature, 2016). Most researchers now favour a route down the Pacific coast, often in boats. Genetic studies since 2007 (the "Beringian standstill") suggest the ancestral population lived in Beringia for several thousand years before spreading south.

  5. OutdatedE01 · 26:06medium confidence
    "the great barrier that blocked their way"

    TodayThe ice sheets did block the interior route for a long time. But as noted above, the first people south of the ice probably went around it along the coast rather than waiting for the interior to melt. The episode's single-path story is the pre-1997 version.

Simplified but fine (5)
  • Siberia and Alaska joined by a land bridge when the sea was lower. The "100 meters, 300 feet" fall in sea level is in the right range (about 120 m at the glacial maximum; the strait is only 30–50 m deep).
  • The migration as a slow drift over generations, following the game, with no plan to reach a "new continent". This matches modern thinking.
  • Hunters using bone needles, hides and gut, killing big game together, and travelling in small bands.
  • The sabre-toothed cat in the elder's story: scimitar-toothed cats (Homotherium) did live in Beringia.
  • The sea flooding the bridge behind them (about 11,000 years ago).
Episode 2

The Hunters

3 issues

As the Ice Age glaciers melt, the descendants of the Siberian migrants spread across North America. One band adopts the spear-thrower and the Clovis spearhead. A greedy group splits off to hunt for its own sake, and the great Ice Age animals disappear. Later the survivors turn to plants, invent the bow and build a respectful bison-hunting culture. A closing narration jumps to the 19th-century slaughter of the bison.

  1. OutdatedE02 · 0:29medium confidence
    "across siberia people arrive here in what we call montana"

    TodayThe episode has people arriving overland east of the Rockies (Montana), which means a trip down the ice-free corridor between the ice sheets. Studies of plant and animal remains show the corridor could not support travellers until about 12,600 years ago (Pedersen et al., Nature, 2016). Older sites south of the ice are now accepted: Monte Verde in Chile (about 14,500 years, accepted 1997), Buttermilk Creek in Texas (about 15,500 years, 2011) and the White Sands footprints in New Mexico (about 21,000–23,000 years, 2021, confirmed 2023). Most researchers now think the first people came down the Pacific coast. To the episode's credit, its date of about 15,000 years ago (see the mishearing note below) is earlier than the Clovis-first dates usual in 1991 and fits the modern picture better.

  2. OutdatedE02 · 24:28medium confidence
    "as the white hunters deliberately exterminated them to deprive the Indians"

    TodayThe killing was real and catastrophic, and some army officers and politicians welcomed it as a way to force Plains nations onto reservations. But Flores (1991) and Isenberg (2000) showed that the main driver was the commercial hide trade, which boomed after a new tanning process (1871) made bison leather valuable for industrial belting. Other factors were drought, competition from horses and cattle, cattle diseases, and Native hunting for the robe trade, which had been thinning herds since the 1840s. The slaughter was not mainly a planned policy, and the Sheridan "let them kill" speech often quoted for it is poorly sourced.

  3. OutdatedE02 · 24:47medium confidence
    "In 60 million buffalo, but by the end of the 19th century, only a few hundred were still alive."

    TodayThe 60 million figure comes from Ernest Thompson Seton (1909). Studies of how many animals the grasslands could feed (Dan Flores, 1991; Andrew Isenberg, 2000) put the Great Plains at about 25–30 million, fewer in droughts. The low point is about right: William Hornaday's 1889 count found about 1,000 bison in all, only a few hundred of them wild in the United States.

Simplified but fine (9)
  • "about 15 million years before our modern era" is almost certainly a transcription error for 15,000 years. Glaciers were melting fast then, although the Ice Age formally ended about 11,700 years ago.
  • The spread over generations "across the Great Plains as far as Nevada", the Great Lakes and the eastern woodlands. Nevada is in the Great Basin, not the Plains.
  • The Clovis point near Clovis, New Mexico. Blackwater Draw is the type site, and Clovis is dated to about 13,000–12,700 years ago (Waters & Stafford 2007). Where the point was first made is unknown, and the show rightly says it took great skill to make.
  • The spear-thrower (atlatl) shown as a new invention that throws farther. It was used with Clovis points, but it is older (Upper Palaeolithic Europe, about 20,000 years ago) and probably came with the first migrants.
  • Driving herds over a cliff ("Massacre. Some got away"): bison jumps are well documented (Olsen-Chubbuck, about 10,000 years ago; Head-Smashed-In, from about 6,000 years ago), but they belong to slightly later bison hunters, not the mammoth era.
  • The extinction of the great sloth, horses, camels and giant armadillos (glyptodonts) after over-hunting. Paul Martin's "overkill" hypothesis was prominent in 1991, and the debate continues today. Most researchers blame a mix of human hunting and rapid climate change (the Younger Dryas). Ancient DNA from sediments (Murchie et al. 2021) suggests horses and mammoths survived in the Yukon a few thousand years longer than once thought. "Disappeared the dun and the buffalo" is likely a mishearing (probably the mammoth and the giant Ice Age bison, since the next lines say "we still have our old friend the buffalo").
  • Plant gathering and a broader diet after the big game declined matches the Archaic period.
  • The bow "invented" after the megafauna disappeared: the episode gives no date. In fact the bow came from the north and reached the Plains only about 2,000–1,500 years ago.
  • The bison prayer and the use of hide, horn, sinew and bone: broadly true of Plains peoples. The "wise elder who forbids waste" theme is a moral lesson. Kill sites show that ancient hunters sometimes wasted many animals.
Episode 3

The Conquerors of the Great North

9 issues

The episode follows the peoples of the Arctic: early Paleo-Eskimo groups and then the Thule, the whale-hunting ancestors of today's Inuit, who spread from Alaska to Greenland and replaced the Dorset culture. It then shows Thorfinn Karlsefni's Norse expedition to Newfoundland, a friendly meeting with the locals that turns sour over a stolen sword, and the Norse withdrawal. It ends by saying that later traders and trappers brought alcohol, violence and disease, which devastated the Inuit.

  1. OutdatedE03 · 1:42high confidence
    "Did they come by land bridges? 10 or 12,000"

    TodayAbout 10,000–12,000 years ago (the Clovis era) was the standard date in 1991 for the first people to reach the Americas. Monte Verde, Chile (about 14,500 years, accepted in 1997) and the White Sands footprints, New Mexico (about 21,000–23,000 years, 2021/2023) push the first arrival back to at least 15,000 years ago and probably earlier. By 10,000–12,000 years ago the land bridge was already flooding (about 11,000 years ago).

  2. Wrong in its dayE03 · 2:02medium confidence
    "agreed that these were the very first migrants to have populated the American continent."

    TodayThe opposite is true. The ancestors of the Eskimo-Aleut peoples (Paleo-Eskimos, about 5,000 years ago, and later the Thule) were the last major migration from Asia, thousands of years after the first Americans. This was standard by 1991 (e.g. Greenberg, Turner & Zegura's three-wave model, 1986). The French original may have said "the last" and been mistranslated.

  3. Wrong in its dayE03 · 14:02medium confidence
    "invented the harpoon with a detachable tip and waterproof jackets"

    TodayToggling harpoons with detachable heads are thousands of years older than the Thule. They were used in the Pre-Dorset and Old Bering Sea cultures and in the Maritime Archaic. The Thule's real innovations were open-water whaling from umiaks with harpoon lines and floats, and wider use of dog sleds.

  4. Wrong in its dayE03 · 21:19medium confidence
    "They will thus cover the 12,000 kilometers that separate Beringia from Greenland,"

    TodayFrom Bering Strait to northwest Greenland is about 3,300 km in a straight line, and to east Greenland about 4,700 km. Coastal routes are longer, but 12,000 km is far too high. This may be a translation or transcription slip.

  5. Common belief thenE03 · 21:38medium confidence
    "along with their more elementary civilization, absorbed by our ingenious, our courageous"

    TodayThe Dorset (heard as "Durets") did disappear around 1300–1500 as the Thule spread. But they were not simply "more elementary". They were skilled sea-mammal hunters and produced some of the finest carvings of the prehistoric Arctic. Ancient-DNA work (Raghavan et al., Science, 2014) found almost no Dorset ancestry in the Inuit, so "replaced" fits better than "absorbed".

    Why it was believedThe idea of technologically "superior" Thule pushing aside a "simpler" people was the usual way of telling the story.

  6. OutdatedE03 · 21:43high confidence
    "At the same time, somewhere in northern Europe, other men were also feeling"

    TodayThe episode has the Thule expansion happen at the same time as the Norse voyages of about 1000 AD. In 1991 the Thule migration east was usually dated to about 1000 AD. Better radiocarbon dating (e.g. Friesen & Arnold, American Antiquity, 2008) puts the rapid spread from Alaska to Greenland at about 1200–1300 AD, two or three centuries after Leif Erikson and Karlsefni.

  7. Wrong in its dayE03 · 22:07medium confidence
    "40 years later, two ships arrive inside of Newfoundland."

    TodayErik the Red settled Greenland in about 985/986. His son Leif Erikson reached Vinland in about 1000, and Thorfinn Karlsefni ("Torfinn Carl") led his expedition in about 1009–1010, roughly 25 years later. Eiríks saga gives Karlsefni three ships and Grœnlendinga saga gives him one, never two. (By coincidence, tree-ring dating at L'Anse aux Meadows shows Norse activity in 1021 AD: Kuitems et al., Nature, 2021.)

  8. Wrong in its dayE03 · 22:16high confidence
    "Straight ahead, guys, there is land, and we are the first to set foot"

    TodayIn both sagas Leif Erikson (and in Grœnlendinga saga his brother Thorvald) had already landed in Vinland before Karlsefni. Karlsefni's expedition used Leif's camp. Leif is missing from the episode.

  9. Common belief thenE03 · 25:04medium confidence
    "their country and the Eskimos remain the soul masters of the far north."

    TodayThe people Karlsefni met in Vinland (the sagas call them Skrælings) were almost certainly not Inuit. Around 1000 AD the Thule had not yet left Alaska, and the Dorset had already left Newfoundland. The locals were probably ancestors of the Beothuk or Innu (the "Little Passage" culture), forest Algonquian-speaking peoples. In Greenland, Norse and Inuit did meet later, and the Norse Greenland colonies lasted until about 1450. ("soul masters" is a mishearing of "sole masters".)

    Why it was believedOlder popular histories used "Skrælings" and "Eskimos" as if they were the same people, and Norse contact with Inuit in Greenland was carried over to Vinland.

Simplified but fine (5)
  • Paleo-Eskimo skin boats and a second wave of Arctic migrants about 4,000–5,000 years ago ("light-skinned boats" is a mistranslation of "skin boats").
  • The Thule coming from the northwest (Alaska), whale hunts with float harpoons, semi-underground winter houses, oil lamps ("lightning" is probably "lighting"), communal caribou hunts, and the men's house that women could not enter (the Alaskan qargi).
  • The Thule making dog sleds central to Arctic life. The tamed-dog scene is a cartoon: dogs had come to the Americas with people thousands of years earlier, and the Thule brought their own sled-dog lineage.
  • The Norse encounter closely follows the sagas: trade in food and goods, Karlsefni forbidding the sale of weapons, a native trying to take a weapon, then violence and the Norse leaving because of constant hostility. This is better than the usual "Vikings conquer" myth.
  • The episode ends by blaming later traders, whalers and trappers for alcohol, violence and epidemics, and it says plainly that the Inuit were "decimated". For example, the Mackenzie Delta Inuvialuit fell from about 2,500 to about 150 people by 1910. This is franker than most children's histories of the period.
Episode 4

The Promised Land

3 issues

As peoples spread across North America, the episode follows a family in the Great Basin between the Rockies and the Sierra Nevada. They gather pine nuts and roots, fish, hunt rabbits with nets alongside rival neighbours, and dig winter pit houses. It then moves to the Pacific Northwest Coast, describing it as a land of plenty with time for art, a shaman healer and marriage customs. It ends with a competitive potlatch between two chiefs and says the potlatch was later outlawed.

  1. Wrong in its dayE04 · 21:29medium confidence
    "to have two wives or two husbands if one had the means and he had them"

    TodayHigh-ranking men on the Northwest Coast, the Haida ("Aida's") among them, sometimes had more than one wife. Marriages between polyandrous households (one wife, several husbands) are not documented as a Haida custom, and there was no rule limiting marriages to "no more than two". The scene invents these details.

  2. Common belief thenE04 · 25:23medium confidence
    "as wealth accumulated these men"

    TodayThe episode presents the potlatch as boastful excess that grew out of wealth. Its main purposes were to confirm rank, names, inheritance and rights to territory, and to share out wealth. The extreme "rivalry" potlatches, where property was destroyed (like the blanket-burning in the episode), were mostly a late-19th-century Kwakwaka'wakw development. Epidemics left many noble titles vacant, and the fur and wage trade brought in a flood of goods (Helen Codere, Fighting with Property, 1950).

    Why it was believedRuth Benedict's Patterns of Culture (1934) made the Kwakiutl "megalomaniac" potlatch famous. Popular accounts treated that late, contact-era form as timeless.

  3. Wrong in its dayE04 · 25:42high confidence
    "faced with all these excesses in the 20th century patelch was"

    TodayCanada banned the potlatch in an amendment to the Indian Act passed in 1884 (in force from 1 January 1885). The ban was strengthened in 1895 and 1918 and only dropped in 1951. Missionaries and Indian agents pushed for it as part of a policy of forced assimilation, not because of "excesses". It led to arrests and confiscation of ceremonial goods, for example after Dan Cranmer's 1921 potlatch. ("patelch" = potlatch.)

Simplified but fine (5)
  • The Great Basin peoples (Shoshone, Paiute, Ute): scarce game, pine nuts and roots stored for winter, seasonal moves, communal rabbit drives with long nets, and semi-underground winter houses.
  • The Northwest Coast as an exceptionally rich foraging region (salmon, eulachon, halibut, sea mammals) with dense villages, a strong sense of rank, and time for carving and weaving. This is standard anthropology.
  • The potlatch gifts: carved boxes, Chilkat-style dancing robes of cedar bark and mountain-goat wool ("scottish fiber" is probably a mishearing of "cedar"), engraved coppers, blankets and totem poles. These are accurate. "Bison horn", "persimmons" and "barbecue" are cartoon or translation slips.
  • Shamans as healers.
  • Hunters sparing an animal they do not need. This echoes the "ecological Indian" image (critiqued by Shepard Krech, 1999), but the scene makes no broad factual claim.
Episode 5

The Tumulus Builders

10 issues

The episode covers the farming cultures of the American Southwest: the Hohokam with their irrigation and ball courts, and the Anasazi (Ancestral Puebloans) at Mesa Verde and Pueblo Bonito, who build a solar observatory and later scatter in a great drought. It then follows the recurring characters east to the Mississippian mound builders and Cahokia's huge Monks Mound, where a chief settles a dispute over field boundaries. It ends with European germs and guns destroying these societies.

  1. OutdatedE05 · 1:03medium confidence
    "And that was how they discovered irrigation."

    TodayThe episode credits the Hohokam (from about 150 BC) with inventing irrigation. Excavations in the Tucson Basin since the late 1990s and 2000s, notably Las Capas, found irrigation canals dating to about 1200 BC. Maize farming in the Southwest goes back to about 2100 BC. Irrigation began in the "Early Agricultural Period", centuries before the Hohokam culture. The Hohokam did build the largest canal systems in pre-Columbian North America (hundreds of kilometres along the Salt and Gila rivers).

  2. OutdatedE05 · 4:20medium confidence
    "The name means old people."

    TodayThe name "Anasazi" is a Navajo word, often rendered "ancient enemies" or "ancestors of the enemies" rather than simply "old people". Since the 1990s archaeologists and the Pueblo nations have generally preferred "Ancestral Puebloans". They were the ancestors not only of the Hopi but of all the Pueblo peoples, including Zuni, Acoma and the Rio Grande Pueblos.

  3. Wrong in its dayE05 · 5:08high confidence
    "the descent to Mesa Verde and they need ropes."

    TodayMesa Verde is a whole mesa (now a national park) with hundreds of cliff dwellings, not one building. Its best-known sites, such as Cliff Palace, Balcony House and Spruce Tree House, have been visited by ordinary tourists for over a century, using stairs and ladders. The inhabitants used carved hand-and-toe-hold trails. Most of the population lived on the mesa top, and the cliff dwellings were built only in the last century of occupation (about 1190–1280).

  4. Wrong in its dayE05 · 5:14high confidence
    "the one that is most remarkable has to be Pueblo Bonito, east of New Mexico."

    TodayPueblo Bonito is in Chaco Canyon, in northwestern New Mexico. It was built in stages from about 850 to 1150 AD.

  5. Wrong in its dayE05 · 16:48medium confidence
    "Just before the year 1200, we see the unfortunate fate of the Anasazi."

    TodayChaco's decline came after a drought in the 1130s–1150s. The final abandonment of the Four Corners region (Mesa Verde included) came about 1280–1300, during the "Great Drought" of 1276–1299 identified from tree rings by A. E. Douglass (1929). The unfinished "temple" fits Mesa Verde's Sun Temple, built in the mid-to-late 1200s. Drought was one factor among several, along with violence, conflict and social change.

  6. Common belief thenE05 · 18:35medium confidence
    "Mexican influence is seen clearly here in the crops that they grow,"

    TodayMaize and beans did come from Mexico. But squash, sunflower and other crops were domesticated independently in eastern North America (the "Eastern Agricultural Complex"; Bruce Smith, Science, 1989). Mound building there is much older than Mississippian times: Poverty Point dates to about 1700 BC, and Watson Brake in Louisiana to about 3500 BC (Science, 1997). Most archaeologists now see Mississippian art and iconography, including the feathered-serpent-like figures, as a local development, not proof of direct Mexican influence. No Mesoamerican trade goods have been securely identified at Cahokia.

    Why it was believedMid-20th-century "diffusionist" archaeology often explained complex societies in the Eastern Woodlands as offshoots of Mesoamerica.

  7. Wrong in its dayE05 · 19:15medium confidence
    "To the east of the spot where the city of St. Louis will one day be built, Ahokia, an area about 500 acres."

    TodayCahokia ("Ahokia") is east of St Louis, across the Mississippi in Illinois. But the settlement covered about 6 square miles (about 1,600 ha, roughly 4,000 acres). Only its central palisaded precinct was smaller (about 200 acres).

  8. Wrong in its dayE05 · 19:31high confidence
    "There are at least 20 inside the city limits and a countless number of them outside."

    TodayCahokia had about 120 mounds within the site, about 70 of which survive in the state historic site.

  9. Wrong in its dayE05 · 23:34medium confidence
    "In three centuries, with the coming of Europeans, this magnificent civilization would disappear."

    TodayCahokia itself was largely empty by about 1350–1400, more than a century before any European arrived. Much of the central Mississippi and lower Ohio valleys was also abandoned (Stephen Williams' "Vacant Quarter", 1990). Other Mississippian chiefdoms, such as Coosa and the Natchez, were still thriving when de Soto arrived and collapsed afterwards. The episode's own chronology also fails: the characters leave the Southwest about 1200–1300 and arrive at a Cahokia that was already past its peak (about 1050–1150).

  10. Common belief thenE05 · 23:53medium confidence
    "In a very short time, these people were turned into a dying race."

    TodayEpidemics, warfare and slave raiding did cause catastrophic losses in the Southeast after de Soto (1539–43). But the descendants of the Mississippian chiefdoms (Muscogee/Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Cherokee, Natchez and others) re-formed into new nations and survive today. The Native population of the US has grown steadily since about 1900. "Dying race" repeats the 19th-century "vanishing Indian" idea.

    Why it was believedThe "vanishing Indian" was a long-standing theme of popular history and art.

Simplified but fine (8)
  • Hohokam ("those who have vanished", from the O'odham word) in southern Arizona, with canals and ball courts. How the ball game was played is uncertain.
  • Pueblo Bonito's great houses, granaries and plazas. The solar calendar recalls Chaco's solstice and equinox markers (the Fajada Butte "Sun Dagger", 1977). The "signal" network echoes Chacoan line-of-sight sites, which are thought to have been used for signalling.
  • Anasazi groups dispersing north (Hopi), into Mogollon country and to the Rio Grande, rather than "vanishing". This is a good corrective to the "mysterious disappearance" myth.
  • About 10,000 mounds in Ohio (Squier and Davis's 1848 estimate). Most of these were built by the much earlier Adena and Hopewell cultures (800 BC–500 AD), not by Mississippians.
  • Cahokia with "over 20,000" people (estimates run from about 10,000 to 20,000-plus), the largest city north of Mexico.
  • Monks Mound ("the one of the monkey"): about 6–7 ha at the base, about 30 m high, about 620,000 m³ of earth, built from an estimated 14–15 million basket loads ("men" is probably "baskets"). Its base is comparable to the Great Pyramid's, but "equal to or exceeds the greatest monuments on Earth" overstates it (Giza is about four times its volume).
  • Kin-owned fields with boundary markers and a chief judging disputes. This rightly contradicts the myth that Native peoples had no notion of property.
  • The closing line "neither savages nor saints" and the direct mention of epidemics and firearms are better than the noble-or-savage stereotypes common at the time.
Episode 6

The Aztecs Before the Conquest

6 issues

The episode is set in 1486, at the start of Ahuitzotl's reign. It shows the Triple Alliance (Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, Tlacopan) forcing a town to pay tribute, then follows Mexica schoolchildren as a priest teaches them about sacrifice to the sun, warrior training, the founding legend of Tenochtitlan and the laws of daily life. It ends with the legend of Quetzalcoatl's promised return and the arrival of Cortés.

  1. Wrong in its dayE06 · 0:32medium confidence
    "Now, they have the same monuments, the same pyramids, same lifestyle as their cousins farther north."

    TodayThe mound-building peoples of the Mississippi valley (episode 05) built earthen platform mounds. They lived in chiefdoms, and their greatest centre, Cahokia, was largely abandoned by about 1350. The Aztecs built stone pyramid-temples and lived in large cities under a tribute empire with writing and markets. A Mesoamerican influence on the Mississippians is possible but unproven. The two were not "the same".

  2. Wrong in its dayE06 · 16:33medium confidence
    "Fate awaits anyone who's caught using peyotl."

    TodayNo known Aztec law punished peyote use. Peyote came mainly from the northern deserts and was linked to the Chichimecs. Hallucinogenic mushrooms (teonanacatl) and similar plants were taken openly at feasts by merchants and nobles, as Sahagún describes. The surviving laws punish drunkenness, not ritual drugs.

  3. Wrong in its dayE06 · 17:16high confidence
    "Homosexuality? It depends on the sex of the couple. For women, it is death. For men, well, nobody's perfect."

    TodayThe colonial sources on Aztec and Texcocan law (Motolinía, Las Casas, Ixtlilxochitl's account of Nezahualcoyotl's laws) all say sex between men was punished by death, and some describe especially cruel deaths. They say the same for women. These sources were written by Spaniards and Christianised Nahuas and may exaggerate, but none of them says men were tolerated. The joke reverses what the sources say.

  4. Wrong in its dayE06 · 26:21high confidence
    "In sacrifices, he took only the blood of serpents and birds, hence his name."

    TodayThe name Quetzalcoatl means "quetzal(-feathered) serpent", the plumed serpent, a deity shown that way since Teotihuacan (about 200 AD). It does not come from what he was offered. The legend of the Toltec priest-king Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl does say he offered snakes, birds and butterflies instead of humans (Anales de Cuauhtitlan). But Quetzalcoatl as a god did receive human sacrifice, for example at Cholula, so "enemy of all violence" is also a legend.

  5. OutdatedE06 · 26:43high confidence
    "But one day soon, he will return. Believe it, all the oracles tell us this."

    TodayThe story that the Aztecs expected Quetzalcoatl to return from the east, and then took Cortés for him, is now widely seen as a post-conquest invention. It does not appear in Cortés's letters. It took shape decades later in Franciscan and Nahua writings that tried to explain the defeat after the fact. Susan Gillespie (The Aztec Kings, 1989) first argued this. Camilla Townsend ("Burying the White Gods", American Historical Review, 2003) and Matthew Restall (Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest, 2003) made it the mainstream view. Around 1991 most narrative histories still told the story.

  6. OutdatedE06 · 26:50high confidence
    "Taken for the god Quetzalcoatl came Cortes, the conquistador."

    TodayThis rests on the same post-conquest legend (see above). Moctezuma's gifts and his caution toward Cortés are now read as normal Mesoamerican diplomacy toward a powerful unknown force, not as religious paralysis (Restall, When Montezuma Met Cortés, 2018).

  • The table manners and the priest's advice paraphrase the huehuetlahtolli (elders' speeches) collected by Sahagún.
  • "30 years later ... came Cortes": he landed in 1519, 33 years after 1486.

Shepard Krech's The Ecological Indian (1999), Dan Flores (1991) and Andrew Isenberg (The Destruction of the Bison, 2000) document buffalo jumps that killed more animals than could be used, and Native market hunting for the hide and robe trade in the 1800s. Historians agree the main cause of the bison's collapse was non-Native commercial hunting, but the "ecological Indian" image is now seen as simplified.

  2. Common belief thenE17 · 14:14medium confidence
    "Another one if you kill an enemy, and especially one if you scalped one."

    TodayAmong Plains peoples the highest war honour was usually counting coup: touching or striking a living enemy, or taking his weapon or horse. That outranked killing him. Scalping carried relatively little prestige in most coup systems, so the show reverses the order. Eagle-feather awards did vary from nation to nation.

    Why it was believedWestern films and pulp fiction made scalping the defining act of "Indian warfare".

Simplified but fine (7)
  • Horses reaching the northern and central Plains in the early-to-mid 1700s (Shoshone around 1700, Blackfoot around 1730, Lakota around 1750), spreading north from Spanish New Mexico after the 1680 Pueblo Revolt. The Apaches were mounted earlier, so the opening encounter is plausible. The show does not project the horse culture back into earlier times, which is better than most popular portrayals.
  • The "big dog" name echoes real names for the horse: Lakota šuŋkawakaŋ ("sacred/mysterious dog") and Blackfoot "elk-dog". Before horses, dogs pulled the travois.
  • Lakota, Cheyenne and others moving west onto the Plains in the 1700s, pushed by better-armed neighbours (Ojibwe, Cree) and the pressure of the fur trade. The Comanche arrived on the southern Plains around 1700 and fought the Spanish through much of the century (peace in 1786).
  • Communal buffalo hunts under a leader's discipline, with no hunting for individual glory; riding up close to kill with arrows; wounded bulls goring horses.
  • Vision quests with fasting and sweat lodges in search of the "great mysterious" power (Lakota Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka); medicine bundles and charms; eagle feathers earned in war.
  • The chief who is also shaman and healer is played for comedy. Political and spiritual leadership were often separate roles, though some leaders held both.
  • Guns, whiskey and epidemics (such as the 1780–82 smallpox epidemic on the Plains) reaching the Plains peoples ahead of white settlement.

The 13 Colonies Towards Independence

The episode surveys the 13 colonies in the mid-1700s: Georgia's founding, population growth, slavery, the Great Awakening and the gap between planters, farmers and frontiersmen. Benjamin Franklin links the story, from the Albany Congress (1754) and his lightning experiments to British trade laws, the Stamp Act, the Boston Tea Party, the Continental Congress and the Minutemen. But the roughly 114 settlers on the Anne (who landed in February 1733) were chosen from the "worthy poor", and none are known to have come out of debtors' prison. Few, if any, came later either.     Why it was believedOglethorpe's prison-reform work and his early pamphlets made "the debtors' colony" a fixture of school textbooks. Georgia historians (e.g. Albert Saye, Betty Wood) had shown otherwise by the 1970s–80s.

  2. Wrong in its dayE19 · 2:57high confidence
    "So 300,000 blacks in the South, who it's understood are supposed to be freed after six or seven years, but things didn't always work out that way."

    TodayBy the mid-1700s, Africans in the colonies were held in lifelong, hereditary slavery. Virginia made slave status pass through the mother in 1662 and codified slavery in its 1705 slave code, and the other colonies did the same. Terms of 4–7 years applied to indentured servants, who were mostly European. Some early Africans in 1620s–40s Virginia did have servant status, but by 1750 none were "supposed to be freed". Presenting slavery as a broken indenture badly minimises it. (The figure of about 300,000 Black people, mostly in the South, is about right for around 1760.)

    TodayIn the colonial South, enslaved people grew tobacco (Chesapeake), rice and indigo (Carolina/Georgia). Cotton became a major plantation crop only after Eli Whitney's gin (1793).

  4. Common belief thenE19 · 3:53high confidence

    TodayThe idea that Native Americans "would not be enslaved" is a myth. Carolina ran a huge Indian slave trade: Alan Gallay (2002) estimates that 24,000–51,000 Native people were captured and sold between 1670 and 1715, many shipped to the Caribbean. The colonies turned to Africans because of epidemics, wars like the Yamasee War (1715) that ended the trade, Native captives escaping easily into familiar country, and a growing Atlantic supply. The flip side of the line, that Africans were "docile", is false too: there were revolts such as New York in 1712 and Stono in 1739.

    Why it was believedThe romantic "noble, freedom-loving Indian" image and older planter apologetics. Native slavery was studied in the 1910s (Lauber, 1913) but left out of popular histories until Gallay (2002) and Reséndez (2016).

    TodayThe 342 chests destroyed on 16 December 1773 were valued by the East India Company at about £9,659, usually rounded to "about £10,000".

Ebony Wood

A family plans to escape north, and the episode closes with the abolition of the trade and an estimate of how many Africans were deported.

The French original probably said gomme (gum arabic, a major Senegal export), which the dub translated as "rubber".

    TodayThe escape plan ("up north, in those states, there's no slaves") puts the story in the United States, where coffee was never a plantation crop. Simplified but fine (7)
  • African rulers raiding to capture and sell people, and trading with Portuguese, English, French and Dutch ships, is accurate. "Great Yavogan" is the yovogan ("chief of the white men"), Dahomey's official who handled European trade at Ouidah. The episode shows African participation without excusing the European buyers, which is more honest than many 1990s accounts.
  • The goods traded (brandy, cloth, gunpowder, guns) are right. "Strings of pearls" is a literal translation of French perles, which here means glass beads.
  • Ships coasting along Africa until full, shipboard revolts (about one voyage in ten saw one, and most failed), captives forced to "dance" on deck for exercise, and bodies oiled before sale are all accurate.

The northern states had abolished or were gradually ending slavery (Vermont 1777 to New Jersey's gradual law of 1804). The episode rightly says that escaping north did not end the danger (Fugitive Slave Acts of 1793 and 1850).
  • The French continued trading illegally after the 1815–18 bans, until the law of 1831 and the 1831/1833 right-of-search treaties with Britain. Brazil (1850) and Cuba (1860s) went on longer. US slavery ended with the 13th Amendment (December 1865).

The episode doesn't say that only about 400,000 of them went to mainland North America, while most went to Brazil and the Caribbean.
Episode 22

The Pioneers

8 issues

The episode follows the young United States from about 1790 to 1850. It covers Daniel Boone's hunting trips into Kentucky, the Louisiana Purchase (shown as a comic haggle), new states joining the Union, slavery in the South, Indian removal, Texas and the Alamo, the Mexican War and a wagon train on the Oregon Trail. It is notably frank about slavery and broken treaties, and it points to slavery as a cause of the Texas conflict. But it mixes up some geography and chronology, and it uses the "ecological Indian" stereotype.

  1. Wrong in its dayE22 · 0:21high confidence

    TodayThe 1790 census counted Boston at about 18,300, Charleston at about 16,400 and Baltimore at about 13,500. These were substantial towns, not places of "a few thousand". (The show's New York figure of 33,000 matches the 1790 census, and 46,000 is roughly right for Philadelphia with its suburbs.)

  2. OutdatedE22 · 7:09medium confidence

    TodayThe Shawnee who caught Boone and John Stewart in December 1769 (led by "Captain Will") took their skins and told them to leave because Kentucky was Shawnee and Cherokee hunting ground. It was a claim of territory, not a belief that animals belonged to everyone. The Shawnee were themselves deeply involved in the commercial deerskin trade. The words echo the invented "Chief Seattle" speech, which was largely written by Ted Perry in 1971. Shepard Krech's The Ecological Indian (1999) brought wide criticism of the "ecological Indian" stereotype.

  3. OutdatedE22 · 9:45high confidence

    TodayFrance was paid 60 million francs (about $11.25 million), and the US also took on about $3.75 million of its citizens' claims against France, so the total was about $15 million. More importantly, the US bought France's imperial claim, not land empty of owners. Robert Lee ("Accounting for Conquest", Journal of American History, 2017) calculated that the United States later spent at least $2.6 billion (nominal) on 222 land cessions from Native nations within the territory. The "bargain" framing of the purchase price looks very different now.

  4. Common belief thenE22 · 10:02medium confidence

    TodayBetween 1800 and about 1830, US population growth came overwhelmingly from natural increase: very high birth rates. Immigration was low during the Napoleonic Wars and averaged only around 10,000 a year until the 1820s. Mass immigration began in the 1830s and 1840s.

    Why it was believedThe "nation of immigrants" story projected the later mass migrations (Irish and German in the 1840s and 1850s) back onto the early republic.

  • Daniel Boone: a poorly schooled youth who was nevertheless literate, a rifle at about 12 or 13, marriage to Rebecca Bryan (1756), a Yadkin Valley farm in North Carolina, debts paid off with skins, and the long hunt of 1769–71 into Kentucky.

The Mormon move to the Great Salt Lake was in 1847.
