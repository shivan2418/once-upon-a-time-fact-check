Episode 1

The Planet Omega

No issues

An unknown, very fast alien ship keeps crossing space watched from Omega, the headquarters of a confederation of worlds. The council splits between attacking it and making contact, and a patrol cruiser that tries to make contact is easily outrun. The only real science is a short classroom scene where Maestro defines the light year, the speed of light and the parsec, and mentions Einstein's relativity and FitzGerald contraction.

Nothing outdated or wrong was found in this episode.

Simplified but fine (4)
  • Speed of light "300,000 kilometers per second" (exactly 299,792 km/s), and the half-finished sum "around 18 million" is right for kilometres per minute.
  • Light year "9,460,800,000,000 kilometers" is exactly 300,000 km/s × 365 days, a correct rounded figure (precise value 9.4607 × 10^12 km).
  • "A parsec is, as you know, 3.26 light years" is correct.
  • Einstein's curved spacetime and FitzGerald (Lorentz–FitzGerald) length contraction are real physics, only name-checked here.
Episode 2

The Saurians

1 issue

After a suspicious breakdown near the planet Lago, which General Grouchley had reported as lifeless, Pierrot leads a reconnaissance mission with Maestro's new android Metro. They find a Triassic-style jungle and a civilisation of intelligent, telepathic reptiles that play a tail-bashing ball game, keep their numbers down by eating surplus eggs, and once exterminated their crocodile-like cousins. The real science is brief: the distance to the Andromeda galaxy, Triassic plants and climate, and where crocodiles, dinosaurs and pterosaurs came from.

  1. Common belief thenE02 · 16:10medium confidence
    "Descendants from the Saltoparkas, originating as crocodiles, dinosaurs, and winged reptiles."

    Today"Saltoparkas" is probably a mishearing of Saltoposuchus, or a blend of it with Euparkeria, the two "thecodonts" that older books showed as the common ancestor of crocodiles, dinosaurs and pterosaurs. Saltoposuchus (about 215 million years ago) is now classed as an early crocodylomorph on the crocodile branch, too late and on the wrong branch to be anyone's ancestor. Euparkeria sits near the base of the group but is not a direct ancestor. Cladistic studies from the mid-1980s on dropped "Thecodontia" as a real group.

    Why it was believedIn popular dinosaur books of the 1960s and 70s, small running thecodonts such as Saltoposuchus and Euparkeria were the standard picture of the "ancestor of the ruling reptiles".

Simplified but fine (3)
  • Andromeda "at a distance of two million light years", seen "as it was" when the light left: 1982 references gave about 2.2 million; today's figure is about 2.5 million.
  • "Vegetation exactly conforms to that of the Triassic period, 200 million years ago": the Triassic ran from about 252 to 201 million years ago (1982 tables said roughly 225–190).
  • "The atmosphere is rich in carbon dioxide. It accelerates the pulse.": breathing extra CO2 does speed up breathing and heart rate. Triassic air did hold several times today's CO2.
Episode 3

The Green Planet

No issues

General Grouchy of Cassiopeia sends a huge ship of soldiers to build a military base on a lush planet beyond Andromeda, but the planet's intelligent plants defend themselves and its "forest king" tells a patrol pilot how humans have always mistreated trees and flowers. The confederation votes to protect the planet. The episode is an ecology fable; apart from naming real constellations, it presents almost no science as fact.

Nothing outdated or wrong was found in this episode.

Simplified but fine (2)
  • "The galaxies of the Swan, the Lizard, the Little Bear, and the Dragon": Cygnus, Lacerta, Ursa Minor and Draco are real constellations near Cassiopeia in our sky, not galaxies. The series uses constellation names as fictional political regions throughout, so this is world-building rather than a factual claim.
  • Plants giving "fresh, pure air" (photosynthetic oxygen) and the long lives of old trees are fair as a general message.
Episode 4

Towards Andromeda

No issues

Pierrot and Psyche's patrol ship is caught in a "gravitational storm" near a cluster of young stars and crashes on a scorching planet. With the robot Metro they trek to cooler ground, work out where they are from the stars, and end up in the hands of rebels armed by Cassiopeia. The real science is light: navigating by star positions, a remark on young star clusters, and the number of stars in our galaxy.

Nothing outdated or wrong was found in this episode.

Simplified but fine (4)
  • "My memory banks classify 200 billion individual stars, and that's just in our own galaxy": within the usual range for the Milky Way (about 100–400 billion).
  • Fixing position from three stars and needing darkness to see constellations is sound navigation.
  • "Young stars ... if one falls into the gravitational field of another, it can unleash immense energy": vague, but close encounters and mergers of stars in dense young clusters are real.
  • The catalogue numbers are used fictionally: the real NGC 7052 is an elliptical galaxy in Vulpecula, not a young star cluster.
Episode 5

The Cro-Magnons

2 issues

Mercenaries are hired to invade a planet whose people live like Ice Age hunters. Omega's computer goes through candidate worlds, giving real data about galaxies and star clusters (M101, M13, M33, M31), and settles on the planet Diane in Andromeda. The patrol helps a Cro-Magnon-style tribe and its old sorcerer, who tells of ancient "sky beings" who taught his ancestors, and the patrol fights off the invaders.

  1. Wrong in its dayE05 · 7:15high confidence
    "Nersi, small planet on the outer edge of spherical star cluster M13 in the constellation of Hercules. Cluster comprises 300,000 stars and has a diameter of 23 minutes of arc or 25,000 light years."

    TodayAbout 23 arcminutes is right for M13's apparent size, but its real diameter is only about 150–170 light years. About 25,000 light years is roughly its distance from us (current estimate about 22,000–25,000 ly), so the script has mixed up distance and diameter. The star count of a few hundred thousand is fine.

  2. Wrong in its dayE05 · 20:05high confidence
    "Plato spoke about an alien civilization brought to Earth people by gods from outer space."

    TodayPlato's Timaeus and Critias tell of Atlantis, founded by the sea god Poseidon. Nothing in them involves outer space or aliens. Reading Plato this way comes from 1960s–70s "ancient astronaut" books (von Däniken and others), not from the texts.

Simplified but fine (6)
  • M101 "in the constellation of the Great Bear ... 23,377,400 light years": the right constellation, and the distance matches the 1970s Sandage–Tammann value (7.2 Mpc). Modern values are about 21–22.5 million ly. The 8-digit precision is fake.
  • M33 "in the constellation of the triangle ... 2 million light years": the right constellation. The distance was usually given as about 2.3 million ly then and about 2.7–3 million ly today.
  • Andromeda galaxy M31, "400 billion stars ... 2 million light years": a fair 1980s round figure (about 2.2 million ly then, about 2.5 million ly today). Star-count estimates still range from a few hundred billion to about a trillion.
  • NGC 752 is a real open star cluster in Andromeda.
  • "In the Cro-Magnon days, the total population was about what it is in one city": estimates for the Upper Palaeolithic world are a few hundred thousand to a few million people.
  • Ice Age hunters living in caves and rock shelters, hunting with spears and firing clay figures in a kiln. Fired-clay figurines such as the Dolní Věstonice Venus date back about 26,000 years.
Episode 6

The Revolt of the Robots

No issues

On a colony planet the robots strike, take hostages and demand control of the planet after someone secretly rewrites their programming through the maintenance robots. Omega sends Metro and a giant robot to negotiate, and a duel between robots settles a peace that gives robots equal rights. It is a parable about labour and machines with no real science or history presented as fact.

Nothing outdated or wrong was found in this episode.

Simplified but fine (2)
  • "All men are also programmed by their genetic material and by their environment": a fair one-line summary of nature and nurture.
  • The David and Goliath reference is used correctly.
Episode 7

The Planet Mytho

No issues

The patrol finds the stranded crew of the ship "Olympus", a family named Jupiter, Hera, Ares, Hermes and so on. They live like gods above a primitive people and refuse to share fire or knowledge with them. The episode retells Greek myths (Peleus and Thetis, Eris's golden apple, the Sphinx's riddle, Prometheus's fire, Pandora) as a story about stranded space travellers, and Metro gives a one-line statement of Newton's law of gravitation.

Nothing outdated or wrong was found in this episode.

Simplified but fine (3)
  • "Each planet attracts its satellites with a force which is exactly proportional to its mass and inversely proportional to the square of the distance": Newton's law, except that the force is proportional to the product of both masses.
  • The myths are told recognisably: Zeus's interest in Thetis and her marriage to the mortal Peleus, Eris and the golden apple, the Sphinx's riddle (answer "man"), Prometheus giving fire, and Hephaestus making Pandora as punishment.
  • The episode mixes the Roman name Jupiter with Greek names (Hera, Ares, Hermes, Demeter); Demeter is correctly his sister.
Episode 8

The Long Voyage

No issues

Three explorers who left Earth in 2023 in suspended animation wake up after about a thousand years and reach the planet Omega. Humans settled it centuries ago, and ships with hyperspace drives now make the trip in days. The old professor is shown Omega's asteroid mining and hydrogen tankers, and Pierrot chases a hijacked mineral train and nearly gets lost in space during a repair. Almost everything is invented future technology. The real science is limited to how stars form from hydrogen and dust, ion engines, plotting orbits around the gravity of large bodies, and matter–antimatter annihilation.

Nothing outdated or wrong was found in this episode.

Simplified but fine (5)
  • Hydrogen "combined with dust particles from earlier stellar explosions, it gravitates towards a center eventually to form new stars": correct in outline. "Millions of centuries" (100+ million years) overstates it: a star like the Sun takes roughly 1–50 million years to go from collapsing cloud to stable star.
  • Early ships following calculated orbits around the "gravitation" of large bodies, and the ion engine as an efficient drive: both real (ion thrusters flew from 1964).
  • "Anti-matter supposes anti-time" loosely echoes the Feynman–Stückelberg idea of antiparticles as particles moving backwards in time. Particle–antiparticle collisions do annihilate into energy, but the idea that this could make "the universe, time, space" disappear is the show's fiction.
  • "As a famous author said, that is the question": Shakespeare, Hamlet.
  • "Forty generations" in a thousand years works out to 25 years a generation, a reasonable figure.
Episode 9

In Cassiopea

No issues

General Grouchy rallies Cassiopeia against the confederation, partly by showing a satirical film of an overcrowded, polluted, car-mad and over-armed Earth. He plans a secret base in the "Triton" system, next to an unstable star that could go supernova. Pierrot, Psyche and Metro are captured while investigating and escape with a smuggler. The real science is limited to the Sun's place in the galaxy and remarks on stars.

Nothing outdated or wrong was found in this episode.

Simplified but fine (3)
  • "The outer edge of the galaxy, the outer region where the solar system is located": the Sun is about 26,000 ly from the centre of a disc about 50,000 ly in radius, so "outer region" is loose but fair.
  • A system with a red giant and a white dwarf, an unstable star that "might explode at any moment in a supernova", and too much carbon dioxide raising the pulse rate are all plausible.
  • The Earth film ("30 billionth inhabitant ... born in Bombay", "40 tons [of explosives] per person", a 176-hour traffic jam) is deliberate satirical exaggeration, not offered as fact. For comparison, the 1980s figure for nuclear arsenals was about 3–4 tonnes of TNT equivalent per person.
Episode 10

A Planet Blown to Pieces

7 issues

Cassiopeia leaves the confederation, and Maestro warns that the star next to its new Triton base is about to become a supernova. Along the way he lectures on the size of the Milky Way, distances to nearby stars, how supernovae work, famous historical supernovae, other galaxies, and the Sun's future as a red giant. The star explodes, and Omega's ships fight through Cassiopeian fire to evacuate the colonists.

  1. Wrong in its dayE10 · 4:22high confidence
    "our own galaxy there in the Milky Way extends over a distance equivalent to that covered in 100 million light years."

    TodayThe Milky Way's disc is about 100,000 light years across (often given today as 100,000–120,000 ly or more). That was the standard figure in 1982 as well. "Million" is probably a slip for "thousand".

  2. Wrong in its dayE10 · 4:55medium confidence
    "And as for Auriga, it's only 47 light years, and Alderbarra, 43."

    TodayCapella (Alpha Aurigae) at about 43 ly (about 45 ly in 1982 references) is fine. Aldebaran is about 65 ly away (Hipparcos, 1997), and 1982 references gave about 68 ly, so 43 ly is wrong. The two figures may have been swapped or misread.

  3. Wrong in its dayE10 · 6:02medium confidence
    "Its outer shell is 15 times colder than its center."

    TodayIn a massive star near the end of its life, the core reaches billions of kelvin while the surface is a few thousand kelvin, a ratio of roughly a million, not 15.

  4. Common belief thenE10 · 6:18medium confidence
    "Yes, in the galaxy NC-34, the constellation of the Great Bear, an explosion pulverized well over a million stars in a few seconds."

    Today"NC-34" is almost certainly NGC 3034, i.e. M82 in Ursa Major. Lynds and Sandage (1963) described it as an "exploding galaxy" with a huge outburst from its core. By the late 1970s–80s it was understood as a starburst galaxy: intense star formation, probably set off by a close pass of M81, drives a "superwind" of gas out of the galaxy. No event destroyed a million stars in seconds, and no supernova could.

    Why it was believedThe "exploding galaxy M82" image from the 1960s stayed in popular astronomy books for years.

  5. Wrong in its dayE10 · 9:22high confidence
    "At that point, it will incorporate its satellites, Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, and Pluto."

    TodayAs a red giant the Sun is expected to swell to about 1 AU at most. Mercury and Venus will be swallowed; Earth is borderline (studies such as Schröder & Smith 2008 suggest it is engulfed). Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and Pluto, at 1.5 to 40 AU, will not be. The models known in 1982 gave the same picture. (The list also oddly leaves out Uranus and Neptune.)

  6. Wrong in its dayE10 · 9:28high confidence
    "But be assured, this will not happen for another five million years."

    TodayThe Sun has about 5 billion years left before it becomes a red giant. Astronomers already said so in 1982. Probably a translation slip (French milliards).

  7. Wrong in its dayE10 · 10:37medium confidence
    "In 1054, the Crab Nebula, in 1752, there was one in the Cassopoeia region, 1604, another one in the Neblia Officius."

    Today1054 (Crab) and 1604 (Kepler's supernova, in Ophiuchus) are right. The Cassiopeia supernova is Tycho's, in 1572. No supernova was seen in 1752. This may be a dub or speech-recognition error.

Simplified but fine (6)
  • "The mean distance lying between the center of the galaxy ... and the solar system is in the region of 27,000 light years": close to today's about 26,000–26,700 ly (GRAVITY 2019). In 1982 the IAU value was still 10 kpc (32,600 ly), but about 8.5 kpc (27,700 ly) was already being proposed and was adopted in 1985.
  • "Centaur is very near the sun, four light years": Alpha Centauri is about 4.4 ly away.
  • A star running out of fuel and collapsing, briefly shining like billions of suns, and throwing off much of its mass at about 10,000 km/s: a fair summary of a core-collapse supernova. At that speed debris would cross 5 billion km in about 6 days; the episode says "some eight days".
  • "Only in the 20th century ... it was realized that ... [many objects] are, in fact, independent galaxies": Hubble's Cepheids in Andromeda (1924–25). Some galaxies are much bigger than the Milky Way, but Andromeda is only about the same size or somewhat larger, not ten times larger.
  • The red giant growing "millions and millions of times" in size works if it means volume (a radius about 100–250 times larger).
  • "In 2421, another one that was recorded in the region of Urn": the series is set in the future, so this is a fictional future supernova, not an error.
Episode 11

Shipwrecked in Space

1 issue

A cruiser damaged by the supernova crash-lands on an uncharted green planet. While the crew struggles to survive, Pierrot, Psyche and Metro explore, meet friendly telepathic "Patakis" and dangerous ape-like "Zabus", and find Cassiopeian prospectors; a cowardly officer, Sanders, wants to surrender to them. Real science comes in brief remarks about Earth's prehistoric animals, mammals and radio and the ionosphere.

  1. Wrong in its dayE11 · 9:51medium confidence
    "But consider, it wasn't so very long ago, a few hundred million years on Earth, the creatures didn't look much like you either. I recall to you the Splendous Clutosaurus and the Demetrodon and the Cignathosaurus. Very nice in its way. And there is also the Moa and the Tyrannosaurus."

    TodayDimetrodon (about 295–272 million years ago), Cynognathus (about 245–235 million years ago) and Tyrannosaurus (about 68–66 million years ago) fit the "hundreds of millions of years" idea, but the moa does not. It was a giant flightless bird in New Zealand, hunted to extinction by the Māori about 600 years ago (around 1400 AD). "Splendous Clutosaurus" is unidentifiable (a speech-recognition garble).

Simplified but fine (4)
  • "Mammals of the plasticine [Pleistocene] era": a reasonable label for Ice Age-looking mammals.
  • "All mammals are edible ... because their biochemical bases are the same": a broad rule of thumb; a few mammal tissues are toxic (e.g. polar bear liver, from too much vitamin A).
  • "This is a relatively old planet. There isn't one active volcano here": reasonable reasoning, since small or old worlds lose internal heat.
  • A short-wave transmitter whose signal is trapped below the ionosphere, so it needs a relay in orbit: broadly correct radio physics.
Episode 12

The Giants

4 issues

Pierrot, Psyche and Metro are sent to the fourth planet of a dying star to warn any inhabitants. They find giant trees and giant termites and are trapped inside a huge termite mound, where they watch soldiers, workers, the king and queen, and a colony breeding frantically as its sun fades. Metro gives a lecture on termites: how old they are, their climate limits, the gut protozoa that digest wood, their number of species, and their use of excrement and dead nestmates. The episode also touches on white dwarf stars and chitin.

  1. Wrong in its dayE12 · 14:25medium confidence
    "King, he's the only one that can fly."

    TodayThe king and queen fly only on the swarming (nuptial) flight, then shed their wings. In an established nest the king cannot fly. The winged members of a mature colony are the young future kings and queens (alates). The line may be garbled in the transcript.

  2. Common belief thenE12 · 15:31medium confidence
    "It would appear that these charming creatures go back to the primary era of just a hop, skip, and jump back in time, so to speak, 250 million years."

    TodayThe oldest known termite fossils are from the Early Cretaceous (about 130–135 million years ago; English and Spanish finds were already described by 1982). Molecular dating puts their origin in the Late Jurassic, about 150–170 million years ago, so not in the "primary era" (Paleozoic, which ended 252 million years ago). Since 2007 (Inward and colleagues) termites have been classed as a group of social cockroaches.

    Why it was believedTermites were long described as "living fossils" related to cockroaches, and older books often dated them back to the Permian or earlier along with cockroaches.

  3. Wrong in its dayE12 · 15:46medium confidence
    "They can live only in tropical climate. Below 20 degrees centigrade, they perish."

    TodayMost termite species are tropical, but many live in temperate zones. Reticulitermes species have damaged houses in western and southwestern France (Bordeaux, La Rochelle) since the 18th–19th centuries and range across Europe and North America. Termites do keep their nests warm and humid, and many tropical species suffer outside that range, but the rule does not apply to termites in general.

  4. Wrong in its dayE12 · 18:47medium confidence
    "Then it concerned human beings in 1304."

    TodayNo known event in 1304 matches "a population that breeds faster when facing death". The usual example is the rise in births after the Black Death (1347–51). The date may be a mishearing or a slip in the dub.

Simplified but fine (7)
  • A dying sun-like star "will change into a white dwarf" after throwing off its outer layers: correct (the red-giant stage comes first). "NGC 16612" is made up; the NGC catalogue stops at 7840.
  • Chitin is "found in the shells of insects ... spiders and lobsters, and also in membranes of certain mushrooms": correct (fungal cell walls).
  • Soldiers cannot feed themselves and are fed by workers; workers and soldiers are blind, wingless and sterile, and many never see daylight: correct.
  • Gut "protozoan" digest the wood and "can attain half the weight of their host": the classic figure (one-third to one-half) for lower termites. Fungus-growing Macrotermes bellicosus has no such protozoa.
  • "More than a thousand different varieties": about 2,000 species were known in 1982 and about 3,000 today. "Bellicoses" is Macrotermes bellicosus; "insertas" is probably a mishearing.
  • Termites use excrement to build and to feed nestmates, and they eat their dead: correct.
  • Leibniz's "predetermined harmony" refers to his "pre-established harmony".
Episode 13

The Incas

2 issues

Maestro and the patrol visit a planet with a "pre-Incan" Andean-style civilisation. They are shown its storehouses, knotted-cord quipu records, rope bridges, ayllu communities, decimal chain of chiefs, chasqui relay runners and gold-working. A local legend tells of bearded sky-gods whose anger made a desert, and a palace faction steals the visitors' lasers before order is restored. Most of the real history comes from what is known of the Inca Empire.

  1. Wrong in its dayE13 · 9:42medium confidence
    "Your society is magnificent, exactly as depicted in all the accounts of prehistory on the Earth. It is an Indian civilization in the year 1000."

    TodayWhat is shown (the decimal chain of officials, chasqui relay runners, state storehouses, the ayllu system under an imperial chief) is the Inca Empire, which expanded from about 1438 and fell to the Spanish in 1532–33. Around 1000 AD the Andes were dominated by Tiwanaku and Wari, which had some similar features (Wari used khipus and roads) but not this system.

  2. Wrong in its dayE13 · 13:48medium confidence
    "These bellows are the largest, most powerful ever built"

    TodayAndean metalworkers had no bellows before the Spanish. They melted metal with blowpipes and wind-driven clay furnaces (huayrachinas) on hillsides, described by Spanish chroniclers such as José de Acosta. (The surrounding transcript is garbled.)

Simplified but fine (5)
  • Quipu (knotted cords) as records of quantities; the colour code given (yellow for gold, etc.) follows the chronicler Garcilaso de la Vega and is only partly confirmed.
  • Ayllu as the basic kin and land-sharing community, and the decimal hierarchy of officials responsible for 10, 100, 1,000 households and so on.
  • Chicha (maize beer), llamas as pack animals, gold panned from rivers, and grass-rope suspension bridges plaited from smaller cords (like the Q'eswachaka bridge, still rebuilt every year).
  • Chasqui relay runners covering "1,000 kilometers in less than three days": at the high end of chroniclers' estimates (about 240 km a day is often quoted), but plausible.
  • The legend of pale, bearded sky-gods echoes the Viracocha myth and 1970s "ancient astronaut" ideas. It is told as a local legend, not as fact.
Episode 14

In the Land of the Dinosaurs

7 issues

Pierrot and Psyche crash-land on a volcanic planet that is in its "age of dinosaurs" and meet Allosaurus, Deinonychus, Brontosaurus, Stegosaurus, Megalosaurus, Tyrannosaurus and the sea reptile Tylosaurus while Metro repairs the ship. Along the way they discuss the "primeval soup" and the origin of life, dinosaur sizes, how long dinosaurs ruled, and why they died out (Metro favours a nearby supernova).

  1. Wrong in its dayE14 · 5:23high confidence
    "Planet formed out of dense gaseous clouds when its sun was formed 400 billion years ago."

    TodayThe universe is about 13.8 billion years old (in 1982 estimates were about 10–20 billion), so no star or planet can be 400 billion years old. With the old British long-scale billion it is even worse. Earth is 4.54 billion years old. Probably a slip for 4 billion.

  2. Wrong in its dayE14 · 5:31medium confidence
    "Primeval soup brewed over 100 million years ago, sufficient to produce life."

    TodayOn Earth, life appeared at least 3.5 billion years ago, and getting from the first cells to large animals such as dinosaurs took more than 3 billion years. A world where life began only 100 million years ago could not yet have dinosaurs.

  3. Wrong in its dayE14 · 15:00medium confidence
    "the same men were very wise in my opinion to appear on earth a hundred million years after these nice moving mountains had disappeared"

    TodayThe last non-bird dinosaurs (including sauropods related to "Brontosaurus") died out 66 million years ago, and the genus Homo appeared about 2.5–2.8 million years ago. The gap is about 63–64 million years, not 100 million.

  4. Wrong in its dayE14 · 15:10high confidence
    "those beauties weigh 10 tons apiece" (Allosaurus)

    TodayAllosaurus weighed about 1.5–2.5 tonnes (large individuals perhaps up to about 4). Books in 1982 gave a similar 2–4 tonnes. Ten tonnes is closer to Tyrannosaurus.

  5. OutdatedE14 · 18:32high confidence
    "and then, one day they all disappeared. They died out."

    Today"They all disappeared" is outdated: birds are living dinosaurs. John Ostrom argued this in the 1970s, it became the consensus in the 1990s, and feathered dinosaurs from China (Sinosauropteryx, 1996, and many since) confirmed it. Only the non-bird dinosaurs died out.

  6. Wrong in its dayE14 · 18:32high confidence
    "ruled over it for 30 million years"

    TodayDinosaurs appeared about 230 million years ago and the non-bird dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago, about 165 million years in all. Books in 1982 already gave about 140–160 million years.

  7. OutdatedE14 · 18:44high confidence
    "There are several explanations for that. Lack of food... Climactic changes. The appearance of glaciers. But the most probable explanation is a supernova explosion so near that its cosmic radiation killed most of life on the planet."

    TodayThe end-Cretaceous extinction (66 million years ago) is now attributed mainly to an asteroid impact, proposed by Alvarez et al. in 1980 from the iridium layer and confirmed when the Chicxulub crater in Mexico was identified in 1990–91. Massive Deccan Traps volcanism probably also contributed. The supernova idea (Schindewolf 1950s; Russell & Tucker 1971) was a serious candidate in the 1970s, but Alvarez's team had already found no plutonium-244 in the boundary clay, which a supernova would have left. There were no glaciers at the end of the Cretaceous; the climate was warm.

Simplified but fine (5)
  • Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen as the building blocks of amino acids in a "primeval soup": the Oparin–Haldane idea and the Miller–Urey experiment (1953). How life began is still unresolved.
  • "Brontosaurus. He weighs at least 30 tons ... herbivorous": the right size range (about 15–30 tonnes). In 1982 the name was officially a synonym of Apatosaurus, but Tschopp et al. (2015) revived Brontosaurus as a valid genus.
  • Brontosaurus herds with adults guarding young in the middle: supported by sauropod trackways, though herd structure is still debated.
  • The planet is called "Jurassic", but the animals mix Jurassic (Allosaurus, Stegosaurus, Brontosaurus, Megalosaurus) and Late Cretaceous (Tyrannosaurus, Tylosaurus) species. These are about 80 million years apart on Earth; this is acceptable on an alien world. Tylosaurus was a mosasaur, a marine reptile, not a dinosaur.
  • Deinonychus, Stegosaurus and Megalosaurus are named correctly.
Episode 15

The Rings of Saturn

9 issues

Psyche takes a holiday trip with Metro to test the theory that the asteroid belt is a planet destroyed in an ancient war. She flies past Uranus and through Saturn's rings, is captured by humanoid machines at a secret base and interrogated. Pierrot rescues her, and they escape past Jupiter into the asteroid belt. Along the way the episode gives many facts about Uranus, Saturn and its rings and moons, Jupiter and its moons, and the asteroids.

Pierrot stays on Earth and is shown round its ocean farms, old drilling platforms and the underwater city of Poseidia.   1. Wrong in its dayE18 · 8:28high confidence
    "No, you realize the pressure here is three tons per square centimeter."

    TodayAt the stated depth of 7,000 m, seawater pressure is about 700 atmospheres (about 70 MPa), or roughly 0.7 tonnes per square centimetre. "Three tons" is about four times too high. Pressure rises by about 1 atmosphere (about 1 kg/cm²) every 10 m, which was textbook knowledge.

  2. Wrong in its dayE18 · 16:24high confidence
    "Their presence accounts for the lapse of five million years"

    TodayThere was no gap between Neanderthals and Cro-Magnons. The two overlapped in Europe: Cro-Magnons (early modern humans) arrived about 45,000–40,000 years ago, and the last Neanderthals died out about 40,000 years ago (about 35,000 in 1982 references). Five million years ago there were not even any members of the genus Homo.

Simplified but fine (8)
  • Food chain "one kilogram of tuna fish required ten kilograms of sardines ... 100 kilograms of animal plankton ... 1,000 of vegetable plankton": the standard rough "10% rule" for energy passed between levels of a food chain.
  • Ocean thermal energy, "a difference of some 20 degrees for each 1,000 meters of depth": OTEC plants do need about 20 °C between warm surface water and cold water from about 1,000 m. The wording "for each 1,000 meters" is off, though: the drop is not repeated per kilometre, and deep water stays at about 2–4 °C.
  • "oh plant is what they really like to eat" (whales) is probably a mishearing of "plankton". Baleen whales eat animal plankton (krill, copepods) and small fish, not plant plankton.
  • Plant plankton as the base of the ocean food chain, using photosynthesis to absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen: correct; it produces roughly half of Earth's oxygen.
  • Metals "cobalt uranium nickel copper ... all from the sea": seabed manganese nodules are rich in nickel, copper and cobalt, and seawater holds dissolved uranium.
  • Rapture of the deep: most divers breathing air "lose their judgment at less than 100 meters" is true (nitrogen narcosis usually starts around 30 m). The 80 m limit is generous for air but could fit a gas mix. Slow, staged decompression stops on the way up are correct.
  • "Australopithecus, those evolved monkeys that peopled the Earth" over a million years ago: Australopithecus lived about 4–2 million years ago (robust relatives until about 1.2 million). They were early hominins (apes), not monkeys. The aliens guiding human evolution is the episode's fiction.
  • Atlantis: the details match Plato (Timaeus and Critias): Egyptian priests told Solon of it, it was "greater than Libya and Asia" put together, it lay beyond the Pillars of Heracles ("On Gibraltar" is probably a mishearing of "beyond Gibraltar"), its army conquered lands around the Mediterranean before Athens defeated it, and it sank about 9,000 years before Solon (about 9600 BC, matching "9,560 years before our era"). Historians and archaeologists regard Atlantis as Plato's invention, and there is no evidence that it existed; the episode frames it as Pete's dreams and a "fanciful theory".
