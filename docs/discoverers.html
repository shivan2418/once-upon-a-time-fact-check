Episode 1

The Chinese, Our Ancestors

1 major error · 11 minor

Maestro opens the series by promising the story of the great discoverers, then spends the episode on early Chinese inventions. He covers row farming and the seed drill, horse harnesses, stirrups, kites and the first man carried by a kite, suspension bridges, the parachute, paper, paper money, printing, movable type and gunpowder. The message is that China was often centuries ahead of Europe. The episode closes on a plea to use inventions for peace rather than war.

  1. Common belief thenE01 · 3:48high confidence
    "Paper, playing cards, the decimal system, steam engines, the parachute..."

    TodayChina never had a steam engine. This claim comes from Robert Temple's popular The Genius of China (1986), which simplified Joseph Needham. Needham argued only that Chinese water-powered bellows (Du Shi, 31 AD) used the same crank-and-connecting-rod layout later used by steam engines. Nothing in China was ever driven by steam. The same list's "hydraulic power, even the steam engine" repeats the error.

    Why it was believedTemple's list of Chinese "firsts" was a bestseller and was widely copied in the late 1980s and 1990s. Many items on its list are real, and this one sounded just as solid.

  2. Common belief thenMinor slipE01 · 3:57medium confidence
    "How blood circulates."

    TodayThe Huangdi Neijing (around the 2nd–1st century BC) describes blood and qi flowing in continuous cycles through channels. That is a real and early idea, but it is not circulation in William Harvey's sense (1628): a heart pumping blood out through arteries and back through veins in a closed loop, proved by measurement. Calling it the discovery of circulation 1,000–2,000 years before Europe overstates it.

    Why it was believedTemple, following Needham's more cautious wording, presented it as "the circulation of the blood".

  3. Common belief thenMinor slipE01 · 4:02medium confidence
    "And brandy as well."

    TodaySecure evidence of distilled alcohol in China dates only from the Jin and Yuan dynasties (12th–13th century). That is about when distilled "aqua vitae" appears in Salerno and elsewhere in Europe. Earlier Chinese dates are argued but not proven, so China being far ahead on brandy is doubtful.

    Why it was believedTemple's book gave an early Chinese date for distilling spirits.

  4. Common belief thenMinor slipE01 · 9:15high confidence
    "The same horse could now pull ten times the load."

    TodayThe Chinese did develop the breast-strap harness (by about the 4th century BC) and the collar harness (around the 5th century AD) well before Europe. However, the claim that the ancient "throat-and-girth" harness choked horses, and that the collar multiplied pulling power several times over, comes from Richard Lefebvre des Noëttes (1931). Trials by J. Spruytte (1977) and later work showed that the ancient harness, properly fitted, did not choke the horse. The "ten times" figure has no basis.

    Why it was believedLefebvre des Noëttes' thesis was taken up by Needham and Lynn White and stayed standard in popular histories of technology.

  5. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE01 · 10:58high confidence
    "The Chinese were 2,000 years ahead."

    TodayThe earliest stirrups are Chinese: a single mounting stirrup on a figurine from 302 AD and paired stirrups by about 322 AD. Stirrups reached Europe with the Avars around the 6th–7th century. That is a lead of roughly 300–400 years, not 2,000. India had toe-loop stirrups even earlier. The show is right that the Egyptians, Greeks and Romans rode without stirrups.

  6. Common belief thenMinor slipE01 · 11:31medium confidence
    "The Chinese stirrup set the stage of history for Western knights in armor."

    TodayThis is Lynn White's "stirrup thesis" (1962): stirrups made mounted shock combat possible and so created feudal knighthood. Historians (Sawyer and Hilton 1963, Bachrach 1970 and others) rejected it soon after. Steppe horsemen conquered before they had stirrups, and Frankish adoption of stirrups did not line up with any change in tactics.

    Why it was believedWhite's Medieval Technology and Social Change was hugely influential and memorable, and popular books kept repeating it.

  7. Common belief thenMinor slipE01 · 11:59medium confidence
    "The universal joint that makes car transmissions possible."

    TodayThe Chinese device (Ding Huan, about 180 AD) was a gimbal (a "Cardan suspension"), not a universal joint. Gimbals were also known earlier in the Greek world (Philo of Byzantium, 3rd century BC). The universal joint used in drive shafts goes back to Cardano and Robert Hooke (17th century).

    Why it was believedTemple's list calls the gimbal a "Cardan suspension", which is easily confused with the cardan (universal) joint.

  8. Common belief thenMinor slipE01 · 17:24medium confidence
    "This is the story of a young man who became emperor in the Zia dynasty."

    TodayThis is the legend of Shun, recorded by Sima Qian (around 90 BC), who escaped from a burning granary by holding two large bamboo hats and jumping. Shun is a legendary ruler, traditionally placed before the Xia dynasty, around 2200 BC (about 4,200 years ago, not "nearly 5,000"). The story is a legend, not evidence that the parachute existed "centuries before our era". ("Zia" is Xia.)

    Why it was believedTemple presented it as the first parachute, and popular books repeated it.

  9. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE01 · 20:05medium confidence
    "Then you will have the invention of the parachute in Europe, straight from China."

    TodayThe European parachute did not come "straight from China". The report that inspired it was Simon de La Loubère's account (1691) of acrobats in Siam (Thailand), not China. It inspired Louis-Sébastien Lenormand, who jumped from a tower in Montpellier in 1783 and coined the word "parachute". André-Jacques Garnerin made the first jump from a balloon on 22 October 1797 over Paris, from about 900–1,000 m. That was a hydrogen balloon, not one of the Montgolfiers'. Faust Vrančić had already published a parachute design in 1595. (The garbled "1080" may be a mishearing of "1680s".)

  10. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE01 · 21:52medium confidence
    "It was only in the 13th century that paper was to be made in Europe."

    TodayPaper was made in Muslim Spain, at Xàtiva near Valencia, by the mid-12th century, and it was used there earlier. The 13th century is right for Christian Europe (Fabriano in Italy, around 1270s). "15 centuries after China" is about right either way.

  11. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE01 · 22:13medium confidence
    "1041, a Chinese Picheng invents movable type, 26 characters in the West, up to 400,000 in China."

    TodayBi Sheng ("Picheng") made ceramic movable type in the 1040s, which is correct. But no Chinese printer used 400,000 characters. The largest classical dictionary, the Kangxi Dictionary of 1716, has about 47,000, and a working font needed a few thousand characters (with many copies of each). Wang Zhen's wooden type (about 1300) had around 60,000 pieces. "400,000" is probably a dub or transcription slip for 40,000.

  12. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE01 · 22:32medium confidence
    "They were rifles, and mortars, and cannons, incendiary missiles, even flamethrowers."

    TodayChina did invent gunpowder weapons: fire lances, bombs, rockets, hand cannons and cannons. Rifling (spiral grooves in the barrel) was a European invention (Germany, late 15th century), so "rifles" is wrong. The Chinese pump flamethrower (919 AD) burned petroleum oil bought from the Arab world, and the Byzantines had been spraying "Greek fire" from siphons since the 670s.

Simplified but fine (21)
  • Porcelain about 1,700 years before Europe: true porcelain dates from the Eastern Han (around the 2nd century AD) and Meissen from 1708–10. Roughly 1,500–1,600 years.
  • Playing cards (Tang dynasty, 9th century) came about five centuries before Europe (1370s).
  • Hand cannons appeared in 13th-century China (the Heilongjiang gun, about 1288). Calling them pistols is loose, but China came first.
  • Decimal place value with counting rods, matches (sulphur-tipped sticks, 577 AD), the compass, the escapement-driven astronomical clock (Yi Xing 725, Su Song 1088), rockets, chain pumps ("conveyor belt"), deep drilling for brine and gas ("petroleum") and woodblock printing are all genuine Chinese firsts.
  • Row cultivation (6th century BC), iron ploughs and the multi-tube seed drill (2nd century BC; misheard as "multiple row sewing machine") came long before Europe's seed drills (16th–18th century).
  • The breast-strap and collar harnesses appeared in China about 1,000 years before Europe, setting aside the "ten times the load" claim.
  • The magic lantern as a Chinese forerunner of cinema is a stretch (the Chinese "trotting horse lamp" was a rotating shadow lamp). The European magic lantern dates from the 1650s.
  • The pound lock (Qiao Weiyue, 984), paddle-wheel boats and the sternpost rudder (1st century AD, Europe about 1180) are fair Chinese firsts.
  • Biological pest control with citrus ants is recorded in 304 AD, about 16 centuries before the West.
  • The monk Huaibing raised sunken iron oxen with boats as floats in the 11th century.
  • Baden Baden-Powell was lifted by a kite in 1894, and Otto Lilienthal's gliders opened the way to powered flight.
  • Kites date to about the 5th–4th century BC, and kites were flown for help during the siege of Nanjing (549).
  • Emperor Gao Yang (Wenxuan of Northern Qi) launched prisoners on kites in 559. Yuan Huangtou flew about 2.5 km and survived, around 1,335 years before Baden-Powell.
  • Bamboo-cable suspension bridges in Sichuan (the Anlan bridge, misheard as "mainland bridge") are traditionally dated to the 3rd century BC, though the date is uncertain. China's iron-chain bridges came about 1,500 years before Europe's (1801).
  • The show rightly corrects the popular idea that Leonardo invented the parachute.
  • Writing appeared on Sumerian clay tablets, though about 5,200 years ago (Uruk, around 3200 BC) rather than 6,000. Egyptian writing came at roughly the same time.
  • Paper dates to the 2nd century BC from archaeology (Fangmatan, Baqiao), well before the traditional Cai Lun date of 105 AD. The show is ahead of most popular books here.
  • Paper armour, paper money (Sichuan jiaozi, early 11th century), death for counterfeiting (printed on Yuan notes) and Europe's first banknotes (Stockholm, 1661) are all correct.
  • The Diamond Sutra (868) is the oldest dated printed book. Undated earlier prints exist (Korea and Japan, 8th century).
  • The Mongols reached Silesia and Hungary in 1241, and Gutenberg's press followed about two centuries later. Any transmission of printing from China to Europe is unproven.
  • Gunpowder came from Daoist alchemists searching for an elixir (9th century), and rockets reached Europe by the 13th century.
Episode 2

Archimedes and the Greeks

1 major error · 6 minor

Maestro praises Greek thought: Pythagoras, Hippocrates, Socrates, Phidias, Protagoras, Anaxagoras and Democritus, whose atoms are acted out in a scene. The gnomon then leads to Eratosthenes measuring the Earth from shadows at Syene and Alexandria. Most of the episode follows Archimedes in Syracuse: the golden crown and "Eureka", the screw pump, the ship moved with one hand, the area of the circle, and the Roman siege in which he dies.

  1. Common belief thenE02 · 1:25medium confidence
    "They rose to heights 2,000 years past before there was really anything new."

    TodayBetween the Greeks and the Scientific Revolution, science moved forward in the Islamic world (al-Khwarizmi, Ibn al-Haytham, al-Biruni), in India (place-value numerals, zero), in China (as episode 1 itself shows) and in medieval Europe (Oxford calculators, Buridan, Oresme). Historians had long rejected the idea of 2,000 empty years.

    Why it was believedThe Renaissance story of a classical golden age, then "dark ages", then rebirth was still standard in popular books.

  2. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE02 · 1:52medium confidence
    "Athena, 60 feet high."

    TodayPhidias' gold-and-ivory Athena Parthenos was about 11.5–12 m (around 38–40 ft), not 60 ft. His bronze Athena Promachos may have been taller, but estimates for it also fall short of 60 ft.

  3. Common belief thenMinor slipE02 · 6:39high confidence
    "the length of the running track used in the olympics 252 000 stadiums that comes to exactly"

    TodayEratosthenes' method was sound. He took the angle of the Sun's shadow at Alexandria (about 7.2°, or 1/50 of a circle) when the Sun stood overhead at Syene, and multiplied by the distance between the two cities. But we do not know which stadion he used. The Olympic track is about 192 m, which gives about 48,000 km, roughly 20% too large. The show's "exactly 39,690 km" assumes a stadion of 157.5 m, a disputed modern reconstruction. Estimates for his stadion range from about 157 to 185 m, so his error was somewhere between about 1% and 16%. The 5,000-stadion distance was itself a rough figure, and Syene is not due south of Alexandria. "Only 300 km off" is a choice of number, not a fact.

    Why it was believedThe "within 1%" version was popular in textbooks and television (it fits the 157.5 m stadion). Few popular accounts mentioned that the length of the stadion is unknown.

  4. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE02 · 7:20medium confidence
    "Alexandria is a peaceful city, and I studied with Euclid, the greatest mathematician in the world."

    TodayEuclid was active around 300 BC, and Archimedes was born around 287 BC. If Archimedes studied in Alexandria (Diodorus says he spent time in Egypt), it was with Euclid's successors. His known correspondents there were Conon of Samos, Dositheus and Eratosthenes. Standard references in 1994 already said "probably studied under Euclid's successors".

  5. Common belief thenMinor slipE02 · 11:07medium confidence
    "Notice the weight of its volume in water that overflowed."

    TodayThe bath, "Eureka" and the naked run through the streets come from Vitruvius, writing about 200 years later. The method he describes, comparing the water displaced by the crown with that displaced by an equal weight of gold, would barely have worked. For a 1 kg crown with 30% silver, the extra volume is about 13 cm³, a rise of a fraction of a millimetre in any reasonable vessel. Galileo (La Bilancetta, 1586) suggested that Archimedes more likely weighed the crown against gold while both hung in water, using his own law of buoyancy. The show gives the Vitruvius version as fact.

    Why it was believedIt is one of the best-known stories in the history of science, and Vitruvius is an ancient source.

  6. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE02 · 14:02medium confidence
    "The blades are turned by the current in the river."

    TodayThe Archimedes screw was turned by hand or by people treading it, as in Roman mines and Egyptian fields. It does not run on its own from a river current. No ancient source describes a screw driven by the current, and Vitruvius says men turned it by treading. Diodorus credits Archimedes with the screw during his stay in Egypt. Some historians think it is older still: Stephanie Dalley argued in the 1990s for Sennacherib's Assyria.

  7. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE02 · 16:45high confidence
    "The squaring of the circle."

    TodayArchimedes did not square the circle. With compass and straightedge it cannot be done, as Lindemann proved in 1882 when he showed that π is transcendental. In Measurement of a Circle he proved that a circle's area equals a right triangle with the radius and the circumference as its sides. He trapped π between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7 (3.1408–3.1429) using polygons of 96 sides, not between an outer and an inner square. The value 3.1416 is Ptolemy's (377/120), not Archimedes'. That areas of circles go as the square of the radius was already in Euclid (XII.2).

Simplified but fine (10)
  • "Eureka" means "I have found it", and the gnomon really can serve as a sundial, find north and measure heights by shadows (Thales' pyramid trick). The "250 feet" pyramid is unnamed, but the Great Pyramid was about 146 m (481 ft).
  • Pythagoras, Hippocrates "founding" medicine, Protagoras' "man is the measure" and Anaxagoras' nous are fair summaries. "Know thyself" was a Delphic maxim adopted by Socrates, and "I know that I know nothing" paraphrases Plato's Apology.
  • Democritus (with his teacher Leucippus) proposed indivisible atoms about 2,400 years ago. His "laughing philosopher" nickname is traditional. Atoms were first imaged in the 1950s–80s (field-ion microscope, then the STM), and modern atoms are not indivisible.
  • The four elements were Empedocles' theory. Showing evaporation as water becoming "air" fairly reflects Greek thinking.
  • Eratosthenes did his work around 240 BC, using the summer solstice at Syene ("Siena"/"Cien" = Aswan), near the Tropic of Cancer, and got 252,000 stadia. In the anecdote, the Sun lit the bottom of a well.
  • Syracuse was a large, rich Greek city in the 3rd century BC. "Half a million" is the top of old estimates, and modern figures are lower.
  • Archimedes' father Phidias was an astronomer (Archimedes says so in The Sand Reckoner). "Court astronomer" is embellishment.
  • Hiero's challenge: Plutarch says Archimedes single-handedly drew a loaded ship ashore with compound pulleys. "Give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth" is attributed to him by Pappus and others ("Comedians" is a mishearing of Archimedes).
  • Hiero II died in 215 BC, and his young grandson Hieronymus was assassinated in 214. Marcellus besieged Syracuse in 214–212 BC, Archimedes' machines held off the Romans, and a soldier killed him despite Marcellus' order to spare him.
  • The sledgehammer test of the crown and the goldsmith's confession are dramatic invention, but the reasoning (same weight, more volume, a less dense metal such as silver) is correct.
Episode 3

Hero of Alexandria

1 major error · 7 minor

The episode starts with Aristotle's Lyceum and his pupil Alexander, then follows the founding of Alexandria, its lighthouse, its giant ships and the Library's hunt for books. Ctesibius shows off his inventions. The main story pairs Hero and Ptolemy against foolish Roman officials: Hero's steam turbine (aeolipile), his self-opening temple doors and his gear-driven lifting machine (baroulkos), and Ptolemy's maps. It ends with the decline of the Alexandrian school and the destruction of 391.

  1. Common belief thenMinor slipE03 · 1:26medium confidence
    "Peripatetic. That means everybody walking about because his students weren't stuck sitting in one place."

    Today"Peripatetic" most likely comes from the peripatos, the covered walkway of the Lyceum where the school met. The story that Aristotle lectured while walking around is an ancient explanation of the name, not established fact.

    Why it was believedThe walking-teacher story is old (Diogenes Laërtius) and appealing, and it was repeated in most popular accounts.

  2. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE03 · 2:19high confidence
    "Ptolemy succeeded Alexander and built a great library and his son Ptolemy II a lighthouse 40 meters high, one of the wonders of the world."

    TodayThe Pharos of Alexandria was about 100–130 m high, one of the tallest buildings of the ancient world, not 40 m. It was begun under Ptolemy I and finished under Ptolemy II (around 280 BC).

  3. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE03 · 2:19medium confidence
    "Just imagine, the mast was 10 stories high, and there were 2,000 oarsmen."

    TodayThe giant "forty" (tessarakonteres) of Ptolemy IV, described by Athenaeus and Plutarch, had 4,000 rowers, plus 400 other crew and about 2,850 marines. The show is right that it was too big to be useful.

  4. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE03 · 7:50medium confidence
    "I am working on this invention, an instrument for measuring any differences in heat."

    TodayNo thermometer is attributed to Ctesibius ("Sestibus"). Philo of Byzantium and Hero described devices in which warmed air expands and pushes water along. These showed the effect but had no scale and measured nothing. The first scaled thermometers came from Santorio and Galileo's circle around 1600–1612.

  5. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE03 · 10:04medium confidence
    "You see, Hero, the Earth's round and the Sun goes around it."

    TodayHero and Ptolemy were not contemporaries. Otto Neugebauer (1938) dated Hero by a lunar eclipse in 62 AD, so Hero was active in the mid-1st century. Ptolemy's observations run from 127 to 141 AD, and the Almagest dates to about 150. The friendship is invented, and Hero would have been dead or very old.

  6. Common belief thenE03 · 10:42medium confidence
    "the earth's as flat as a millstone we all know that you've only to look around you"

    TodayEducated Romans knew the Earth was a sphere: Cicero (Dream of Scipio), Pliny the Elder, Strabo and Seneca all say so. The show plays Roman officials as flat-earthers for comedy. In fact, Greek and Roman learned culture agreed on a round Earth, and Ptolemy wrote under Roman rule.

    Why it was believedThe myth that people before Columbus thought the Earth was flat was spread in the 19th century (Washington Irving, Draper, A. D. White). Historians had exposed it (J. B. Russell, Inventing the Flat Earth, 1991), but it remained common in popular culture.

  7. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE03 · 21:49high confidence
    "It's a bit like a lyre or a harp, but with small hammers that come down upon the strings."

    TodayHero did not invent a hammered string instrument. That idea (dulcimer, then clavichord and piano) is medieval and later. Hero's real musical inventions were organs: he described Ctesibius' water organ (hydraulis) and an organ blown by a windmill. Clea and the instrument are fiction.

  8. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE03 · 22:54high confidence
    "Be among the last scientists of the school of Alexandria."

    TodayAlexandria went on producing major scholars for three more centuries after Hero: Ptolemy, Diophantus (3rd century), Pappus (4th century), Theon and his daughter Hypatia (died 415), and John Philoponus (6th century).

Simplified but fine (13)
  • Aristotle founded the Lyceum in Athens around 335 BC ("more than 23 centuries ago") and was Alexander's tutor. Plato's Academy came earlier.
  • Alexander founded Alexandria in 331 BC. The Museum and Library were Ptolemaic projects, not Alexander's, so the founding dialogue is dramatization.
  • Demetrius of Phalerum organizing the Library, the searching of ships for books, keeping originals and returning copies (Galen's account), Xenodotus as first librarian and "400,000" scrolls all match ancient sources. "A million different words" is probably a mishearing (ancient figures top out at about 700,000 scrolls).
  • Ctesibius did invent the force pump, an improved water clock and air-spring devices. The line-drawing clock statue is a loose rendering of his pointer clock.
  • Caesar's fire in Alexandria was in 48 BC (the show says '47). How much of the Library burned is disputed.
  • Egypt was a Roman province from 30 BC, and Alexandria stayed a scientific centre for centuries.
  • Ptolemy's geocentric system and star catalogue were standard until Copernicus.
  • Hero's aeolipile was a working steam reaction turbine with no practical use, and Vitruvius describes simpler aeolipiles earlier.
  • Hero's automatic temple doors are explained correctly in the show: heated air pushes water into a bucket, and cooling siphons it back so a counterweight closes the doors. The device runs on hot air, not steam.
  • The baroulkos ("Barakos"), a gear train that lifts large loads with a small force, really is described by Hero.
  • Ptolemy's Geography reached Latin Europe in about 1406–10 and influenced Columbus and Copernicus. No maps drawn by Ptolemy himself survive, only maps rebuilt from his coordinates. "Father of geography" is fair.
  • Ptolemy described armillary instruments (the astrolabon). The flat astrolabe owes more to Hipparchus and later writers.
  • "The library was pillaged by the Romans": Caesar's fire (48 BC) and Aurelian's war (270s) damaged it. In 391 the Christian patriarch Theophilus destroyed the Serapeum. "Byzantine Empire" is anachronistic for 391, since the Roman Empire was not formally split until 395.
Episode 4

The Measuring of Time

2 major errors · 10 minor

A quick history of timekeeping. It covers the origins of the week, Egyptian water clocks for night work, the clepsydra used to limit Athenian speeches, unequal "seasonal" hours, hourglasses and Alfred the Great's candle clock. It then tells of Su Song's great astronomical clock and its destruction, the first European mechanical clock, the Dondis' dials and astrarium in Padua, and Galileo's pendulum. It ends with the longitude problem, Harrison's chronometer, wristwatches, quartz and atomic clocks, and asks whether we have mastered time or become its slaves.

  1. Common belief thenE04 · 4:50medium confidence
    "The slaves work from sunup to sunset each day, O'Pharaoh. I don't have enough men."

    TodayThe pyramids were built by paid and fed crews of workers, many of them conscripted for a season, not by slaves. The workers' tombs at Giza (found in 1990) and Mark Lehner's excavation of their town show bread, beer and meat rations and medical care. Water clocks are also an anachronism here: the oldest known clepsydra (Karnak, about 1400 BC) is more than a thousand years later than the Giza pyramids. To be fair, the scene's workers are paid in wheat and garlic, which is closer to the truth than its "slaves".

    Why it was believedHerodotus, mixing it up with the Hebrew slavery of Exodus, and Hollywood epics made pyramid slaves the default image. The Giza finds were only starting to reach the public in 1994.

  2. Common belief thenMinor slipE04 · 8:19medium confidence
    "Then we'll all give you an hourglass with sand."

    TodayThere is no reliable evidence of sandglasses in Greek or Roman times, or in Charlemagne's day. The first sure depiction is Lorenzetti's fresco in Siena (1338), and sandglasses appear in ships' records in the 14th century. A 12-hour hourglass for Charlemagne is a legend.

    Why it was believedOlder clock histories repeated unsourced stories of ancient and Carolingian hourglasses.

  3. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE04 · 8:43medium confidence
    "The Byzantine Empire decided to honor the Emperor and present him with a magnificent gift."

    TodayThe famous clock given to Charlemagne came from the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid in 807. It was a brass water clock that dropped bronze balls onto a cymbal and had mechanical horsemen, not a Byzantine oil clock. Oil-lamp clocks (glass reservoirs marked with hours) date from about the 17th–18th century.

  4. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE04 · 11:33medium confidence
    "Just a few years later, unfortunately, the Song Dynasty was overthrown and replaced,"

    TodaySu Song's clock tower was finished in 1088–1094. In 1127, more than 30 years later, the Jurchen Jin took Kaifeng, ending only the Northern Song; the Southern Song lasted until 1279. The Jin moved the clock to their capital at Yanjing (Beijing), but they could not get it working, and it decayed. It was not destroyed on an emperor's order to drive out evil spirits; that scene is invented.

  5. Common belief thenE04 · 12:31high confidence
    "It's in France that the very first mechanical clock was to be invented."

    Today"Brother Gilbert" is Gerbert of Aurillac, a Benedictine who became Pope Sylvester II (died 1003). He did not invent the mechanical clock. The "horologium" he set up at Magdeburg was probably a sundial or a star-sighting instrument. The weight-driven clock with a verge-and-foliot escapement appeared somewhere in Europe around 1270–1300, and its inventor is unknown. The device in the scene, a weight, a drum, cogwheels and a striker with no escapement, would not keep time. Su Song's clock, shown just before, already had an escapement in 1088.

    Why it was believedThe Gerbert story appears in many older clock histories and French school books, though historians of horology (Lynn White, Needham, Landes's Revolution in Time, 1983) had dismissed it.

  6. Wrong in its dayMinor slipE04 · 21:57medium confidence
    "Came Galileo's pendulum clock and his telescope."

    TodayGalileo designed a pendulum clock in 1641, but it was never finished in his lifetime. Christiaan Huygens built the first working pendulum clock in 1656–57. Pendulum clocks did not work on a rolling ship. Galileo's Jupiter-moons method (proposed 1612) worked on land but was impractical at sea, as the scene jokes.
