Episode 1

And Earth Was Created

12 issues

The episode runs from the formation of Earth and the first cells, through early sea life, amphibians and a parade of dinosaurs, to the rise of mammals and primates. It then follows a line of apes and early humans (Proconsul, Ramapithecus, the australopithecines, Homo habilis) to Homo erectus mastering fire about 700,000 years ago.

  1. OutdatedE01 · 4:00medium confidence
    "With the Cambrian period came various new forms of life, medusas, phallobites, ostracoderm, the very first fish."

    Today"Phallobites" is a speech-recognition error for "trilobites". In 1978 armoured jawless ostracoderms from the Ordovician were the oldest well-known fish. Since 1999, fossils from Chengjiang in China (Myllokunmingia, Haikouichthys; Shu et al., Nature 1999) have pushed the first fish-like vertebrates back to the early Cambrian, about 518 million years ago. Those animals were soft-bodied and were not ostracoderms.

  2. Wrong in its dayE01 · 6:01high confidence
    "The secondary period saw the triumph of the dinosaurs, whose descendants include crocodiles, as well as birds."

    TodayBirds are descendants of dinosaurs. Crocodiles are not: they are cousins of the dinosaurs on a separate archosaur branch, and that branch is as old as the dinosaurs. This was established long before 1978.

  3. OutdatedE01 · 7:04high confidence
    "And here's the all-round champ, the largest beast ever to have walked the Earth. Brachiosaurus, 24 meters long, 12 high."

    TodaySeveral titanosaurs found later were bigger than Brachiosaurus: Argentinosaurus (1993), Dreadnoughtus (2014), Patagotitan (2017). The famous mounted Berlin skeleton was moved to its own genus, Giraffatitan (Taylor 2009). Newer mass estimates for brachiosaurs also tend to be below 50 tonnes, often around 30–45 t.

  4. Common belief thenE01 · 7:34medium confidence
    "The fabled dragon, Pteranodon, the largest flying reptile with a wingspan of 8 meters."

    TodayThe largest known flying reptiles are azhdarchids such as Quetzalcoatlus, with wingspans of about 10–11 m. Pteranodon's wingspan is now put at about 6–7 m at most.

    Why it was believedFor decades Pteranodon was the textbook "biggest flier". Quetzalcoatlus was announced to specialists only in 1975 (Lawson, Science) and took years to reach popular books.

  5. OutdatedE01 · 7:48high confidence
    "The duck model, Anatosaurus."

    TodayAnatosaurus is no longer a valid name. Its species were moved to Edmontosaurus (E. annectens) and other genera in later revisions (Brett-Surman 1979–1990). The duck-billed hadrosaur shown is real; only the name is out of date.

  6. OutdatedE01 · 8:15high confidence
    "The King Tyrannosaurus, 15 by 6 meters, 8 tons"

    Today"15 by 6 meters" reflects the old upright, tail-dragging "kangaroo" pose. T. rex is now reconstructed with a horizontal back and tail, about 12–13 m long (the largest specimen, "Sue", found in 1990, is about 12.3 m) and about 4 m tall at the hips. The 8-tonne weight is still about right.

  7. OutdatedE01 · 8:53medium confidence
    "Unlike the mastodons, these rodents could adapt to climatic conditions and natural upheavals. The old of the secondary period gradually faded away."

    TodayThe episode suggests the dinosaurs faded out gradually because they could not cope with climate change. The end of the non-bird dinosaurs is now tied mainly to a sudden asteroid impact 66 million years ago. This was proposed by Alvarez et al. in 1980, the Chicxulub crater was identified in 1991, and a broad consensus was reached by 2010 (Schulte et al., Science). Deccan volcanism may also have played a part. (Two more notes: "mastodons" is probably a mistranslation of French mastodontes, meaning "giant beasts", since real mastodons lived much later. The early mammals were not rodents.)

  8. OutdatedE01 · 11:29medium confidence
    "When food became scarce, they had to leave the forest for the first time and set out into the open country. Easier from an upright position. As he straightened up, Proconsul's pelvis became stronger"

    TodayThis is the "savanna hypothesis", in which apes became upright by moving from forest into open grassland, with Proconsul on the direct line. Proconsul is now seen as an early four-legged tree-dwelling ape, not a forerunner of upright walking. Later fossils of the earliest hominins (Orrorin 2001, Sahelanthropus 2002, Ardipithecus fully published 2009) show that upright walking began in wooded settings, not open savanna.

  9. Wrong in its dayE01 · 13:13medium confidence
    "Thousands of years went by, and the great anthropomorphic apes evolved."

    TodayThe step from Proconsul (about 20 million years ago) to the australopithecines (about 4–2 million years ago) took millions of years, not thousands. The error may come from the translation.

  10. OutdatedE01 · 13:18high confidence
    "Ramapithecus, 1 meter 30."

    TodayIn 1978 Ramapithecus was widely presented as the earliest human ancestor. A more complete face of the related Sivapithecus, found in 1980 and published by Pilbeam in 1982, together with molecular dating, showed that it was an ancestor or relative of orangutans. It has since been merged into Sivapithecus.

  11. Common belief thenE01 · 16:53medium confidence
    "Obustus was hunted to extinction. Homo habilis now reigned alone."

    Today"Obustus" is a mishearing of "Robustus". No evidence shows that Homo hunted Paranthropus robustus to extinction. Paranthropus lived alongside Homo (including H. erectus) until about 1.2 million years ago, so H. habilis never "reigned alone".

    Why it was believedThe "killer ape" picture of early humans was everywhere in popular books (Raymond Dart; Robert Ardrey's African Genesis, 1961), even though specialists treated it only as speculation.

  12. OutdatedE01 · 17:34medium confidence
    "700,000 years before our time, Pithecanthropus, or Ape Man, more correctly called Homo erectus"

    TodayHomo erectus appeared much earlier, about 1.9–2 million years ago in Africa (for example the Drimolen skull DNH 134, published in 2020). It had reached Georgia (Dmanisi, excavated from 1991) and Java well over a million years ago. The date of 700,000 years suits only late Asian populations such as Peking Man.

Simplified but fine (10)
  • Earth's age as "nearly 5 billion years" (it is 4.54 billion) and "more than a billion years" before the first cell: fine as round numbers. Note on scale: the dub's "billion" is the short-scale billion (10^9), since it equates 4 billion years with "40 million centuries". So these figures are not long-scale (10^12) errors.
  • Wegener's continental drift: one landmass (Pangaea) that later broke apart. Basically right.
  • Stegosaurus at 8 m and about 6 t with a tiny brain; dinosaurs ruling for more than 100 million years: fine.
  • "Brontosaurus": the name was out of favour in 1978 but has been accepted again as a valid genus since 2015 (Tschopp et al.).
  • Diplodocus called "the tallest" is probably a translation slip for "the longest". At 30 m its length is a generous estimate.
  • Elasmosaurus and the Loch Ness monster are offered only as a question, not as a claim.
  • The chain from Aegyptopithecus and Pliopithecus to Proconsul and the australopithecines is shown as a neat single line. It is really a branching bush, but this is acceptable for children.
  • Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania (misheard as "Old Duvet"), an australopithecine brain size of about 450 cm³, and a robustus diet based mostly on plants: fine.
  • Ice-age sea levels about 100 m lower than today (about 120 m at the peak), with land bridges: fine.
  • Homo erectus using fire about 700,000 years ago fits current evidence (Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, about 790,000 years ago).
Episode 2

Neanderthal Man

4 issues

The episode follows a Neanderthal-era tribe as it makes stone tools (the two-sided hand axe and the cleaver), learns to cook meat over fire, quarrels over hunting grounds with a neighbouring tribe, and marks the days with a notched lunar "calendar". As the ice age returns, the tribe moves south and buries its elder, Maestro. The narrator closes by saying Neanderthals vanished "without a trace" 25,000 years ago.

  1. Wrong in its dayE02 · 5:15medium confidence
    "The bifacial or two-sided cutting tool, useful for a variety of tasks. The hand axe, which would become the all-purpose tool."

    TodayThe episode shows the hand axe being invented in the Neanderthal era. Two-sided (Acheulean) hand axes were in fact made by Homo erectus more than a million years earlier: about 1.5 million years ago at Olduvai, as was already known in 1978, and now about 1.76 million years ago at Kokiselei in Kenya (2011). Neanderthals mostly made Mousterian flake tools, though they also made some hand axes.

  2. OutdatedE02 · 25:26high confidence
    "25,000 years before our time, Neanderthal man ended his 100,000 years as master of Europe by disappearing without a trace."

    TodayNeanderthals did not vanish without a trace. Most people living today whose ancestry is from outside Africa carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA, which shows the two groups interbred (Green et al., Science 2010, Neanderthal genome).

  3. OutdatedE02 · 25:26high confidence
    "25,000 years before our time, Neanderthal man ended his 100,000 years as master of Europe"

    TodayImproved radiocarbon dating puts the disappearance of Neanderthals at about 40,000 years ago (Higham et al., Nature 2014). Even 1970s handbooks usually gave about 35,000 years. The Neanderthal line was also in Europe far longer than 100,000 years: the Sima de los Huesos fossils from about 430,000 years ago are early Neanderthals (Meyer et al., Nature 2016).

  4. OutdatedE02 · 25:54medium confidence
    "Was he killed by the intense cold, genetic mutation? To this day, these questions remain unanswered."

    TodayThere is still no single agreed cause. Current research points to a mix of factors: very small, inbred populations (shown by genomics, e.g. Prüfer et al. 2014), competition with incoming Homo sapiens, and absorption into sapiens groups through interbreeding. Sudden climate swings may also have played a part. "Unanswered" is still partly true, but the answer is now framed very differently.

Simplified but fine (5)
  • Pithecanthropus gradually giving way to later forms ("Sinanthropus", Swanscombe, Montmaurin, Neanderthal, Solo and Rhodesian Man) is a simplified, ladder-like family tree. "Florenscomb" and "Montmoren" are mishearings of Swanscombe and Montmaurin.
  • The Neanderthal ancestors who invented cooking with fire: fire use is much older, but cooking by early humans is plausible.
  • Neanderthals burying their dead fits current evidence, for example Shanidar Z (2020).
  • Few people lived to old age, and violent or accidental death was common: fine.
  • Neanderthals coping with ice-age cold and moving with the herds: fine.
Episode 3

Cro-Magnon Man

2 issues

The episode introduces Cro-Magnon (modern humans) in Europe, who live alongside Neanderthals for a time and then replace them. It shows tool trading, mammoth hunting, using every part of the animal, making paint for cave art, two tribes joining together, an initiation, and a calendar. It ends about 10,000 years ago, with the big game gone and a world population of about 10 million.

  1. OutdatedE03 · 1:31high confidence
    "When spatial ice receded over 35,000 years ago, there appeared for the first time Cro-Magnon, or Modern Man."

    Today"Spatial ice" is a mishearing of "glacial ice". Homo sapiens first appeared in Africa about 300,000 years ago (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, re-dated by Hublin et al., Nature 2017). Modern humans reached Europe by about 45,000–47,000 years ago (Bacho Kiro, Bulgaria, 2020), perhaps even 54,000 (Grotte Mandrin, France, 2022). Also, the ice was not retreating 35,000 years ago: the coldest part of the last ice age was still to come, peaking about 20,000 years ago.

  2. OutdatedE03 · 1:38high confidence
    "Tances is slim that he descended from Neanderthal Man since the two lived side-by-side for 5,000 years. Cousin or conqueror, Cro-Magnon Man did win out in the end."

    Today"Tances" is a mishearing of "Chances". It is still true that modern humans did not evolve from Neanderthals. But the two groups interbred, so present-day Europeans do partly descend from Neanderthals, at about 1–2% of their DNA (Green et al. 2010). Some early European H. sapiens had a Neanderthal ancestor only a few generations back (Oase 1, Fu et al. 2015). The "cousin or conqueror" choice leaves out this third option. Current estimates of the overlap in Western Europe are shorter than 5,000 years, roughly 1,400–5,000 years (Higham et al. 2014; Djakovic et al. 2022).

Simplified but fine (5)
  • Cro-Magnon groups working bone, horn and hide, and using every part of a kill: fine.
  • Making paints from ground minerals and burnt bone for faces, cave walls and hides: fine. Real pigments were mostly ochre, charcoal and manganese.
  • Hunting mammoth and large herds, and big game becoming scarce as the ice age ended: fine. The causes of the megafauna extinctions (climate, human hunting) are still debated.
  • A calendar marked by notches: the idea that some Upper Palaeolithic bones record the moon's phases (Marshack) is still debated, but it is fair to show.
  • A world population of about 10 million around 10,000 years ago is within modern estimates (roughly 1–10 million).
Episode 4

The Fertile Valleys

10 issues

The episode covers the start of farming and the Neolithic, megaliths at Carnac, early towns, and Mohenjo-daro on the Indus. It shows a Mesopotamian flood leading to shared canal-building and the first writing, then pyramid-building and Ramses II in Egypt. It ends with the Exodus and Old Testament episodes (Jericho, Samson, David and Goliath, Solomon) and the idea of one God.

  1. Wrong in its dayE04 · 5:33medium confidence
    "5,000 years before Christ, Mesopotamia, where major cities flourished, Jarmo in Iraq, Jericho in Palestine, and in Turkey, Shatal Hayyuk. These highly evolved peoples already knew how to alloy metals."

    Today"Shatal Hayyuk" is a mishearing of Çatalhöyük. Jericho and Çatalhöyük are not in Mesopotamia. All three were farming villages or towns, not "major cities", and all are older than 5000 BC: Jericho goes back to about 9000 BC, and Jarmo and Çatalhöyük to about 7000 BC. True cities came with Uruk, around 3500 BC. These sites worked native copper, but bronze-making (alloying) came later.

  2. Wrong in its dayE04 · 7:19medium confidence
    "Roadways are completely paved."

    TodayThe streets of Mohenjo-daro were mostly beaten earth. Its famous feature was covered brick drains running beside them. The city's grid layout, brick houses and wheeled carts are correctly described.

  3. Wrong in its dayE04 · 13:25high confidence
    "along the river Nile, 2,700 years before our time" / "Thirteen hundred years before our time ruled Ramses II"

    TodayThese should be 2700 BC (the pyramid age) and about 1279–1213 BC (Ramses II). "Before our time" (meaning before today) is almost certainly a dub slip for "before our era" (BC). As spoken, it would put the pyramids about 700 BC.

  4. Wrong in its dayE04 · 16:02medium confidence
    "A thousand blocks of stone, some weighing two tons."

    TodayThe Great Pyramid alone has about 2.3 million blocks averaging about 2.5 tonnes, and some granite beams weigh 50–80 tonnes. "A thousand" is probably a translation slip for "thousands".

  5. OutdatedE04 · 17:09high confidence
    "A daily prison for more than a hundred thousand men."

    TodayThe figure of 100,000 workers comes from Herodotus. Excavations of the Giza workers' town and cemeteries (Lehner and Hawass, from the 1990s) and the Wadi al-Jarf papyri (found in 2013, including the diary of the official Merer) show a workforce of perhaps 20,000–30,000. Many were rotating teams of labourers who were housed, fed and given medical care. They were not slaves or prisoners.

  6. Wrong in its dayE04 · 21:01high confidence
    "In later centuries came invasions by the Hyksos, war with the Hittites"

    TodayThe Hyksos ruled in about 1650–1550 BC, before Ramses II, not after him. The great war with the Hittites (the Battle of Kadesh, 1274 BC) happened during Ramses II's own reign.

  7. Wrong in its dayE04 · 21:09high confidence
    "Ten centuries later, in A.D. 30, Cleopatra would choose suicide over surrender to Augustus"

    TodayCleopatra died in 30 BC, not AD 30. This is probably a dub slip.

  8. OutdatedE04 · 22:04medium confidence
    "Pharaoh had no choice but to let the Israelites go, for they had a rendezvous with destiny."

    TodayHere the Exodus is told as history and placed under Ramses II. In 1978 many scholars in the Albright tradition still thought a historical Exodus was likely. Decades of fieldwork in Sinai and Canaan have found no trace of it, and most historians now treat it as a founding tradition, not an event that can be documented (for example Finkelstein and Silberman, The Bible Unearthed, 2001).

  9. Common belief thenE04 · 22:40high confidence
    "Jericho Seven times the walls will tumble down"

    TodayJericho had no walled town for Joshua to conquer in the 13th century BC. Its Bronze Age walls were destroyed around 1550 BC, and the site was then almost empty.

    Why it was believedIn the 1930s John Garstang claimed to have found Joshua's collapsed walls, and Bible-history books kept repeating this. Kathleen Kenyon's excavations (1952–58) had already disproved it for specialists.

  10. Common belief thenE04 · 24:39high confidence
    "For the first time, men heard these words, Thou shalt not kill, thou shalt not covet. And men heard for the first time about one God."

    TodayLaws against killing are much older: the Code of Ur-Nammu (about 2100 BC) and Hammurabi's laws (about 1750 BC) come centuries earlier. The pharaoh Akhenaten's worship of a single god, the Aten (about 1350 BC), also comes before any likely date for Moses. Many scholars think strict Israelite monotheism took shape only in the 7th–6th centuries BC.

    Why it was believedWestern school and Sunday-school tradition taught the Ten Commandments and Mosaic monotheism as humanity's moral "first".

Simplified but fine (7)
  • Farming started after the ice age, when the big game was gone, and the Mesolithic gave way to the Neolithic (polished stone): fine.
  • The Carnac megaliths in Brittany belong to prehistory: fine (about 4500–3300 BC).
  • Shared canal and dike work along Mesopotamian rivers led to organised society: a classic and reasonable simplification.
  • Writing began with pictographs on clay tablets: right. In reality the first texts were bookkeeping, not stories.
  • A local river flood remembered as the "Great Deluge" is a reasonable hypothesis (flood layers at Ur, Shuruppak and elsewhere).
  • Ramses II as the great builder-pharaoh, tax collectors taking the harvest, and Shelley's "Look upon my works" line (Ozymandias, a poem about Ramses II): fine.
  • Cleopatra's death ending about 30 centuries of pharaonic history: fine.
Episode 5

The First Empires

12 issues

The episode moves through the 2nd and 1st millennia BC: the Indo-European migrations, Hammurabi's laws, Abraham, the Achaeans, Ramses II at Kadesh, the Dorians, the Phoenician alphabet, Assyria and the fall of Nineveh. It then follows Persia from Zarathustra and Cyrus the Great to Darius I at Persepolis and the Persian Wars, and ends with Alexander the Great's conquest of Persia and his death in Babylon.

  1. OutdatedE05 · 1:52medium confidence
    "the Indo-Europeans for lack of a better idea of where they came from."

    TodayAncient-DNA studies have largely settled the question for Europe. Indo-European languages were most likely carried by Yamnaya-related herders from the Pontic–Caspian steppe from about 3000 BC (Haak et al. and Allentoft et al., Nature 2015). Lazaridis et al. (Science 2022) also point to a possible earlier homeland south of the Caucasus. In 1978 the homeland question was still wide open.

  2. Common belief thenE05 · 2:14high confidence
    "the sun god Seamus handed down the civilized world's first legal code to Hammurabi"

    Today"Seamus" is a mishearing of Shamash. Earlier law codes exist: the Code of Ur-Nammu (about 2100 BC), the Laws of Lipit-Ishtar (about 1930 BC) and the Laws of Eshnunna. Hammurabi's is the best-preserved and most famous, not the first.

    Why it was believedHammurabi's stele was found in 1901 and became the textbook "first law code". The Ur-Nammu tablets were published only in 1952, and popular books took decades to catch up.

  3. OutdatedE05 · 3:03medium confidence
    "Abraham. The Israelite left Ur and headed towards Canaan."

    Today"Frowned by Abraham" just before is a mishearing of "drowned. Abraham…". Abraham is a figure of religious tradition with no historical or archaeological evidence. After Thomas Thompson (1974) and John Van Seters (1975), and the archaeology that followed, most historians treat the patriarch stories as later legends, not history. The episode presents the journey as a dated historical event.

  4. OutdatedE05 · 3:49medium confidence
    "In 100 BC, the Dorians invaded, armed with invincible iron."

    Today"100 BC" is a mishearing or slip for about 1100 BC. A "Dorian invasion" bringing iron weapons is no longer accepted. The collapse of Mycenaean Greece around 1200–1100 BC is now explained by several causes (drought, earthquakes, internal unrest, trade breakdown, the "Sea Peoples"), and iron spread gradually through trade. There is little archaeological sign of a Dorian invasion (Chadwick 1976 and later work; Cline, 1177 B.C., 2014).

  5. Wrong in its dayE05 · 5:03high confidence
    "612 bc the medes and the babylonians are now allies led by nebuchadnezzar daily siege to the city of minata"

    TodayIn 612 BC the Babylonians who besieged Nineveh ("minata") were led by King Nabopolassar, with the Median king Cyaxares. His son Nebuchadnezzar II became king only in 605 BC.

  6. Wrong in its dayE05 · 6:56medium confidence
    "Young Persian Prince Cyrus was educated at Zarathustra's school."

    TodayThere is no evidence for this; it is an invention. Zoroaster's dates are very uncertain, and many specialists (already Mary Boyce in 1975) place him centuries before Cyrus, perhaps 1500–1000 BC.

  7. Wrong in its dayE05 · 6:56medium confidence
    "when he decided to take the throne from his dull-witted cousin as he ages the need"

    Today"As he ages the need" is a mishearing of Astyages. Astyages, the Median king whom Cyrus overthrew in about 550 BC, was his grandfather (according to Herodotus), not his cousin.

  8. OutdatedE05 · 9:06medium confidence
    "For the first time in history, conquered cities were not looted, their inhabitants left unharmed, and a conqueror showed clemency."

    TodaySince Amélie Kuhrt's analysis of the Cyrus Cylinder (1983), historians read it as standard Mesopotamian royal propaganda in an old style, not a new humanitarian charter. The Nabonidus Chronicle also records Cyrus massacring people at Opis. Cyrus was relatively tolerant, but he was not the "first" merciful conqueror.

  9. Wrong in its dayE05 · 9:17high confidence
    "It was the birth of the world's first empire."

    TodayEarlier empires include Sargon of Akkad's (about 2300 BC), New Kingdom Egypt and Assyria, which this same episode shows. Cyrus' Persia was the largest empire up to its time, not the first.

  10. Common belief thenE05 · 9:42medium confidence
    "Smiridus, the magus, an imposter, usurped the Persian throne. And seven of the royal princes decided to eliminate him. This horse, Winnied first, would have to carry out the task."

    Today"Smiridus" is Smerdis/Gaumata, and "Winnied first" means "whinnied first". The story of Darius winning the throne because his horse neighed first is a legend told by Herodotus. Many historians also suspect that Darius made up the "impostor" story in his Behistun inscription to justify seizing the throne from the real Bardiya.

    Why it was believedPopular histories of Persia retold Herodotus' colourful stories and the Behistun account at face value.

  11. Wrong in its dayE05 · 21:34medium confidence
    "There were great battles, Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis. No one was winning, so a truce was called."

    TodayThe Greeks clearly defeated the Persian invasions at Marathon (490 BC), Salamis (480) and Plataea/Mycale (479). Fighting went on for decades before the peace of about 449 BC (the Peace of Callias), but "no one was winning" misstates the outcome.

  12. Wrong in its dayE05 · —:—medium confidence
    "from Lebanon, teak from India, gold from Sardis, lapis lazuli from Sagdiana, … At Persopolis, Darius the Great built himself the Palace of Marvel"

    TodayThis list of materials and craftsmen comes from Darius' foundation charter for his palace at Susa (inscription DSf, found in 1929), not Persepolis. A few items are also garbled: Egypt supplied silver and ebony, and the stone columns came from Elam ("Korea" is a mishearing).

Simplified but fine (8)
  • Hammurabi's laws on debt service for three years, careless wives thrown into the river, the river ordeal and drowning for adultery, and the three classes of people: broadly accurate paraphrases (§§117, 129, 132, 143).
  • The Achaeans of Mycenae taking over Minoan Crete, and Ramses II presenting the near-defeat at Kadesh as a victory: fine.
  • Phoenician cities (Tyre, Sidon, Byblos) spreading an alphabet that, through Greek and Latin, became ours: fine.
  • Assyrian brutality, the fall of Nineveh in 612 BC, and Nebuchadnezzar making Babylon the great city of its age: fine.
  • "Nebuchadnezzar was succeeded by Nabonidus" skips three short reigns (Amel-Marduk, Neriglissar, Labashi-Marduk).
  • Cyrus letting the Jews return and rebuild their Temple, and Isaiah calling him "anointed": fine.
  • Babylon sending 500 boys as eunuchs (Herodotus) and "remember the Athenians" (a servant reminding Darius; "Jesus" in the transcript is a mishearing of "Darius"): fine as retellings of Herodotus.
  • Alexander's dates and battles (Granicus 334 BC, misheard as "4 BC"; Issus 333; Gaugamela 331) and his death in Babylon, possibly from malaria: fine. He died at 32, just short of 33.
Episode 6

The Age of Pericles

6 issues

The episode starts in Minoan Crete, shown as a rich and peaceful society, then moves to 5th-century BC Athens under Pericles: democracy, theatre (Sophocles' Antigone), the Delphic oracle, slavery, the rebuilding of the Acropolis and Spartan education. It ends with a long list of Athenian thinkers, the Peloponnesian War, the death of Socrates, Aristotle tutoring Alexander, and the claim that Greek thought then "slept" for 2,000 years.

  1. Wrong in its dayE06 · 2:39high confidence
    "But wasn't it McAdam who invented tar for road paving 3,000 years later?"

    TodayJohn Loudon McAdam's "macadam" roads (1820s) used compacted layers of crushed stone without tar. Tar-bound "tarmac" was patented by Edgar Purnell Hooley in 1902.

  2. OutdatedE06 · 4:50high confidence
    "but no bloodthirsty gods no conquering army no war just happiness prosperity and pleasure"

    TodayThe picture of peaceful Minoans goes back to Arthur Evans. Evidence found from 1979 onward has overturned it: a probable human sacrifice at the Anemospilia temple (Sakellarakis, 1979), children's bones with cut marks at Knossos (Warren, 1980), and a steadily growing record of weapons, warrior graves and fortifications (e.g. Molloy 2012). Minoan Crete was not free of war or violent ritual.

  3. Wrong in its dayE06 · 5:40high confidence
    "500 years before our time, Athens was a young democracy."

    TodayThis should be about 500 BC (Cleisthenes' reforms, 508/507 BC). "Before our time" is a recurring dub slip for "before our era". As spoken, it means around AD 1500.

  4. OutdatedE06 · 5:49medium confidence
    "the great king Darius I was building magnificent palaces in Persopolis, while somewhere in the Far East lived Buddha and Confucius."

Episode 9

The Carolingians

7 issues

The episode covers the barbarian invasions and the fall of the Western Roman Empire, then Clovis and the Franks (baptism, Salic law fines, the vase of Soissons) and Charles Martel at Poitiers. It shows serfdom and military service under Charlemagne, his envoys (missi dominici), the stirrup, his push for schooling and his reforms of writing, measures and money. It ends with the break-up of the empire after the Treaty of Verdun and the Viking raid on Nantes in 843.

  1. OutdatedE09 · 6:11medium confidence
    "496, meet Clovis, the only Catholic king in the West"

    TodayThe traditional date of 496 for Clovis' baptism comes from a reading of Gregory of Tours. Many historians now prefer about 508, or at least a date after 500, based on letters by Avitus of Vienne and others (e.g. Danuta Shanzer 1998; Ian Wood). The date is still debated.

  2. Common belief thenE09 · 8:57medium confidence
    "Quaché, the Arabs came face to face with Charles Martel. Martel had just saved Europe from Islam."

    Today"Quaché" is a mishearing of "In 732". Many historians now see Poitiers/Tours (732 or 733) as defeating a large raid, not as the turning point that "saved Europe". Arab expansion was also slowed by internal conflict (including the Berber revolt of 740), and Arab forces held Narbonne until 759.

    Why it was believedGibbon's famous passage and French national history made Poitiers a defining battle of civilisation. Specialists began questioning this from the mid-20th century.

  3. Common belief thenE09 · 19:33medium confidence
    "The stirrup, my latest invention. … At last, equipped with stirrups, your cavalry will be invincible."

    TodayThe stirrup was invented in Asia (paired stirrups in China by the 4th century AD) and reached Europe through steppe peoples such as the Avars. Lynn White Jr.'s thesis (1962) that stirrups gave the Franks unstoppable shock cavalry, and with it feudalism, has been widely rejected (Bachrach 1970 and later). Stirrups are rare in Carolingian finds, and Frankish armies did not depend on them.

    Why it was believedWhite's Medieval Technology and Social Change (1962) was hugely influential, and its "great stirrup controversy" took years to reach general books.

  4. Common belief thenE09 · 21:07high confidence
    "As of this very moment, it will be compulsory for all clerks and for all children to go to school and learn to read."

    TodayCharlemagne's Admonitio generalis (789) ordered monasteries and cathedrals to keep schools for boys, mainly to train clergy. There was no compulsory schooling for all children. That came in France only with the Ferry laws of 1881–82.

    Why it was believedThe French school myth of Charlemagne as the "inventor of school" (as in the song "Sacré Charlemagne") was a staple of French schoolbooks.

  5. Wrong in its dayE09 · 23:19high confidence
    "From then on, writing characters would be those we know today. numerals would be Arabic."

    TodayThe writing part is roughly right: Carolingian minuscule is the ancestor of our lowercase letters. The numerals part is wrong. Charlemagne's world used Roman numerals. Hindu-Arabic numerals first appear in Latin Europe in the late 10th century (the Codex Vigilanus, 976, and Gerbert of Aurillac) and spread mainly after Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202).

  6. Wrong in its dayE09 · 23:28high confidence
    "One pound would equal 20 sous, one sous, 12 deniers. In France, this was the rule till 1789, and the same system exists in England today."

    TodayThe Carolingian system of £1 = 20 shillings and 1 shilling = 12 pence is correctly described. But Britain switched to decimal currency on 15 February 1971, so it no longer "existed in England today" even when the episode was made in 1978. France replaced the livre with the decimal franc in 1795, a few years after 1789.

  7. OutdatedE09 · 24:37medium confidence
    "Charlemagne died in the year 814 at the age of 72."

    Today814 is correct. His birth is now usually placed in 747 or 748, not 742 (Werner 1973; Matthias Becher 1992), which makes him about 66 at death. Biographies around 1978 still usually gave 742.

  • Salic law's scale of fines, with killing a Frank (200 solidi) costing more than killing a Roman (100): fine.
  • The vase of Soissons is an anecdote from Gregory of Tours, told as such. Clovis killing off his royal relatives: fine.
  • Serfs, lords taking a share of the harvest, military service tied to landholding, and the missi dominici inspecting justice: fine as simplifications.
  • The elephant Abul-Abbas, a gift from Harun al-Rashid (arrived 802): fine.
