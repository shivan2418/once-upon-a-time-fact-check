# Once Upon a Time... Life — s01e01: The Cell Planet

The episode tells the story of how the first cells formed in the "primal soup" and shows the main parts of a cell as a factory: the membrane, mitochondria that make energy, ribosome "assembly lines," lysosomes, and a nucleus whose chromosomes send RNA messengers with production orders. A skin injury sets off an urgent order for collagen, and a careless worker nearly sends out the master DNA instead of an RNA copy.

## Outdated (since 1987)

- **Quote:** "Take the brain with its three layers. The first layer we inherited from the reptiles. The second, the Paleocortex, from the mammals. And the third, the Neocortex"
  **Correct today:** This is Paul MacLean's "triune brain" idea. Brain scientists have rejected it. The brain did not grow by adding new layers on top of old ones. Reptiles and birds have structures that correspond to parts of our cortex, and all brain parts evolved together (comparative neuroanatomy from the 1990s onward, summarized by Cesario et al., 2020).
  **Confidence:** High
  **Severity:** major

- **Quote:** "the human body has 60,000,000 million of them" (later "60,000,000,000", probably a dubbing or Whisper slip)
  **Correct today:** Careful counts give about 30 to 37 trillion human cells (Bianconi 2013; Sender, Fuchs and Milo 2016). That is about half the old 60 trillion estimate, which is exactly what the episode gives for 1987. ("60,000,000 million" is 60 trillion written in a way that cannot be misread whether "billion" means a thousand million or, in older British and French usage, a million million.)
  **Confidence:** High
  **Severity:** minor

- **Quote:** "The centriole, the command post that controls the unity or the division of the cell."
  **Correct today:** Centrioles help organize the machinery that pulls chromosomes apart. They do not decide when a cell divides. That decision is made by cell-cycle control proteins (cyclins and CDKs; Nobel Prize 2001). Some cells divide without any centrioles (plants, and fruit flies engineered without them, Basto 2006).
  **Confidence:** Medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Common belief at the time

- **Quote:** "the liquid in which our cells bathe is almost identical to the seawater from which we originally come."
  **Correct today:** The fluid around our cells is only about one third as salty as seawater (about 0.9% versus 3.5%), and its mix of minerals is also different. This was an old idea that had already been abandoned by 1987.
  **Why it was believed:** Archibald Macallum's 1926 theory that body fluids preserve the saltiness of the ancient ocean was repeated for decades in biology textbooks and popular science as "we carry the sea inside us."
  **Confidence:** Medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1987)

- **Quote:** "Approximately 5,000 million years ago, single-celled, the cell developed."
  **Correct today:** Earth is about 4,540 million years old, so cells cannot be 5,000 million years old. The oldest signs of life are roughly 3,500 to 3,800 million years old. Earth's age had been known since 1956.
  **Confidence:** High
  **Severity:** minor

- **Quote:** "every day 500,000 million of them vanish and are replaced at the rate of 50 million each second."
  **Correct today:** The two numbers contradict each other: 50 million per second adds up to about 4,300 billion per day, not 500 billion. Measurements put turnover at about 330 billion cells a day (Sender and Milo 2021), which is about 4 million per second. The per-second figure is roughly 13 times too high.
  **Confidence:** Medium
  **Severity:** minor

- **Quote:** "And the third, the Neocortex, the most important, developed very rapidly. It only took 500,000 years to form."
  **Correct today:** All mammals have a neocortex, and it is well over 100 million years old. It did not appear in human evolution, and it did not form in 500,000 years.
  **Confidence:** High
  **Severity:** major

## Simplified but fine

- "Mitochondria, which are probably ancient bacteria": correct. This is now confirmed beyond reasonable doubt.
- "Fingers used to be fish fins": correct in broad terms (see fossil fish-to-limb transitions such as Tiktaalik).
- The tailbone ("Six" is a Whisper error for "coccyx") as "what remains of our monkey's tail": the tailbone really is what is left of a tail. The ancestor was an early primate, not a modern monkey.
- Red blood cells "lost our nucleus so we can't reproduce": correct.
- RNA messengers carrying copies of the instructions, with the master DNA staying in the nucleus: correct.
- Proteins making up "half our body substance": roughly true for the body's dry weight, though not if water is counted.
- Cell size of "one hundredth of a millimeter": a typical size.
