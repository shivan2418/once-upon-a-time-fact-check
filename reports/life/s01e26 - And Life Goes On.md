# Once Upon a Time... Life — s01e26: And Life Goes On

Grandpa explains ageing: cells are constantly replaced, but they slow down, and in old age the heart, lungs and defenses weaken. He passes on his genes to his grandchildren. The story then follows a newborn baby learning to use its senses and building its own immune defenses, and ends with a science-fiction space voyage in which human chromosomes carry humanity into the future.

## Outdated (since 1987)

- **Quote:** "nerve cells die in their thousands each day without ever being replaced"
  **Correct today:** Studies in the 1990s found that healthy ageing loses far fewer neurons than once thought (Morrison & Hof, 1997). Brain cells mainly lose connections rather than dying off. Researchers also found evidence that adults can grow some new neurons in a memory area of the brain called the hippocampus (Eriksson et al., 1998), though how much this happens is still debated.
  **Confidence:** High

- **Quote:** "Dose of Alpha Interferon to activate your bodily defenses" (to treat a cold)
  **Correct today:** Interferon nose sprays were tested against colds in the mid-1980s and dropped. They caused nose irritation and bleeding and weren't practical. Interferon is not used to treat colds.
  **Confidence:** Medium (this is set in an imagined future)

## Common belief at the time

- **Quote:** "ten times longer to voyage to Andromeda, our nearest galactic neighbor"
  **Correct today:** The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds (about 160,000–200,000 light-years away) were known to be much closer long before 1987. The Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy, discovered in 1994, is closer still. Andromeda is only the nearest *large* galaxy. Its distance (about 2.5 million light-years) does work out to roughly "ten times" the episode's own galaxy figure.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular astronomy books routinely called Andromeda "our nearest neighbour galaxy," meaning the nearest large spiral, and treated the closer Magellanic Clouds as satellites of the Milky Way rather than neighbours.
  **Confidence:** High

## Wrong (even in 1987)

- **Quote:** "They're here on transfer from the bloodstream of the mother ... seconded to us from the maternal organism" (about the lymphocyte patrol)
  **Correct today:** A baby's borrowed protection is mostly the mother's antibodies, which cross the placenta before birth and come through breast milk. The mother's immune cells (lymphocytes) don't move in and run the baby's defenses. The thymus as the place where the baby's own T-cells are trained is correct.
  **Confidence:** Medium-high

- **Quote:** "even if we could touch the speed of light ... it would take us a quarter of a million years to cross our own galaxy"
  **Correct today:** The Milky Way's main disc is about 100,000 light-years across (some newer estimates go higher), so crossing it at light speed takes about 100,000 years. Astronomers used that figure in 1987 too.
  **Confidence:** High

## Simplified but fine

- "the speed of light, which is 300,000 kilometers per second": correct.
- Skin is constantly shed and replaced from below: correct.
- Leaky heart valves, a slowing heart and weaker breathing in old age: correct in outline.
- Children inherit traits such as nose shape and eye colour through chromosomes: correct.
- Old people's cuts take "ten times longer" to heal: exaggerated, since healing is slower but not ten times slower. It's a grandfather's figure of speech.
- "a hundred years at most" for a human lifespan: said in a science-fiction setting. The verified record (Jeanne Calment, 122) was set in 1997.
