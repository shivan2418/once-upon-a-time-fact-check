# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e13: George Stephenson, Full Steam Ahead!
The episode traces steam power from Hero of Alexandria's useless toy through Denis Papin's pressure cooker, Newcomen's mine engine, Watt's more efficient engine, Cugnot's road carriage and Murdoch's model, to the first locomotives. It then follows George Stephenson and his son Robert from the Killingworth colliery engines to Locomotion, the Liverpool and Manchester route and the 1829 Rainhill trials, and ends on modern 300 km/h trains.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "I would prefer you take it in through the door, sir, and not through the wall."
  **Today:** Cugnot's steam *fardier* (1769–71), built to haul artillery, is real. The story that it ran out of control and knocked down a wall of the Paris Arsenal is not in contemporary records. It first appears decades later, and historians treat it as a legend.
  **Why it was believed:** The "first car, first car crash" anecdote has been repeated in French popular histories and motoring books since the 19th century.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "My opinion, it should be a new design, a boiler with pipes."
  **Today:** The multi-tubular boiler that made Rocket (1829) work was suggested by Henry Booth, treasurer of the Liverpool and Manchester Railway. Marc Seguin had already patented a fire-tube boiler in France in 1827–28. Rocket itself was designed and built mainly by Robert Stephenson at the Newcastle works. George was the railway's engineer, but he was not the lone inventor of the boiler.
  **Why it was believed:** Samuel Smiles's *Lives of the Engineers* (1857–62) made George Stephenson the single "Father of Railways", and popular books followed him. To its credit, the show does give Robert a real role in running the workshop.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "And so it was, children, that that was all that Denis Papin left us, the pressure cooker, but things would change."
  **Today:** Papin's steam digester (1679), the forerunner of the pressure cooker, came *before* his engine work, not after it, and he invented the safety valve for it. In 1690 he described the first steam cylinder with a piston, worked by condensing steam, which is the principle Newcomen's engine later used. He also built a paddle boat (1707). So the pressure cooker was far from all he left, and the French show undersells its own inventor.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "It's not a toy, it's a scale model of the very first locomotive on rails."
  **Today:** William Murdoch's model of about 1784 was a steam *road* carriage, not a rail locomotive. The first locomotive to run on rails was Richard Trevithick's, at Penydarren in 1804. Murdoch was also a Scot from Ayrshire, not an Englishman, though "Fire the Englishman" may be a transcription error.
  **Confidence:** high

## Simplified but fine
- Steam power being "barely two centuries" old is about right if you count from Watt (1760s–1780s). Newcomen's engine dates from 1712.
- Hero's aeolipile (1st century AD) was a curiosity with no practical use. The gap to Papin was about 1,600 years rather than 2,000, and there were minor steam devices in between (Taqi al-Din, Branca, Somerset).
- Newcomen's 1712 atmospheric engine is rightly called the first steam engine used in industry, mainly to pump water from mines. It did waste a great deal of coal.
- Watt's improvements (from 1765 in Glasgow) cut the waste of steam. The "drawer" sending steam to each side of the piston in turn describes the double-acting engine (1782) and its slide valve.
- Watt did discourage Murdoch's steam carriage experiments, and the anecdote of Murdoch testing his model at night is traditional.
- The first public railway, the Surrey Iron Railway, opened in 1803 and was horse-drawn. Correct.
- "Catch Me If You Can" is Trevithick's *Catch Me Who Can* (1808), run on a circular track in London at a shilling a ride. It was too heavy for the rails. The show does not name Trevithick, and young Stephenson's presence there is dramatic licence.
- Stephenson's first locomotive, Blücher (1814), was paid for by Lord Ravensworth's colliery partners at Killingworth. The direct drive from piston rods to the wheels came in his 1815 patent with Dodds.
- Robert Stephenson was sent to school and briefly to Edinburgh University, and Robert Stephenson & Co. (1823) built Locomotion No. 1.
- The Stockton and Darlington (1825) is usually called the first public railway to use steam locomotives. The Middleton Railway had used steam locomotives commercially since 1812, and most S&D traffic, including passengers, was still horse-drawn at first.
- The route dispute is compressed. William James surveyed an early Liverpool and Manchester line and was later replaced. Stephenson's own 1825 survey was rejected by Parliament before he came back as engineer. He did insist on gentle gradients.
- Rainhill, October 1829: a £500 prize, a 10 mph (16 km/h) minimum, and Brandreth's horse-powered treadmill *Cycloped*, which managed about 5 mph (8 km/h). All correct.
- The Liverpool and Manchester opened in September 1830, the year after Rainhill. Correct.
- Rocket did go "over 30 km/h". It averaged about 12 mph (19 km/h) pulling its load and reached about 30 mph (48 km/h) running light.
- The closing prediction: the TGV ran at 300 km/h in 1994. Records have since passed 500 km/h (574.8 km/h in 2007, 603 km/h for a maglev in 2015), but regular service tops out around 350 km/h on wheels and 431 km/h on the Shanghai maglev.
