# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e09: Galileo Galilei
The episode follows Galileo from his childhood in Pisa through the swinging-lamp pendulum, his falling-body and inclined-plane experiments, his professorships at Pisa and Padua, the telescope and his discoveries in the sky, and his trial and abjuration before the Inquisition in 1633. It teaches that experiment and mathematics, not authority, decide how nature works, and that Galileo paid a price for saying the Earth moves.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Yes, but still, it moves."
  **Today:** No record from 1633 has Galileo saying "Eppur si muove" at his abjuration. It would have been a dangerous thing to say in front of the Inquisition. The phrase first appears in print in Giuseppe Baretti's *Italian Library* (1757). A Spanish painting from about the 1640s, found in 1911, carries the words, which suggests the legend circulated soon after his death. The episode frames its opening and closing around the line as if Galileo really said it.
  **Why it was believed:** It is one of the most quoted lines in the history of science and appears in nearly every popular biography.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "Marcantonio, we're going to create a device that will make distant objects appear much closer."
  **Today:** Galileo did not invent the telescope. Spectacle-makers in the Netherlands made the first ones; Hans Lipperhey applied for a patent in October 1608. Galileo heard of the device in 1609, worked out how it worked, and quickly built much better ones (about 8x, then about 20x). His real achievement was turning it on the sky and publishing what he saw (*Sidereus Nuncius*, 1610). The episode never mentions the Dutch and has him present it to the Doge as "this invention".
  **Why it was believed:** Galileo was the first to make the telescope famous, so popular accounts often credit him with inventing it.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "don't forget my young friend only 10 years ago giordano bruno said the earth revolved"
  **Today:** Bruno was burned in Rome in 1600, about ten years earlier, so the date is right. But his condemnation rested mainly on theological charges: denying the Trinity, the divinity of Christ and transubstantiation, plus pantheism and an infinity of inhabited worlds. Copernicanism was a small part of it at most. Historians since Frances Yates (1964) have stressed this. He was not a martyr for heliocentric astronomy.
  **Why it was believed:** Nineteenth-century anticlerical writers made Bruno a martyr of science, and popular books kept that image.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "Yes, they thought the sun and all the planets revolved around the earth and had always done so, and God help anybody who didn't agree."
  **Today:** Before 1616 the Church did not persecute Copernicans. Copernicus dedicated his book (1543) to Pope Paul III, and it was read and taught for 70 years. Jesuit astronomers at the Collegio Romano confirmed Galileo's telescope discoveries and honoured him in 1611. Trouble began only with the 1616 decree, and Galileo's 1633 trial was shaped by politics, his own provocations, and the alleged breach of the 1616 injunction. The show partly concedes this through the Pope's support and the Spanish pressure scene, but its framing is the simple "science versus Church" story.
  **Why it was believed:** The "conflict thesis" of Draper and White (1870s–1890s) was still how most popular books told the story.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "So, according to my observations, the sun does not move around the earth, it's the contrary."
  **Today:** Galileo had strong arguments but no proof. The phases of Venus (1610) ruled out Ptolemy's system, but they fitted Tycho Brahe's system, in which the Sun circles the Earth, just as well. The proof Galileo himself offered in the *Dialogue* was his theory of the tides (mentioned at the trial: "only the seas are influenced by this astounding speed"), and that theory was wrong. Direct evidence that the Earth moves came with Bradley's stellar aberration (1729) and Bessel's stellar parallax (1838). Bellarmine's demand for a real demonstration was not unreasonable for its time.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular history treated Galileo as the man who proved Copernicus right, and the Church as rejecting proof he already had.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "A few years later, Galileo at 14 was admitted to the University of Pisa to begin his medical studies."
  **Today:** Galileo enrolled at Pisa to study medicine in 1581, at 17. Around 14 he was a pupil at the Vallombrosa monastery.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Galileo went on to use his invention in making clocks."
  **Today:** Galileo only designed a pendulum clock, in 1641–42 when he was blind, and dictated the design to his son Vincenzo. It was never finished in his lifetime. Christiaan Huygens built the first working pendulum clock in 1656–57.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The speed of a falling body increases by the square of the time it falls."
  **Today:** It is the *distance* fallen that grows with the square of the time. Speed grows in simple proportion to time. Galileo's inclined-plane law (published 1638) says the distances are as the squares of the times, i.e. 1, 4, 9, 16. The bells scene shows the right idea, since the bells are spaced so they ring at equal times, but the stated law is wrong.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "with a salary of a thousand thorns per month."
  **Today:** In 1609 the Venetian Senate gave Galileo a life appointment at Padua with his salary raised to 1,000 florins *a year*, not a month. "Thorns" is a mishearing of "florins".
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Jupiter clear as day with its two little moons."
  **Today:** Galileo found four moons of Jupiter in January 1610 (Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, which he called the Medicean stars). On the first night he saw three "stars" beside the planet, and by 13 January he had seen all four. "Two" matches none of his records.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "These sunspots, they're not where they were a few days ago. It proves we are moving around the sun."
  **Today:** The steady drift of sunspots across the disc shows that the *Sun rotates* (about once a month). It does not show that the Earth orbits the Sun. Galileo's sunspot letters (1613) argued that the spots were on the Sun, so the heavens were not perfect and unchanging. Only later, in the *Dialogue*, did he argue that the yearly change in the spots' paths favoured Copernicus, and even that was not a proof.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "The worthy head of the Society of Jesus, Cardinal Gaspard Borgia"
  **Today:** Cardinal Gaspar de Borja y Velasco was Spain's ambassador and did protest in the consistory of March 1632 that Urban VIII was soft on the Protestants, which put pressure on the Pope just as the *Dialogue* appeared. But he was not head of the Jesuits. The Jesuit Superior General was Muzio Vitelleschi.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Born in Pisa in 1564: correct. The childhood race and the "spaghetti and chocolate cake" line are invented and anachronistic. Chocolate barely reached Italy around 1600.
- The swinging-lamp story comes from Viviani's biography. The lamp now shown in Pisa cathedral was hung in 1587, after the supposed observation. "The length of the pendulum determines how fast it swings" is right.
- "Look at a circle, or even better, a parabola" is muddled: Galileo thought the pendulum was exactly isochronous on a circular arc, which is only true for small swings. Huygens showed (1673) that a cycloid is the truly isochronous path.
- The pulse-measuring pendulum (pulsilogium) is real; Santorio Santorio published it in 1602, probably from Galileo's idea.
- The hydrostatic balance for measuring density (*La Bilancetta*, 1586) is real.
- Professor of mathematics at Pisa at 25 (1589) and at Padua at 28 (1592): correct. He stayed 18 years at Padua (1592–1610), not 20.
- Dropping a heavy and a light rock together: the Leaning Tower test is a Viviani story Galileo may never have performed, but the idea (equal fall, ignoring air resistance) is right. The episode sensibly drops them into water.
- Timing the inclined plane "with my pendulum": Galileo describes a water clock, and bells or musical beats are a modern reconstruction (Stillman Drake). The idea of measuring equal times is fine.
- Thermometer: Galileo made an air thermoscope around 1593, without a proper scale.
- Proportional compass: the geometric and military compass (1597) was mainly a calculating instrument, also used for surveying distances.
- Santucci's armillary sphere of the Ptolemaic universe (Antonio Santucci, Florence, 1588–93) is real, a nice detail.
- "cardinal bellerman ... the grand inquisitor": Robert Bellarmine was a leading cardinal-inquisitor of the Holy Office, not its head (the Pope presided).
- Demonstration of the telescope to the Doge and Senate from the campanile of St Mark's, August 1609, with ships seen hours early: correct.
- The Moon's phases were understood in antiquity. Galileo's real lunar discovery was its mountains and craters, which showed it was not a perfect sphere.
- Saturn shown as having "satellites", then corrected to rings: fair. Galileo saw Saturn as a triple body, and Huygens identified the ring in 1655–59.
- The "dark glass": Galileo and Castelli mostly projected the Sun's image onto paper. His blindness (1637) came from eye disease, not from looking at the Sun.
- "Marina" is Marina Gamba, his partner (never his wife), the mother of his three children: correct.
- The Bible "uses simple language" argument comes from Galileo's letters to Castelli (1613) and the Grand Duchess Christina (1615).
- The Dominican Niccolò Lorini (misheard "Brother Lorini") and Tommaso Caccini attacked him in 1614–15: correct.
- Federico Cesi (misheard "Prince Chazy") founded the Accademia dei Lincei. Galileo was welcomed by Maffeo Barberini, newly elected as Urban VIII, in 1624.
- The *Dialogue* (1632) was licensed, dedicated to Grand Duke Ferdinando II, and Simplicio seemed to mock the Pope's own argument: correct.
- The disputed 1616 injunction document ("A forgery?") is a real historical controversy, handled fairly.
- Vincenzo Maculano da Firenzuola was the inquisitor (commissary general), and Galileo was held in comfortable rooms: correct.
- Ptolemy's theories were about 1,500 years old, not "a thousand".
- Galileo's "we don't feel the motion" argument is about uniform motion (his ship example), not "constant acceleration". The dub line is garbled.
- Threatened with torture and forced to abjure in June 1633, then house arrest at Arcetri: correct.
- Rehabilitation: John Paul II accepted the commission's findings in 1992. The ban on Copernican books had been lifted in 1822–35.
