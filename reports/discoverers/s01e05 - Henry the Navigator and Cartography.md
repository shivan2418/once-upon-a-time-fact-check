# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e05: Henry the Navigator and Cartography
The episode follows Prince Henry of Portugal from the capture of Ceuta (1415) to his "research centre" at Sagres, where caravels are designed, maps are improved and Gil Eanes finally rounds Cape Bojador (1434). It covers the first Portuguese slave trading, the push down the African coast, and ends with Bartolomeu Dias rounding the Cape of Good Hope (1488) and a quick list of the voyages that followed. The lesson is that organised, patient exploration beat fear and superstition.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "And at Sagres, on the southwestern tip of Portugal, Henry will have a genuine research institute built,"
  **Today:** There was no "School of Sagres" with resident astronomers, cartographers and shipwrights. Henry did have a household at Sagres (Vila do Infante, from the 1440s) and employed a few experts, such as the Majorcan "Mestre Jácome". But no source describes an institute, and most of the early voyages were planned from Lagos and Lisbon. The episode's closing line repeats the idea.
  **Why it was believed:** The idea grew from a 1625 remark by Samuel Purchas and 19th-century nationalist histories. Historians such as Duarte Leite and later Peter Russell ("Prince Henry the Navigator: the rise and fall of a culture hero", 1984) took it apart, but popular books kept it.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "Henry of Portugal is trying to sail around the African continent to find a route to the Orient."
  **Today:** Henry's recorded aims, as given by his chronicler Zurara, were to learn what lay beyond Bojador, open trade (especially Guinea gold), find Christian allies such as Prester John, fight Islam and convert people. Sailing round Africa to India became Portuguese policy only under João II, in the 1480s. The Venetian plot to stop Henry is fiction built on this anachronism.
  **Why it was believed:** Looking back from Vasco da Gama, older histories gave Henry the India plan.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The rudder was my idea."
  **Today:** No one person designed the caravel at Henry's court. It developed from existing Portuguese and Andalusian fishing and coasting boats. The lateen sail came from the Mediterranean and Arab world, and Europe had used the sternpost rudder since about 1200. The early exploration caravels were small two-masted lateen ships (caravela latina). Three-masted and square-rigged versions came later in the century. Lateen rig lets a ship sail closer to the wind, not straight into it.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular histories gave the caravel to the Sagres "school" together with everything else.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Look, here is the well-known Catalan Atlas drawn by your father."
  **Today:** The Catalan Atlas (about 1375) is credited to Abraham Cresques of Majorca, and his son Jehuda Cresques was a cartographer. However, the claim that Jehuda ran Henry's map-making comes from identifying him with the "Mestre Jácome of Majorca" mentioned by Duarte Pacheco Pereira. That is a guess, not a documented fact. Jehuda had in fact converted in 1391 and lived as Jaume Ribes in Barcelona.
  **Why it was believed:** The identification was repeated as fact in 19th- and 20th-century popular accounts of Sagres.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Stars on the horizon."
  **Today:** In the 1430s Portuguese pilots sailed by compass, dead reckoning and coastal landmarks. The first recorded Portuguese latitude reading from the stars with a quadrant is Diogo Gomes's, around 1460. Eanes would not have worked out that he was "on the same latitude as Cape Bojador" from the stars.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular accounts credited Henry's "school" with astronomical navigation from the start.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "I sailed in closer despite the terrible heat and saw the ocean falling into a bottomless pit."
  **Today:** The fears Zurara records for Bojador were real but practical, plus one learned idea. Sailors feared shoals, strong currents and winds that might stop them getting back. Scholars held the old idea that the torrid zone was too hot to live in and would burn men black. Educated Europeans knew the Earth was round, and "falling off the edge" is largely a 19th-century invention (see Jeffrey Burton Russell, *Inventing the Flat Earth*, 1991). Here the pit is a liar's tale, which the episode exposes, so this is mild.
  **Why it was believed:** Washington Irving and others made "medieval flat-earth fears" a standard part of the exploration story.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Anyway, the church hasn't yet decided whether these creatures are human,"
  **Today:** The Church never debated whether Africans had souls. The idea of a "debate about souls" is a myth that partly garbles the 1550 Valladolid debate about Native Americans, which was about how they should be treated, not whether they had souls. What the papacy did was authorise Portuguese enslavement in the bulls *Dum Diversas* (1452) and *Romanus Pontifex* (1455).
  **Why it was believed:** "Did they have souls?" was a common shorthand for colonial racism in popular history.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "Well, Henry did discourage it. He opposed it, but..."
  **Today:** Henry ran the early Portuguese slave trade and profited from it. He held the monopoly on Guinea voyages and took the royal fifth. Zurara describes him on horseback at the first big slave sale at Lagos in 1444, taking his 46 captives. The episode's "he opposed it" has no source.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "All the Jews were chased from Spain."
  **Today:** Spain expelled its Jews in 1492, more than 30 years after Henry died (1460). In Henry's time the relevant events were the 1391 pogroms in Castile, Aragon and Majorca, which forced many conversions, including that of the Cresques family.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "But most valuable, you will have a map with complete details concerning Africa and the Indian Ocean."
  **Today:** Before Dias, no Portuguese map showed southern Africa or the Indian Ocean in detail. Finding them out was the point of his voyage. In 1487 João II sent Pêro da Covilhã overland at the same time to gather information on the Indian Ocean. Henry had been dead 27 years and his captains had only reached Sierra Leone.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "1499, Vasco de Gama sails to Calcutta in India."
  **Today:** Da Gama reached Calicut (Kozhikode), not Calcutta, in May **1498**. He returned to Lisbon in 1499.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Portugal's population of about a million around 1400, João I's victory over Castile (Aljubarrota, 1385, about 30 years before Ceuta) and the taking of Ceuta in 1415 are right. Henry was a younger son, not the heir.
- The "silent barter" of salt and goods for West African gold is described in medieval and Portuguese sources (Cadamosto), although the idea goes back to Herodotus.
- Zarco and Teixeira reached Porto Santo and Madeira in 1418–20, and settlement began about 1420. The Azores were found by 1427 and settled from 1439.
- About 15 failed attempts on Bojador, followed by Gil Eanes's success in 1434, is right. Eanes's first attempt turned back. He brought home plants as proof, the "Saint Mary's roses" (garbled as "Mary, Mary"). "Bohador/Bochador/Bojado" are mishearings of Bojador.
- Baldaia's 1435–36 voyage did take horses to explore inland.
- The Portuguese "policy of secrecy" (Jaime Cortesão) is debated, but the chart-making was real.
- A gimbal-mounted compass in the 1430s is doubtful. Gimbaled ship compasses are well recorded only in the 16th century, and the "little object" is shown only vaguely.
- The first Portuguese slave raids in 1441 and slavery existing in Africa before them are right. In Senegambia a horse did buy several slaves: Cadamosto gives 9–14.
- Arguin (1445–48) is in present-day Mauritania, north of the Senegal River. Senegal and Guinea were reached in the mid-1440s and the Gambia (Cadamosto) in 1455–56.
- Crossing the equator does lose the Pole Star. Solar latitude tables based on Zacuto's Hebrew *Ha-Hibbur ha-Gadol* (translated as the *Almanach Perpetuum*) came into use in the 1480s.
- João II became king in 1481. Dias sailed with two caravels and a supply ship, was blown south by storms, turned north and landed on the far side of the Cape (1488).
- "1892" and "1419, Magellan" are mishearings or dub slips for 1492 and 1519. Cabral reached Brazil in 1500.
