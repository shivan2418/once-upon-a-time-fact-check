# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e20: Henry Ford and the Adventure of the Motorcar
The episode goes from the Sumerian wheel through Leonardo's spring cart, Cugnot, Murdoch, Lenoir, Otto, Benz and Daimler to the first fancy cars. It then follows Henry Ford from the farm and the Edison company to the kitchen-sink engine, the Quadricycle, racing, the Selden patent fight, the Model T, the assembly line and the $5 day. The message is that Ford made the car affordable for ordinary people, and gave us traffic jams too.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "And I propose right now to double the salaries of all our workers."
  **Today:** The $5 day (January 1914) roughly doubled pay, but it was not mainly generosity. Turnover on the new assembly line ran around 370% a year, and higher pay cut it sharply and raised output. Only workers who passed checks by Ford's Sociological Department got the full rate. Economic historians (Meyer 1981, Raff and Summers 1987) describe it as an efficiency wage. The Dodge brothers' lawsuit (decided in 1919) was about Ford holding back dividends to expand, not about wages.
  **Why it was believed:** Ford presented the $5 day as profit-sharing, and the "benevolent Ford" story was standard in popular histories.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "This would be the first internal combustion engine, so-called because regular explosions of the air and fuel mixture supplied the power to push the pistons."
  **Today:** Otto's 1876 engine was not the first internal combustion engine. Lenoir's gas engine (1860) came first and was sold commercially, and the episode itself says so later. Otto's real advance was the four-stroke cycle with compression (the principle was patented by Beau de Rochas in 1862). Also, Otto's engine was fired by a gas flame through a slide valve, not by the "electric spark" shown. Lenoir's engine was the one with a spark.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "It was a Frenchman, René Lenoir,"
  **Today:** He was Jean Joseph Étienne Lenoir, born in Luxembourg (then part of the Netherlands) and later a naturalized Frenchman. Earlier the episode calls him "André Lenoir". Either name may be a dub or transcription error.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The biggest seller in history."
  **Today:** About 15 million Model Ts were built (1908–27). The Volkswagen Beetle passed that total in February 1972, so by 1994 the Model T was no longer the best-selling car ever.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In 1927, the Golden Arrow attained 370 kilometers an hour."
  **Today:** Segrave's Golden Arrow set its record of 231 mph (372 km/h) in March **1929**. In 1927 Segrave's Sunbeam reached about 328 km/h. The Blue Flame's 1,001 km/h in 1970 is correct.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Your own wedding's something not to miss."
  **Today:** Ford married Clara Bryant in April 1888 and joined the Edison Illuminating Company in Detroit in 1891, so he was not working there on his wedding day. A small dramatic shortcut.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Wheeled vehicles appear around 3500 BC in Mesopotamia ("Samaria" is Sumer) and in Central Europe at about the same time.
- Leonardo's spring-driven cart is real. Cugnot's steam fardier dates from 1769–70, and Murdoch's model steam carriage from about 1784.
- Benz's three-wheeled Patent-Motorwagen (1885–86) is usually counted as the first true automobile. Daimler and Maybach's four-wheeled motor carriage came in 1886. Calling Benz's a "motorized tricycle" is a matter of definition.
- The early makes listed are real: Peugeot, Panhard et Levassor, De Dion-Bouton, Fiat 3½ HP (1899), Rolls-Royce Silver Ghost (1907).
- Ford was a Dearborn farmer's son (born 1863) who fixed steam engines and became chief engineer at Edison Illuminating.
- The kitchen-sink engine test with Clara (Christmas Eve 1893) is accurate. The two-cylinder Quadricycle (1896) was too wide for the shed door, so he knocked out bricks.
- The Detroit Automobile Company (1899–1901) did collapse because Ford kept refining instead of producing. In October 1901 he won a race against Winton.
- "A hundred years ago" Cadillac and Olds are a few years early. Olds started in 1897 and Cadillac in 1902.
- Selden's patent (granted 1895) covered a road vehicle with an engine, not the internal combustion engine itself. In 1911 the court ruled it valid but not infringed, because Ford used Otto-type engines and Selden's was a Brayton type.
- The licensed manufacturers' association (ALAM) did snub independents at the New York shows. Ford himself set a speed record of 91 mph on frozen Lake St. Clair in January 1904.
- A Model T won the 1909 New York–Seattle race and was later disqualified.
- The moving assembly line came in 1913–14 and borrowed from meatpacking and other industries. The Model T fell from about $850 (1908) to $440 (1915) over several years, not two.
- The 10-millionth Model T came in 1924.
