# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e02: Archimedes and the Greeks
Maestro praises Greek thought: Pythagoras, Hippocrates, Socrates, Phidias, Protagoras, Anaxagoras and Democritus, whose atoms are acted out in a scene. The gnomon then leads to Eratosthenes measuring the Earth from shadows at Syene and Alexandria. Most of the episode follows Archimedes in Syracuse: the golden crown and "Eureka", the screw pump, the ship moved with one hand, the area of the circle, and the Roman siege in which he dies.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "the length of the running track used in the olympics 252 000 stadiums that comes to exactly"
  **Today:** Eratosthenes' method was sound. He took the angle of the Sun's shadow at Alexandria (about 7.2°, or 1/50 of a circle) when the Sun stood overhead at Syene, and multiplied by the distance between the two cities. But we do not know which stadion he used. The Olympic track is about 192 m, which gives about 48,000 km, roughly 20% too large. The show's "exactly 39,690 km" assumes a stadion of 157.5 m, a disputed modern reconstruction. Estimates for his stadion range from about 157 to 185 m, so his error was somewhere between about 1% and 16%. The 5,000-stadion distance was itself a rough figure, and Syene is not due south of Alexandria. "Only 300 km off" is a choice of number, not a fact.
  **Why it was believed:** The "within 1%" version was popular in textbooks and television (it fits the 157.5 m stadion). Few popular accounts mentioned that the length of the stadion is unknown.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Notice the weight of its volume in water that overflowed."
  **Today:** The bath, "Eureka" and the naked run through the streets come from Vitruvius, writing about 200 years later. The method he describes, comparing the water displaced by the crown with that displaced by an equal weight of gold, would barely have worked. For a 1 kg crown with 30% silver, the extra volume is about 13 cm³, a rise of a fraction of a millimetre in any reasonable vessel. Galileo (*La Bilancetta*, 1586) suggested that Archimedes more likely weighed the crown against gold while both hung in water, using his own law of buoyancy. The show gives the Vitruvius version as fact.
  **Why it was believed:** It is one of the best-known stories in the history of science, and Vitruvius is an ancient source.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "They rose to heights 2,000 years past before there was really anything new."
  **Today:** Between the Greeks and the Scientific Revolution, science moved forward in the Islamic world (al-Khwarizmi, Ibn al-Haytham, al-Biruni), in India (place-value numerals, zero), in China (as episode 1 itself shows) and in medieval Europe (Oxford calculators, Buridan, Oresme). Historians had long rejected the idea of 2,000 empty years.
  **Why it was believed:** The Renaissance story of a classical golden age, then "dark ages", then rebirth was still standard in popular books.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "Alexandria is a peaceful city, and I studied with Euclid, the greatest mathematician in the world."
  **Today:** Euclid was active around 300 BC, and Archimedes was born around 287 BC. If Archimedes studied in Alexandria (Diodorus says he spent time in Egypt), it was with Euclid's successors. His known correspondents there were Conon of Samos, Dositheus and Eratosthenes. Standard references in 1994 already said "probably studied under Euclid's successors".
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "The squaring of the circle."
  **Today:** Archimedes did not square the circle. With compass and straightedge it cannot be done, as Lindemann proved in 1882 when he showed that π is transcendental. In *Measurement of a Circle* he proved that a circle's area equals a right triangle with the radius and the circumference as its sides. He trapped π between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7 (3.1408–3.1429) using polygons of 96 sides, not between an outer and an inner square. The value 3.1416 is Ptolemy's (377/120), not Archimedes'. That areas of circles go as the square of the radius was already in Euclid (XII.2).
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "The blades are turned by the current in the river."
  **Today:** The Archimedes screw was turned by hand or by people treading it, as in Roman mines and Egyptian fields. It does not run on its own from a river current. No ancient source describes a screw driven by the current, and Vitruvius says men turned it by treading. Diodorus credits Archimedes with the screw during his stay in Egypt. Some historians think it is older still: Stephanie Dalley argued in the 1990s for Sennacherib's Assyria.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Athena, 60 feet high."
  **Today:** Phidias' gold-and-ivory Athena Parthenos was about 11.5–12 m (around 38–40 ft), not 60 ft. His bronze Athena Promachos may have been taller, but estimates for it also fall short of 60 ft.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- "Eureka" means "I have found it", and the gnomon really can serve as a sundial, find north and measure heights by shadows (Thales' pyramid trick). The "250 feet" pyramid is unnamed, but the Great Pyramid was about 146 m (481 ft).
- Pythagoras, Hippocrates "founding" medicine, Protagoras' "man is the measure" and Anaxagoras' *nous* are fair summaries. "Know thyself" was a Delphic maxim adopted by Socrates, and "I know that I know nothing" paraphrases Plato's *Apology*.
- Democritus (with his teacher Leucippus) proposed indivisible atoms about 2,400 years ago. His "laughing philosopher" nickname is traditional. Atoms were first imaged in the 1950s–80s (field-ion microscope, then the STM), and modern atoms are not indivisible.
- The four elements were Empedocles' theory. Showing evaporation as water becoming "air" fairly reflects Greek thinking.
- Eratosthenes did his work around 240 BC, using the summer solstice at Syene ("Siena"/"Cien" = Aswan), near the Tropic of Cancer, and got 252,000 stadia. In the anecdote, the Sun lit the bottom of a well.
- Syracuse was a large, rich Greek city in the 3rd century BC. "Half a million" is the top of old estimates, and modern figures are lower.
- Archimedes' father Phidias was an astronomer (Archimedes says so in *The Sand Reckoner*). "Court astronomer" is embellishment.
- Hiero's challenge: Plutarch says Archimedes single-handedly drew a loaded ship ashore with compound pulleys. "Give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth" is attributed to him by Pappus and others ("Comedians" is a mishearing of Archimedes).
- Hiero II died in 215 BC, and his young grandson Hieronymus was assassinated in 214. Marcellus besieged Syracuse in 214–212 BC, Archimedes' machines held off the Romans, and a soldier killed him despite Marcellus' order to spare him.
- The sledgehammer test of the crown and the goldsmith's confession are dramatic invention, but the reasoning (same weight, more volume, a less dense metal such as silver) is correct.
