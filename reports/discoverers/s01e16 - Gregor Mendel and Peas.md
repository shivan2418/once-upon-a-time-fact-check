# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e16: Gregor Mendel and Peas
The episode tells Gregor Mendel's life: a poor peasant boy in Silesia and Moravia, then an Augustinian friar in Brno who crossed thousands of pea plants and found dominant and recessive traits. It shows his work being ignored and then rediscovered in 1900, and it races through Morgan, Avery, Watson and Crick, McClintock and the genetic code to genetic engineering. Along the way it explains in simple terms how a recessive trait can disappear in one generation and come back in the next.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Mendel was too far ahead of his time."
  **Today:** Mendel's 1866 paper was printed in a journal sent to over 100 libraries, it was cited a handful of times before 1900 (for example in Focke's 1881 survey), and he corresponded with the botanist Carl Nägeli. Historians such as Robert Olby (1979) and Augustine Brannigan (1979) argue that his work was neglected less because it was "too advanced" than because it was read as a study of plant hybrids, which is mostly what Mendel intended. They also point out that the "rediscovery" story was partly shaped by the 1900 priority disputes.
  **Why it was believed:** The "lonely genius ignored by his time" story came from the 1900 rediscoverers and Iltis's 1924 biography, and it was standard in textbooks.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "What he discovered was the gene."
  **Today:** Mendel spoke of "elements" (*Elemente*) and characters. He gave statistical rules of inheritance, but he did not clearly describe paired, particle-like "genes" (the show's line "pairs of little particles"). Olby's 1979 paper "Mendel no Mendelian?" questions whether he thought of paired factors at all. The word "gene" dates from Johannsen (1909), and the modern gene concept grew out of 20th-century work.
  **Why it was believed:** Textbooks read 20th-century Mendelism back into Mendel's 1866 paper.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The little finches that ate leaves had thin beaks to cut them up, and the ones that ate nuts had large beaks to break them."
  **Today:** This repeats the finch legend (see episode 15): Darwin did not work out beak adaptation in the field. Thin-billed Galápagos finches mostly eat insects and large-billed ground finches crack seeds, so "leaves" and "nuts" are also wrong.
  **Why it was believed:** David Lack's 1947 book made Darwin's finches the textbook example of adaptation.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "I've observed for 20 years to learn that."
  **Today:** Mendel's pea experiments ran from about 1856 to 1863, roughly eight years, and he presented them in February and March 1865. The later line "20 years of research" is wrong for the same reason.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "And the peas will have a yellow pod."
  **Today:** This mixes up two of Mendel's traits. Yellow is dominant for the seed (cotyledon) colour, but for pod colour green is dominant over yellow. A plant with one yellow-seed and one green-seed factor has yellow *seeds*, not a yellow pod.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "These bees here, they produce twice as much honey as the others."
  **Today:** Mendel did keep bees and tried to cross local bees with Italian and Cyprian strains. The project largely failed, because queens mate in flight and he could not control the matings, so simply putting males in a hive with a queen doesn't work. One hybrid strain was reportedly so aggressive that it had to be destroyed. No high-yield bee came out of his work.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "It's the genetic code."
  **Today:** "Mr. Ocha" is probably Severo Ochoa. His lab did contribute to working out the code, but the decisive work was Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei's 1961 experiment, followed by Nirenberg's and Har Gobind Khorana's groups (Nobel Prize 1968, with Holley). Ochoa's 1959 Nobel was for synthesising RNA. No single person decoded the code.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Now we've learned that DNA is contained in genes which transmit characteristics."
  **Today:** This is backwards: genes are stretches of DNA. The episode said so correctly a moment earlier ("genes are made of DNA"), so this may be a dubbing slip.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- The opening cross is right: smooth and yellow are dominant, wrinkled and green recessive. About 28,000 plants is the figure usually quoted.
- Six billion people was a slight rounding up in 1994 (about 5.6 billion). The world reached 6 billion in 1999 and is about 8.2 billion now. Counting to 6 billion at one number a second does take about 190 years.
- Mendel's father grafted fruit trees and owed his lord three days of forced labour (*robot*) a week. Both details are accurate. Moravia was a centre of scientific sheep breeding.
- Mendel was poor and often hungry at school. His physics teacher Friedrich Franz recommended him to the Augustinian ("Augustan") St Thomas's Abbey in Brno, which he joined in 1843.
- "Brother Matthias" is probably Matouš Klácel, the abbey's botanist-philosopher. In reality Klácel emigrated to the USA and died there in 1882, so the deathbed scene in the garden is invented.
- The traits shown (pod colour, flower colour, stem length) are among Mendel's seven. Counting the parents as the first generation, the recessive green does reappear in the third generation (F2) in about a quarter of the offspring.
- "Inherit from aunts and uncles" is loose: relatives look alike because they share ancestors.
- Darwin did write in the *Origin* that the laws of inheritance were "quite unknown". Mendel's experiments started in 1856, before the *Origin*.
- Mendel did fruit-tree breeding and serious weather observation. The show leaves out his failed hawkweed (*Hieracium*) crosses, which discouraged him, and his election as abbot in 1868.
- The 1900 rediscovery by de Vries, Correns and Tschermak, and Bateson's extension of the rules to animals, are accurate.
- Morgan and Muller (fruit flies, genes on chromosomes, 1910s), Avery (DNA as the genetic material, 1944), Watson and Crick's model (1953) and McClintock's jumping genes are accurate in outline. Rosalind Franklin is left out.
- The genetic engineering claims were somewhat ahead of events in 1994: recombinant insulin dated from 1982 and the first gene-therapy trial from 1990. Since then, gene therapies such as Zolgensma (2019) and the CRISPR-based Casgevy (2023) have been approved for some inherited diseases.
- The warning about altering future generations proved timely: He Jiankui's 2018 gene-edited babies caused a worldwide outcry, and editing inheritable human genes for reproduction remains banned or blocked in most countries.
