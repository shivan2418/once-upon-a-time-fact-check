# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e04: The Measuring of Time
A quick history of timekeeping. It covers the origins of the week, Egyptian water clocks for night work, the clepsydra used to limit Athenian speeches, unequal "seasonal" hours, hourglasses and Alfred the Great's candle clock. It then tells of Su Song's great astronomical clock and its destruction, the first European mechanical clock, the Dondis' dials and astrarium in Padua, and Galileo's pendulum. It ends with the longitude problem, Harrison's chronometer, wristwatches, quartz and atomic clocks, and asks whether we have mastered time or become its slaves.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "The slaves work from sunup to sunset each day, O'Pharaoh. I don't have enough men."
  **Today:** The pyramids were built by paid and fed crews of workers, many of them conscripted for a season, not by slaves. The workers' tombs at Giza (found in 1990) and Mark Lehner's excavation of their town show bread, beer and meat rations and medical care. Water clocks are also an anachronism here: the oldest known clepsydra (Karnak, about 1400 BC) is more than a thousand years later than the Giza pyramids. To be fair, the scene's workers are paid in wheat and garlic, which is closer to the truth than its "slaves".
  **Why it was believed:** Herodotus, mixing it up with the Hebrew slavery of Exodus, and Hollywood epics made pyramid slaves the default image. The Giza finds were only starting to reach the public in 1994.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Then we'll all give you an hourglass with sand."
  **Today:** There is no reliable evidence of sandglasses in Greek or Roman times, or in Charlemagne's day. The first sure depiction is Lorenzetti's fresco in Siena (1338), and sandglasses appear in ships' records in the 14th century. A 12-hour hourglass for Charlemagne is a legend.
  **Why it was believed:** Older clock histories repeated unsourced stories of ancient and Carolingian hourglasses.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "It's in France that the very first mechanical clock was to be invented."
  **Today:** "Brother Gilbert" is Gerbert of Aurillac, a Benedictine who became Pope Sylvester II (died 1003). He did not invent the mechanical clock. The "horologium" he set up at Magdeburg was probably a sundial or a star-sighting instrument. The weight-driven clock with a verge-and-foliot escapement appeared somewhere in Europe around 1270–1300, and its inventor is unknown. The device in the scene, a weight, a drum, cogwheels and a striker with no escapement, would not keep time. Su Song's clock, shown just before, already had an escapement in 1088.
  **Why it was believed:** The Gerbert story appears in many older clock histories and French school books, though historians of horology (Lynn White, Needham, Landes's *Revolution in Time*, 1983) had dismissed it.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "the so-called number four, accurate enough to win the Admiralty Prize."
  **Today:** On its 1761–62 trial H4 was accurate enough to meet the Act's top requirement. But the Board of Longitude never formally awarded Harrison the prize. He received money in stages, and Parliament granted him £8,750 in 1773 after George III intervened, for about £23,000 in total. The prize was set by Parliament and run by the Board of Longitude, not the Admiralty.
  **Why it was believed:** "Harrison won the longitude prize" is a natural shortening, used in many popular accounts.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "The Byzantine Empire decided to honor the Emperor and present him with a magnificent gift."
  **Today:** The famous clock given to Charlemagne came from the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid in 807. It was a brass water clock that dropped bronze balls onto a cymbal and had mechanical horsemen, not a Byzantine oil clock. Oil-lamp clocks (glass reservoirs marked with hours) date from about the 17th–18th century.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Just a few years later, unfortunately, the Song Dynasty was overthrown and replaced,"
  **Today:** Su Song's clock tower was finished in 1088–1094. In 1127, more than 30 years later, the Jurchen Jin took Kaifeng, ending only the *Northern* Song; the Southern Song lasted until 1279. The Jin moved the clock to their capital at Yanjing (Beijing), but they could not get it working, and it decayed. It was not destroyed on an emperor's order to drive out evil spirits; that scene is invented.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Came Galileo's pendulum clock and his telescope."
  **Today:** Galileo designed a pendulum clock in 1641, but it was never finished in his lifetime. Christiaan Huygens built the first working pendulum clock in 1656–57. Pendulum clocks did not work on a rolling ship. Galileo's Jupiter-moons method (proposed 1612) worked on land but was impractical at sea, as the scene jokes.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "An error of five minutes means a two-degree error in longitude."
  **Today:** The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so 4 minutes of time equal 1° of longitude. A 5-minute error is 1.25° (about 140 km at the equator). A 2° error needs 8 minutes.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "No real effort was made until a naval tragedy occurred off the English coast on the rocks of the Silly Islands."
  **Today:** The Scilly wreck of Admiral Shovell's fleet (1707, about 1,400–2,000 dead) did help lead to the Longitude Act of 1714. But "no real effort" before it is wrong. Spain offered a prize from 1567 and again in 1598, which Galileo competed for. The Dutch States General also offered one. The Paris Observatory (1667) and Greenwich Observatory (1675) were founded largely to solve longitude.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Robert Hooke had already invented the spring-driven clock."
  **Today:** Spring-driven clocks with a coiled mainspring date from the early 15th century. Hooke (around 1658–60) and Huygens (1675) argued over the *balance spring*, the hairspring that regulates a watch, and the priority dispute is accurately shown. This may be a translation slip for the French *spiral*.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "and the first wristwatches appeared in 1910."
  **Today:** Breguet made a wristwatch for Caroline Murat, Queen of Naples, in 1810–12, and Patek Philippe made one for a Hungarian countess in 1868. Soldiers wore wrist "wristlets" from the 1880s, and Cartier's Santos dates from 1904. Wristwatches became common for men during and after the First World War.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "which is accurate to within one second every 3,000 years,"
  **Today:** That figure suits caesium clocks of around 1960. By 1994, primary standards such as NIST-7 (1993) were accurate to about 1 second in a million years or better. Today's optical clocks (2010s–2020s) reach about 1 second in 10–30 billion years. (The number could be a dub slip.)
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- The show rightly says that nobody knows how the seven-day week began, and that other cultures used weeks of 5, 8 or 10 days. The full moon recurs every 29.5 days, not 30, and the Etemenanki ziggurat as an observatory ("Tower of Babel") is a traditional guess.
- Egyptian night hours were counted by water clocks (from about 1400 BC). Outflow does not run at a constant rate, which is why the Karnak clepsydra has sloping sides.
- Athenian courts timed speeches with a clepsydra, and Hero wrote a (lost) treatise on water clocks. The "pocket clocks" of the Romans were portable sundials.
- Ancient "seasonal" hours, 12 for day and 12 for night, changed length with the seasons.
- Alfred's candle clock (Asser: six 12-inch candles, each burning four hours) is described correctly. Asser says Alfred vowed half his time to God, not a third, and candle clocks existed earlier in China.
- Su Song's clock (Kaifeng, 1088) drove an armillary sphere and a celestial globe and gave the time through a water-powered escapement ("Sousong" = Su Song).
- Strasbourg Cathedral's first astronomical clock was built in 1352–54.
- Jacopo Dondi built Padua's public clock (1344), and the family was later known as "Dondi dall'Orologio". Whether he invented the round dial is uncertain, and minute hands came much later (16th century).
- Giovanni Dondi's astrarium (1348–64, about 16 years) had seven faces, not six, and showed the calendar, Sun, Moon, planets and lunar nodes for eclipse prediction. The rival "Guido" is fiction.
- Louis XI did own portable clocks.
- Galileo noticed that a pendulum's swing takes the same time regardless of its size, and that the period depends on length (proportional to its square root).
- William Clement (about 1670) is usually credited with the anchor escapement, and Harrison's H4 solved longitude at sea.
- Quartz wristwatches (Seiko Astron, 1969) and atomic clocks are correctly placed at the end of the story.
