# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e12: Antoine Lavoisier and Chemistry
The episode follows Antoine Lavoisier from his prize for street lighting and his water-into-earth experiment, through his marriage to Marie-Anne Paulze, the discovery of oxygen and the overthrow of phlogiston, the ice calorimeter with Laplace, the synthesis of water and the metric system, to his execution as a former tax farmer in 1794. It teaches conservation of mass, the role of oxygen in burning and breathing, that water is a compound, and that knowledge must come from experiment.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "the republic has no more need of chemists or of scientists"
  **Today:** The famous "La République n'a pas besoin de savants" is not in any record of the trial. It first appears years later and is usually given to the tribunal's president, Coffinhal, not to Fouquier-Tinville. Denis Duveen (1954) and later historians traced it to legend. Lavoisier was condemned, with 27 other tax farmers including his father-in-law, on charges about the Ferme générale, not for being a scientist.
  **Why it was believed:** The line is repeated in almost every popular account of Lavoisier's death.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Nothing is destroyed, nothing is created, only transformed."
  **Today:** The slogan is a later paraphrase. Lavoisier wrote in the *Traité élémentaire de chimie* (1789) that nothing is created in operations of art or nature, and that the amount of matter is the same before and after. The idea of conservation was also much older (ancient Greeks; Lomonosov, 1748–56). It also does not follow from the 1768–69 water experiment as the episode suggests; that showed the "earth" came from the glass.
  **Why it was believed:** "Rien ne se perd, rien ne se crée, tout se transforme" is taught in French schools as Lavoisier's own words.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "Gentlemen, whenever we heat calcinate of mercury, we obtain a quite special gas. Which we call oxygen."
  **Today:** Priestley (1774) called the gas "dephlogisticated air" and defended phlogiston until his death in 1804. Lavoisier coined "oxygène" (1777). Carl Wilhelm Scheele had isolated the gas first ("fire air", about 1771–72), and the episode omits him. Priestley's account in Paris in October 1774 was given at a dinner with Lavoisier, not at the Academy.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Air is composed of at least three different gases."
  **Today:** Lavoisier's air was oxygen ("vital air") and nitrogen (his "azote"). Hydrogen is not a component of air in any real amount. The episode adds it to the list. (Argon, about 1% of air, was found in 1894.)
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Your potassium chlorate might replace the saltpeter that we're lacking."
  **Today:** Potassium chlorate was Claude-Louis Berthollet's discovery (1786–87), not Lavoisier's. Lavoisier ran the Gunpowder Administration from 1775 and mainly improved saltpetre production. The 1788 trial of chlorate powder at the Essonnes mill exploded and killed two people while Lavoisier and his wife were nearby.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Democritus taught atoms of many shapes, not "one element". The four elements come from Empedocles and Aristotle.
- Phlogiston (Becher, Stahl) was a principle of flammability, not heat itself. Ironically, Lavoisier later made heat an element of his own, "caloric".
- The Academy's gold medal for the street-lighting essay (1766), when Lavoisier was 22. He joined the Academy and bought into the Ferme générale in the same year (1768): correct.
- The water-into-earth experiment: water was heated in a sealed pelican for 101 days, not a month. The flask lost the weight of the residue and the total weight did not change, as the episode shows.
- His 1771 marriage to Marie-Anne Paulze, daughter of a fellow tax farmer, to escape the Comte d'Amerval, backed by her uncle the finance minister Terray: accurate. She was 13 and he was 28.
- Experimental farm (Fréchines) and the large burning lens: correct.
- Naming oxygen, and respiration as a slow combustion, are correct. Lavoisier wrongly thought oxygen was in all acids, hence "acid-former".
- Ice calorimeter with Laplace (1782–83), measuring a guinea pig's body heat: correct.
- Cavendish made water from "inflammable air" and "dephlogisticated air" (1781) but interpreted it in phlogiston terms. Lavoisier and Laplace (1783) declared water a compound and named hydrogen.
- "H2O" is the modern formula. Lavoisier had weight ratios, the 2:1 volume ratio came in 1805, and the formula was fixed in the 1860s.
- The hydrogen balloon (J. A. C. Charles, 1783) is fine as an illustration.
- His list of simple substances (*Traité*, 1789, 33 entries including light and caloric), and his calling it a revolution: fine.
- The Commission on Weights and Measures, the old pounds (16 ounces to the Paris pound, plus gros, grains, scruples), and the kilogram as a cubic decimetre of water: correct.
- Lavoisier's work on taxation and on old-age and savings schemes is broadly right.
- Louis XVI was executed in January 1793 and the academies were abolished in August 1793. Marat had attacked Lavoisier in print from 1780 and in 1791.
- Executed on 8 May 1794 with other tax farmers. "My life has been long and happy enough..." closely follows his last letter. "200 years ago" is exact for 1994.
