# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e06: Johannes Gutenberg and Printing
The episode traces writing from Sumer, Egypt and China, then shows Chinese bamboo books, the invention of paper (in a wasp's-nest legend) and Bi Sheng's movable clay type. It then follows Gutenberg as he moves from wooden blocks to cast metal type and a screw press borrowed from the wine press. It ends with Fust's loans and the lawsuit that cost Gutenberg his workshop. The lesson is that inventions build on earlier ones and that printing made books available to everyone.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "and look i just invented paper quickly ink"
  **Today:** Paper was not invented in one moment by Cai Lun (the official in this scene, whose name the transcript garbles) in 105 CE. Archaeologists had found older hemp paper from the Western Han dynasty (2nd–1st century BCE) at Baqiao (1957) and Fangmatan (1986). Cai Lun is now seen as the man who improved paper-making and presented it to the court. The wasp's-nest inspiration is a later legend.
  **Why it was believed:** Chinese official history (the *Hou Hanshu*) gives Cai Lun the credit, and Western popular books repeated it.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "selling your poor man's bible for? Half a florin."
  **Today:** Woodcut "block books" such as the *Biblia pauperum* do not predate Gutenberg. Allan Stevenson's watermark studies in the 1960s dated surviving block books to about 1450–1470 and later, so they were made at the same time as type printing or after it, not before. Single-sheet woodcuts did exist before Gutenberg.
  **Why it was believed:** The neat sequence "block books first, then movable type" was the standard story in older histories of printing.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "And so, as a result, the court of justice sentences you to the confiscation of all your"
  **Today:** The only record of the 1455 case is the Helmasperger Notarial Instrument, which records Gutenberg's oath and part of the judgment. It is not known exactly what Fust took. Most historians think Fust gained the Bible type and equipment as security. Gutenberg seems to have kept a workshop and gone on printing, and in 1465 the Archbishop of Mainz gave him a court post and pension. He was not left penniless and thrown out.
  **Why it was believed:** "Genius robbed by his financier" is the usual romantic version of the story.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "Around the same time, the Egyptians invented hieroglyphics, a sacred script, and almost"
  **Today:** Egypt (about 3200 BCE) is right, but the "almost at the same time, the Chinese" that follows is not. The earliest Chinese writing, on Shang oracle bones, dates to about 1250 BCE, some 2,000 years after Sumer. Older Neolithic signs are not accepted as writing.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "And yes, Bang was right. It would take another 1,000 years for printing to"
  **Today:** Bi Sheng ("Bang") made movable clay type around 1040 (described by Shen Kuo). That was about 400 years before Gutenberg, not 1,000. Even Chinese woodblock printing (7th–8th century) was only about 700 years earlier. Korea was printing with cast metal type by the 13th–14th centuries (the *Jikji*, 1377). The scene is also confused: Bi Sheng's type was carved from clay and fired, not made by pressing clay onto a master character.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "I would need 8,000 florins. 8,000?"
  **Today:** Fust lent 800 guilders in 1450 and another 800 in about 1452, both at 6%. In 1455 he sued for 2,026 guilders including interest. The episode's 8,000 + 8,000 = 16,000 is ten times too high, possibly a translation error. The 6% interest is right.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "We will buy the paper in England"
  **Today:** The paper for the Gutenberg Bible was Italian, from Piedmont: watermarks show a bull's head and a bunch of grapes. England had no paper mill until John Tate's, in about 1490.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "I am from Mayo's. I have come to see you on behalf of Johan Fust. He is my father-in-law."
  **Today:** Peter Schöffer came from Gernsheim and had been a scribe in Paris. He married Fust's daughter Christina years later, in the 1460s. Gutenberg had moved back to Mainz by 1448 on his own, so Schöffer did not fetch him from Strasbourg.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Sumerian writing (about 3400–3200 BCE) grew out of keeping accounts of goods such as grain.
- Bamboo-strip books really were heavy, and silk was the costly alternative. Bamboo slips were in use for well over a thousand years.
- Paper reached Europe (Spain) in the 11th–12th centuries, roughly a thousand years after it appeared in China.
- The episode rightly says many people worked on printing and that history picked out one name (Gutenberg). This is better than the lone-genius story.
- Gutenberg ("Hans", that is Henne or Johannes) did experiment in Strasbourg in the 1430s–40s with partners, which explains the Alsace wine. "Antoine" is invented, and "Malins" and "Malenz" are mishearings of Mainz.
- Type metal of lead, tin and antimony is the standard account ("slate gold" is probably a mishearing of tin). No type from Gutenberg's own shop survives. The usual story is a steel punch struck into a copper matrix. In 2001 Agüera y Arcas and Needham found that his earliest type may have been cast from temporary matrices such as sand or clay, so the clay scene is unexpectedly close to that idea.
- The screw press was adapted from wine, paper and linen presses. Oil-based ink was one of Gutenberg's key improvements.
- About 180 Bibles (around 135 on paper and 45 on vellum) need several thousand calfskins, so "200" and "8,000 calves" are in the right range. Six presses are thought to have been used.
- The 1455 lawsuit over Fust's loans at 6% interest happened. Gutenberg's wish that "the simplest of peasants" should own a Bible is fiction: his Bible was an expensive lectern book for churches and the rich.
- About 10 million books printed by 1500 fits modern estimates of the incunabula (about 9–12 million copies). "10 billion a year" (short scale) is about right in order of magnitude for the 1990s.
