# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e25: Neil Armstrong, the Moon and Space
The episode runs through dreams of reaching the Moon from Lucian's whirlwind and the Persian king's eagles to Tsiolkovsky's rocket theory, Goddard's liquid-fuel rocket and von Braun's V-2. It then stages the Soviet–American space race as a table-tennis match between leaders. Most of the episode reconstructs Apollo 11: Armstrong's Gemini 8 emergency, the boulder-strewn landing site, the computer alarms and the first step.

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "Just when our rockets with the 4,000-kilometer range were almost ready."
  **Today:** The intercontinental A9/A10 "Amerika-Rakete" existed only as studies and design drawings. It was nowhere near ready in 1945. The V-2 (about 320 km range) was Germany's only operational ballistic missile.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "They couldn't get back, not enough fuel to return to orbit."
  **Today:** The lunar module had a separate ascent stage with its own propellant. Until touchdown the crew could abort at any time by firing it back to orbit, and a landing on uneven ground was not by itself a death sentence. The real risks were running the descent stage dry before landing (about 25 seconds of margin) and a failure of the single ascent engine afterwards.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "We've lost them. They'll die on the moon, and they'll know they're going to die."
  **Today:** Mission Control never lost contact with Eagle. Earth talked to the lunar module directly while Columbia was behind the Moon. What was uncertain was Eagle's exact position (it landed about 6 km past the target), and Collins failed to spot it through his sextant. The panic is dramatisation.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Americans, Russians, Chinese, Indians, Eskimos, people in Africa, Puan, and the French, the Italians."
  **Today:** The audience of about 500–600 million is a fair estimate, but China did not broadcast the landing, and Soviet television did not show it live, only brief clips later. Russian and Chinese viewers were mostly not watching.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- **Quote:** "The Greeks dreamed of a tornado carrying a ship to the moon." This is Lucian of Samosata's *True History* (2nd century AD), in which a whirlwind carries a ship to the Moon.
- **Quote:** "Around the year 1000, the Persian king, Caicos, had another idea." This is the legendary king Kay Kāvus, whose eagle-drawn throne appears in Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh* (completed about 1010). The legend is older than the text.
- "169" is a mishearing of 1609. Galileo's telescope showed lunar mountains and craters.
- **Quote:** "In 1700, the Englishman David Russon had an idea." David Russen's *Iter Lunare* (1703) proposed launching by a giant steel spring on a mountaintop. The cannon objections that follow (crushing acceleration) belong to Jules Verne's 1865 idea.
- **Quote:** "The first to achieve this was a Russian, Konstantin Chalovsky." Tsiolkovsky worked out the rocket theory (1903) and proposed liquid hydrogen and oxygen, but he launched nothing. "Achieve" really means "show it was possible", as the following lines make clear.
- **Quote:** "The moon is 380,000 kilometers from the Earth and circles our planet every 27 days." About 384,400 km and 27.3 days (sidereal). Fine.
- Goddard's first liquid-fuel rocket (gasoline and liquid oxygen) flew on 16 March 1926. He remained little known and was mocked by the *New York Times* in 1920.
- V-2 range of about 320 km and gyroscopic guidance are correct. The episode leaves out the concentration-camp slave labour at Mittelbau-Dora, where more people died building the V-2 than were killed by it.
- **Quote:** "To Siberia or to Texas?" Von Braun's team surrendered to the Americans and went to Fort Bliss, Texas. The Soviets took other specialists (e.g. Gröttrup) to Gorodomlya Island, not Siberia.
- **Quote:** "Flora, the first American satellite, is put in orbit." A mishearing of Explorer 1 (31 January 1958). Sputnik 1 and Sputnik 2 (with Laika) came in 1957, and Luna 3's far-side photos in 1959.
- Gagarin and Titov (1961), Shepard and Grissom's suborbital flights (1961), Glenn and Mariner 2 (1962), Tereshkova and Cooper's 22 orbits (1963), and the order Khrushchev, Kennedy, Johnson, Brezhnev are all correct.
- **Quote:** "It lasted 10 minutes, and then an American, white, took one for 21 minutes." Leonov's spacewalk (March 1965) lasted about 12 minutes and Ed White's (June 1965) about 20–23 minutes.
- Gemini 8 (1966): docking with the Agena, a stuck thruster (on the Gemini, not the Agena) causing a violent spin, and an emergency splashdown in the Pacific. Fair.
- **Quote:** "Three rotations an hour." The "barbecue roll" passive thermal control turned the spacecraft about three times an hour. Accurate.
- Lunar orbit insertion behind the Moon, out of contact. The 1202/1201 program alarms ("the computer couldn't take any more data") came from an overloaded guidance computer. Armstrong took manual control to fly past the boulders of West Crater, and dust blocked his view. All accurate.
- **Quote:** "Wait, just a second. Pressure's still too high." True: the hatch would not open until the residual cabin pressure dropped.
- **Quote:** "Supposed to say, uh, one small step for a man, giant leap for mankind." A neat nod to the missing "a" debate.
- **Quote:** "Look, children, the moon, humanity's dream for millions of years, you realize." Our species is about 300,000 years old. Treat it as rhetoric.
- "1901" is a mishearing of 1969. The closing note on later international cooperation (Mir–Shuttle, ISS) is fair.
