# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e19: Guglielmo Marconi and Sound Waves
The episode opens with a quick history of messages: runners, signal fires, smoke signals and Chappe's optical telegraph. It then explains invisible electromagnetic waves through Hertz and follows Marconi from attic experiments in Bologna to England, the Channel link, the 1901 transatlantic signal and the Titanic. It ends with Bell Labs' transistor as the next step in communication.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "It's an Italian, Guglielmo Marconi, who was the first to have the idea."
  **Today:** Others had the idea of signalling with Hertzian waves before Marconi's 1895 tests, or at the same time. Oliver Lodge sent Morse signals by radio at Oxford in 1894, and Popov, Jagadish Chandra Bose and Tesla were working on it too. Marconi's real contribution was making it work over long distances and selling it, with a grounded high antenna and a better coherer. In 1943 the US Supreme Court struck down key Marconi patent claims because of earlier work by Lodge, Tesla and Stone. The show does credit Branly's coherer and Popov's antenna, which is better than most popular accounts.
  **Why it was believed:** Marconi's Nobel Prize (1909, shared with Braun) and his company's publicity made him "the inventor of radio" in popular books.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "Uh, well, the sun's distance is 50 million kilometers."
  **Today:** The Sun is about 150 million km away. Whisper may have dropped "a hundred and", so this may be a transcription error.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "and the SOS was heard by several ships"
  **Today:** Several ships heard the Titanic's CQD/SOS in 1912, but only the Carpathia reached the scene and picked up survivors, about 705–712 of roughly 2,224 people aboard. "One out of three" is right, and "740" is a little high.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- "Claude Schaap, 1793" is Claude Chappe's optical telegraph, adopted in 1793 (first line Paris–Lille, 1794).
- Radio waves pass through walls, Hertz produced and detected them (1886–88), and they travel at about 300,000 km/s. All correct.
- Marconi failed university and naval-academy entrance and studied privately and at technical schools. The still-raid scene is invented.
- The attic tests at Villa Griffone (1894–95) and the transmission over a hill to an assistant (about 2 km, 1895) match the sources.
- The Italian ministry showed no interest, and in February 1896 he moved to England, where customs damaged his apparatus.
- The Channel link (Wimereux–South Foreland, March 1899) covered about 50 km, roughly 30 miles.
- In December 1901 he claimed to receive the letter "S" from Poldhu in Cornwall at Signal Hill in Newfoundland, about 3,500 km, though the signal went from England, not to it. Some people doubted the claim at the time. Glace Bay on Cape Breton followed in 1902. The "North Pole" line is garbled.
- "1999" is probably a mishearing of 1909, when the RMS Republic's radio call saved almost everyone aboard.
- Marconi was already famous (Nobel Prize 1909) before the Titanic, though the disaster made shipboard radio compulsory.
- The government payment figures can't be checked and are best treated as dramatization.
- Bardeen and Brattain ("Bertain", "Walter") with Shockley invented the transistor at Bell Labs in December 1947. It amplifies and switches current, as said.
