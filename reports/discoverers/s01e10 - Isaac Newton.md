# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e10: Isaac Newton
The episode follows Isaac Newton from a farm boy making water clocks, through Cambridge, the plague years at Woolsthorpe (prism experiments and the falling apple), his laws of motion and universal gravitation, Halley's push to publish, the quarrels with Hooke and Leibniz, and his later life at the Mint and the Royal Society. It teaches that gravity holds the Moon and planets in orbit and that Newton's mechanics still underpins modern technology.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "The apple fell on me."
  **Today:** The apple story is genuine in that Newton told it himself in old age (to William Stukeley and John Conduitt, 1726). He said that seeing an apple fall at Woolsthorpe set him thinking about gravity reaching to the Moon. The apple hitting his head is a later embellishment. Here it falls on his sister rather than on Newton, but the scene is the same legend.
  **Why it was believed:** The bonk-on-the-head version is a staple of cartoons and children's books.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "If only I could measure the Earth's radius, precisely."
  **Today:** The old story says Newton's Moon test failed in 1666 because he used a poor figure for the Earth's size, so he shelved gravity for 20 years until Picard's measurement (1669–71). Florian Cajori (1928) and later Newton scholars (I. Bernard Cohen, Richard Westfall, 1980) showed this is doubtful. In 1666 Newton did not yet have *universal* gravitation, or the proof that a sphere attracts as if all its mass were at its centre. Both came in 1684–87, after Halley's visit. The episode repeats both the radius-delay story and the "all worked out in the plague year" story ("He's already made most of his great discoveries").
  **Why it was believed:** Newton's own late recollections encouraged the "annus mirabilis" legend, and popular books repeated it.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Almost none of our great discoverers were recognized by the contemporaries."
  **Today:** Newton is a counter-example: he became Lucasian professor at 26, president of the Royal Society, Master of the Mint and a knight, and he received a state funeral in Westminster Abbey. Critics like Huygens and Leibniz objected to particular ideas (action at a distance), as the episode shows, but they admired his work.
  **Why it was believed:** The "misunderstood genius" story is a standard theme of popular history of science.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "do you realize children that astronauts are floating in space millions of miles over our head right now"
  **Today:** In 1994 astronauts (the Space Shuttle, Mir) orbited about 300–400 km up, a few hundred miles. Only the Apollo crews ever went farther, to the Moon about 384,000 km (240,000 miles) away. Communications satellites sit about 36,000 km up. "Millions of miles" is far off.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "So it must have an acceleration 3,600 times greater than that of the apple."
  **Today:** It is the reverse. The Moon is about 60 Earth radii away, so by the inverse-square law its acceleration toward the Earth is 3,600 times *smaller* than the apple's: about 2.7 mm/s² against 9.8 m/s². That match was the whole point of Newton's Moon test. The numbers given just before (6,400 km, 380,000 km, factor 60) are right.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "Before Newton, nobody had ever realized that it's because of centrifugal force that the moon doesn't fall to earth."
  **Today:** Newton's insight was the opposite: no outward force holds the Moon up. Seen from outside, only gravity acts on the Moon. It keeps "falling" toward the Earth, but its sideways motion (inertia) means it keeps missing, as the episode's own cannonball example later shows. "Centrifugal force balances gravity" only describes things from a rotating frame. In any case the idea was not new with Newton: Huygens worked out centrifugal force around 1659 (published 1673), and Borelli (1666) and Hooke (1679) described orbits as a balance between a pull toward the centre and the tendency to fly off.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "Saturn is a hundred times larger than the Earth. You'd feel heavier, so heavier."
  **Today:** Saturn has about 95 times the Earth's mass, but its radius is about 9.5 times larger, so gravity at its cloud tops is only about 1.07 times Earth's. You would barely feel heavier. (A child says this line, but the episode lets it stand.)
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "So, my young friend, you have discovered a comet."
  **Today:** Halley did not discover the 1682 comet that bears his name. Many astronomers saw it, and the Great Comet of 1680 was first found by Gottfried Kirch. Halley was already a Fellow of the Royal Society known for his southern star catalogue, not a novice. What prompted the *Principia* was Halley's visit to Newton in August 1684 with the question of what orbit an inverse-square force produces. Halley used Newton's theory to show that the comets of 1531, 1607 and 1682 were one object only in 1705, after the *Principia* was published, and he predicted its return in 1758.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- "In the very year he died, Isaac Newton was to be born": Galileo died in January 1642 and Newton was born on 25 December 1642 (Old Style). In the Gregorian calendar the dates are 1642 and 1643.
- Water clocks and sundials as a boy, and a mother who wanted him to farm, come from Stukeley's account: correct.
- "1992 sir" (for the discovery of America) is a mishearing or joke, and "2A plus 2B plus 2AB" is a garbled (a + b)² = a² + b² + 2ab.
- Entered Trinity College, Cambridge in 1661 as a subsizar, working for his keep: correct.
- The 1665 Great Plague (bubonic plague, not the 1347–51 Black Death) officially killed about 68,600 in London, and probably closer to 100,000. Cambridge closed, and Newton went to Woolsthorpe.
- The prism experiments: white light is a mix of colours, a single colour does not split further (the *experimentum crucis*), and the colours can be recombined into white. The episode gets this right.
- "Anna" is presumably his half-sister Hannah Smith.
- The law of gravitation: it applies to all bodies, not just bodies "in orbit", and the force goes with the product of the masses. The magnet analogy for weakening with distance is fine as an illustration.
- The Great Fire of London (1666) destroyed about 13,200 houses. By then the plague had largely ended in London.
- The reflecting telescope (1668) was a forerunner of today's big telescopes rather than "the modern telescope". Lucasian professor in 1669 at 26 (the episode says 27).
- The oblate Earth is in the *Principia* (1687), later confirmed by the French expeditions of the 1730s, including Maupertuis (episode 11).
- The laws of motion and Newton's cannonball thought experiment for orbits are correct. Inertia had been stated by Galileo and Descartes.
- "a child at the shore of a great unknown ocean" is close to a real Newton saying, recorded in old age (Spence's *Anecdotes*).
- Hooke's priority claim on the inverse-square law (1686) is real. The calculus dispute with Leibniz was real, though the plagiarism charges ran mostly from Newton's camp against Leibniz. Today both are credited independently. Huygens and Leibniz did reject attraction acting at a distance.
- Newton's interest in alchemy and theology, and his hunting of counterfeiters (e.g. William Chaloner) as Warden (1696) and Master (1699) of the Mint: correct.
- President of the Royal Society in 1703. He was knighted in 1705 by Queen Anne at Cambridge, a political honour, not by the Royal Society.
- "Everything Newton taught us... still true" is qualified a moment later with relativity: fair.
