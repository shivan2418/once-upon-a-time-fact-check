# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e22: Marie Curie
The episode follows Maria Skłodowska from her childhood in Russian-ruled Warsaw to her studies at the Sorbonne, her marriage to Pierre Curie and their isolation of radium in a leaky shed. It goes on to her 1903 doctorate, her two Nobel Prizes, her defeat at the Académie des Sciences, her mobile X-ray units in the First World War and her death from radiation-related anaemia. Its themes are perseverance, sharing science freely, and the barriers that women faced in science.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "The University of Paris has never awarded a doctorate to a woman. It's unprecedented, and a foreigner, well."
  **Today:** The American astronomer Dorothea Klumpke received a doctorate in mathematical sciences from the University of Paris in 1893, ten years before Curie, with a thesis on Saturn's rings. Women had received Paris medical doctorates since Madeleine Brès in 1875. Curie's 1903 thesis made her the first woman in France with a doctorate in *physics*.
  **Why it was believed:** Eve Curie's 1937 biography and the many popular lives based on it presented Marie as the first woman at every step, and Klumpke had been largely forgotten.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "At the time, not one other woman had a doctorate in science."
  **Today:** Besides Klumpke in Paris, several women had science doctorates elsewhere by 1903. Sofia Kovalevskaya received a mathematics doctorate from Göttingen in 1874, and a number of women had science PhDs from Swiss and American universities.
  **Why it was believed:** The same "first woman" framing found in popular Curie biographies.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "It was diagnosed for at the time they didn't know that radiation could be deadly."
  **Today:** By 1934 the danger was well known. Radiologists had been dying of radiation injuries since the 1900s, the British X-ray and Radium Protection Committee issued rules in 1921, and the "Radium Girls" deaths were public by 1925–28. Several of Curie's own co-workers had died. What was missing was a precise idea of the safe dose, and Curie herself long played down the link to her own illness. Her death certificate gave "aplastic pernicious anaemia".
  **Why it was believed:** The "innocent pioneers who could not have known" story was the standard heroic account.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "She's Polish, born in Warsaw, which has just been occupied by the Russians,"
  **Today:** Warsaw had been under Russian rule since 1815, as capital of the Congress Kingdom of Poland. What was new around 1867 was the harsh Russification that followed the crushed January Uprising of 1863–64.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Mathematics from the University of Paris to, first, Marie Sklodowska."
  **Today:** She came first in the physics licence (1893) and second in the mathematics licence (1894).
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "It's the highest scientific award in all of England."
  **Today:** The Royal Society's Davy Medal, given jointly to Pierre and Marie in 1903, is a chemistry medal. The Society's highest award is the Copley Medal.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "She spends her vacation in Switzerland where she meets a remarkable young man, Albert Einstein."
  **Today:** They had already met at the first Solvay Conference in Brussels in 1911, and Einstein wrote her a supportive letter during the Langevin scandal that year. The 1913 trip was a hiking holiday in the Engadin that they took together.
  **Confidence:** high

## Simplified but fine
- Born November 1867, so she was 3–4 in 1871. Tsar Alexander II and the clandestine Polish-history lessons under Russian school inspectors fit the period.
- The sisters' agreement was that Marie worked as a governess to fund Bronia's medical studies in Paris, and then went herself in 1891. That was about six years, not seven.
- Pasteur was alive (d. 1895) when she arrived. She met Pierre in 1894. Pierre had discovered piezoelectricity with his brother Jacques (1880) and worked on symmetry in physics.
- Becquerel (1896) used uranium salts on photographic plates wrapped in black paper, sometimes with metal sheets in between. "Tube wrapped in aluminum" is close enough.
- **Quote:** "The uranium radiation seems to have a variable factor, depending on the degree of purity." This is garbled. Her key finding (1898) was the opposite: the radiation depended only on the amount of uranium, which made it an atomic property. Pitchblende was more active than its uranium content allowed, which pointed to new elements. The show skips polonium (July 1898) and goes straight to radium (December 1898).
- "Monsieur Schultz" is a mishearing of Paul Schützenberger, director of the École de physique et chimie, who lent them the shed. The pitchblende came from Joachimsthal in Bohemia (then Austria), as the show says.
- **Quote:** "We have processed more than 10 tons of pitch blend to obtain one gram of radium." In 1902 they had about 0.1 g of radium chloride from about a tonne of residues, so the ratio is about right but they never had a full gram then.
- Refusing to patent the radium process and her coining of "radioactivity" (1898) are accurate. The heat output of radium was measured by Pierre Curie and Laborde in 1903.
- 1903 Nobel in Physics shared with Becquerel, with the Curies' half about 70,000 francs. The Osiris Prize was 1903. "Kaz Prize" and "Gagné Prize" are mishearings of the La Caze and Gegner prizes.
- The radium burn: Becquerel was burned by a tube in his waistcoat pocket and Pierre burned his arm on purpose, which led to radium therapy ("Curietherapy").
- After Pierre's death (1906) she took over his chair, the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne.
- The Academy vote of January 1911 is accurate: Branly 30, Curie 28 in the second round. "Marcel Brouin" is Marcel Brillouin. The 1911 Chemistry Nobel is correct.
- About 20 radiological cars ("petites Curies") and about 200 fixed posts in WWI, with about 150 women trained as operators. "A million x-rays" is the usual estimate.
- 1921 US tour and gift of a gram of radium from President Harding at the White House. The Dempsey–Carpentier fight was July 1921, so the reporter anecdote is chronologically plausible.
