# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e21: Aviation
The episode starts with Icarus and Leonardo, then moves to the Montgolfier balloons, the Channel crossing by Blanchard and Jeffries and a parade of failed 19th-century flying machines. It covers Ader, Lilienthal, Maxim and Chanute, then follows the Wright brothers in detail from the toy helicopter to the gliders, Kitty Hawk, the 1904–05 flights and the 1908 Le Mans demonstrations. It ends with Blériot's Channel crossing, Adrienne Bolland, Lindbergh and the Comet.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "leonardo da vinci did more than anyone to realize that"
  **Today:** Leonardo's notes on flight were not published until the late 19th century, so they influenced nobody who actually built aircraft. The founder of aeronautics was Sir George Cayley. In 1799 he described the modern fixed-wing aircraft, with lift, thrust and control kept separate, and in 1853 his glider carried a person. The episode never mentions him. Giovanni Borelli (1680) was the one who showed that human muscles are too weak for flapping flight.
  **Why it was believed:** Leonardo as the prophet of flight was a fixture of popular histories, while Cayley was little known outside Britain.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "The Montgolfier brothers made a balloon, and they went off in free flight."
  **Today:** The Montgolfiers built the balloon, but the first crewed free flight (21 November 1783) carried Pilâtre de Rozier and the Marquis d'Arlandes. Joseph Montgolfier flew only once, in 1784, and Étienne never did. Charles and Robert's hydrogen balloon flew ten days later.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "It was a four-place biplane with two propeller gasoline engines."
  **Today:** Langley's Aerodrome A was a tandem-wing monoplane with one pilot, Charles Manly. A single 52 hp Manly-Balzer engine drove two propellers. It fell into the Potomac on 7 October and 8 December 1903, the second time nine days before Kitty Hawk.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "6,000 pounds sterling was a fortune."
  **Today:** The Daily Mail prize for flying the Channel in 1909 was £1,000. The figure may be garbled in the transcript.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "alibaba and his flying carpet"
  **Today:** The flying carpet in the *Arabian Nights* tradition belongs to Prince Husain in the tale of Prince Ahmed and Pari-Banu, not to Ali Baba, and neither story is Chinese. The transcript is garbled here, so the dub may have listed China (kites) separately.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Blanchard and the American Dr. John Jeffries crossed the Channel by balloon on 7 January 1785 and threw almost everything overboard to stay up.
- Lana de Terzi's vacuum airship (1670), Gusmão's "Passarola" (1709), Bacqueville's Seine jump (1742) and Letur are all real. Moy's Aerial Steamer (1875) did lift a few inches while tethered.
- Ader's bat-winged Éole hopped about 50 m in 1890, and his Avion III failed in 1897. To its credit, the show does not repeat the French claim that Ader flew first.
- Otto Lilienthal (with his brother Gustav) made about 2,000 glides and died after a stall in August 1896. Maxim tested his steam rig in 1894, and Chanute flew gliders on the Lake Michigan dunes in 1896.
- The toy helicopter from Bishop Milton Wright (1878), Chanute's *Progress in Flying Machines* (1894) and Wilbur's 1901 "not within a thousand years" are all accurate.
- The Wrights' camber and wing-warping work is fair. The rear surface was a movable rudder, not a "tail aileron". Their 1902 glide of 26 seconds and about 190 m matches the record.
- "Captain Faber" is Ferdinand Ferber.
- On 17 December 1903 the first flight covered 37 m. The 260 m flight was Wilbur's fourth that day. The coin toss is accurate.
- On 20 September 1904 they flew their first full circle, 1,244 m. In October 1905 they flew 39 km in 39 minutes. The press and the US Army ignored them.
- The skeptical French aviators, the 1908 Le Mans flights and Edith Berg's tied skirt (the origin of the hobble skirt) are all accurate.
- Latham's Antoinette, designed by Levavasseur (misheard "Lavoisier"), ditched in the Channel, and Blériot was met at Dover in July 1909.
- Adrienne Bolland crossed the Andes (1921), Lindbergh flew the Atlantic solo (1927), and the Comet became the first jetliner (1952).
