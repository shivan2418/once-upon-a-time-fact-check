# Once Upon a Time... The Discoverers — s01e01: The Chinese, Our Ancestors
Maestro opens the series by promising the story of the great discoverers, then spends the episode on early Chinese inventions. He covers row farming and the seed drill, horse harnesses, stirrups, kites and the first man carried by a kite, suspension bridges, the parachute, paper, paper money, printing, movable type and gunpowder. The message is that China was often centuries ahead of Europe. The episode closes on a plea to use inventions for peace rather than war.

## Outdated (since 1994)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Paper, playing cards, the decimal system, steam engines, the parachute..."
  **Today:** China never had a steam engine. This claim comes from Robert Temple's popular *The Genius of China* (1986), which simplified Joseph Needham. Needham argued only that Chinese water-powered bellows (Du Shi, 31 AD) used the same crank-and-connecting-rod layout later used by steam engines. Nothing in China was ever driven by steam. The same list's "hydraulic power, even the steam engine" repeats the error.
  **Why it was believed:** Temple's list of Chinese "firsts" was a bestseller and was widely copied in the late 1980s and 1990s. Many items on its list are real, and this one sounded just as solid.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "How blood circulates."
  **Today:** The *Huangdi Neijing* (around the 2nd–1st century BC) describes blood and *qi* flowing in continuous cycles through channels. That is a real and early idea, but it is not circulation in William Harvey's sense (1628): a heart pumping blood out through arteries and back through veins in a closed loop, proved by measurement. Calling it the discovery of circulation 1,000–2,000 years before Europe overstates it.
  **Why it was believed:** Temple, following Needham's more cautious wording, presented it as "the circulation of the blood".
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "And brandy as well."
  **Today:** Secure evidence of distilled alcohol in China dates only from the Jin and Yuan dynasties (12th–13th century). That is about when distilled "aqua vitae" appears in Salerno and elsewhere in Europe. Earlier Chinese dates are argued but not proven, so China being far ahead on brandy is doubtful.
  **Why it was believed:** Temple's book gave an early Chinese date for distilling spirits.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The same horse could now pull ten times the load."
  **Today:** The Chinese did develop the breast-strap harness (by about the 4th century BC) and the collar harness (around the 5th century AD) well before Europe. However, the claim that the ancient "throat-and-girth" harness choked horses, and that the collar multiplied pulling power several times over, comes from Richard Lefebvre des Noëttes (1931). Trials by J. Spruytte (1977) and later work showed that the ancient harness, properly fitted, did not choke the horse. The "ten times" figure has no basis.
  **Why it was believed:** Lefebvre des Noëttes' thesis was taken up by Needham and Lynn White and stayed standard in popular histories of technology.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The Chinese stirrup set the stage of history for Western knights in armor."
  **Today:** This is Lynn White's "stirrup thesis" (1962): stirrups made mounted shock combat possible and so created feudal knighthood. Historians (Sawyer and Hilton 1963, Bachrach 1970 and others) rejected it soon after. Steppe horsemen conquered before they had stirrups, and Frankish adoption of stirrups did not line up with any change in tactics.
  **Why it was believed:** White's *Medieval Technology and Social Change* was hugely influential and memorable, and popular books kept repeating it.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The universal joint that makes car transmissions possible."
  **Today:** The Chinese device (Ding Huan, about 180 AD) was a gimbal (a "Cardan suspension"), not a universal joint. Gimbals were also known earlier in the Greek world (Philo of Byzantium, 3rd century BC). The universal joint used in drive shafts goes back to Cardano and Robert Hooke (17th century).
  **Why it was believed:** Temple's list calls the gimbal a "Cardan suspension", which is easily confused with the cardan (universal) joint.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "This is the story of a young man who became emperor in the Zia dynasty."
  **Today:** This is the legend of Shun, recorded by Sima Qian (around 90 BC), who escaped from a burning granary by holding two large bamboo hats and jumping. Shun is a legendary ruler, traditionally placed before the Xia dynasty, around 2200 BC (about 4,200 years ago, not "nearly 5,000"). The story is a legend, not evidence that the parachute existed "centuries before our era". ("Zia" is Xia.)
  **Why it was believed:** Temple presented it as the first parachute, and popular books repeated it.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1994)
- **Quote:** "The Chinese were 2,000 years ahead."
  **Today:** The earliest stirrups are Chinese: a single mounting stirrup on a figurine from 302 AD and paired stirrups by about 322 AD. Stirrups reached Europe with the Avars around the 6th–7th century. That is a lead of roughly 300–400 years, not 2,000. India had toe-loop stirrups even earlier. The show is right that the Egyptians, Greeks and Romans rode without stirrups.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Then you will have the invention of the parachute in Europe, straight from China."
  **Today:** The European parachute did not come "straight from China". The report that inspired it was Simon de La Loubère's account (1691) of acrobats in Siam (Thailand), not China. It inspired Louis-Sébastien Lenormand, who jumped from a tower in Montpellier in 1783 and coined the word "parachute". André-Jacques Garnerin made the first jump from a balloon on 22 October 1797 over Paris, from about 900–1,000 m. That was a hydrogen balloon, not one of the Montgolfiers'. Faust Vrančić had already published a parachute design in 1595. (The garbled "1080" may be a mishearing of "1680s".)
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "It was only in the 13th century that paper was to be made in Europe."
  **Today:** Paper was made in Muslim Spain, at Xàtiva near Valencia, by the mid-12th century, and it was used there earlier. The 13th century is right for Christian Europe (Fabriano in Italy, around 1270s). "15 centuries after China" is about right either way.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "1041, a Chinese Picheng invents movable type, 26 characters in the West, up to 400,000 in China."
  **Today:** Bi Sheng ("Picheng") made ceramic movable type in the 1040s, which is correct. But no Chinese printer used 400,000 characters. The largest classical dictionary, the Kangxi Dictionary of 1716, has about 47,000, and a working font needed a few thousand characters (with many copies of each). Wang Zhen's wooden type (about 1300) had around 60,000 pieces. "400,000" is probably a dub or transcription slip for 40,000.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "They were rifles, and mortars, and cannons, incendiary missiles, even flamethrowers."
  **Today:** China did invent gunpowder weapons: fire lances, bombs, rockets, hand cannons and cannons. Rifling (spiral grooves in the barrel) was a European invention (Germany, late 15th century), so "rifles" is wrong. The Chinese pump flamethrower (919 AD) burned petroleum oil bought from the Arab world, and the Byzantines had been spraying "Greek fire" from siphons since the 670s.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Porcelain about 1,700 years before Europe: true porcelain dates from the Eastern Han (around the 2nd century AD) and Meissen from 1708–10. Roughly 1,500–1,600 years.
- Playing cards (Tang dynasty, 9th century) came about five centuries before Europe (1370s).
- Hand cannons appeared in 13th-century China (the Heilongjiang gun, about 1288). Calling them pistols is loose, but China came first.
- Decimal place value with counting rods, matches (sulphur-tipped sticks, 577 AD), the compass, the escapement-driven astronomical clock (Yi Xing 725, Su Song 1088), rockets, chain pumps ("conveyor belt"), deep drilling for brine and gas ("petroleum") and woodblock printing are all genuine Chinese firsts.
- Row cultivation (6th century BC), iron ploughs and the multi-tube seed drill (2nd century BC; misheard as "multiple row sewing machine") came long before Europe's seed drills (16th–18th century).
- The breast-strap and collar harnesses appeared in China about 1,000 years before Europe, setting aside the "ten times the load" claim.
- The magic lantern as a Chinese forerunner of cinema is a stretch (the Chinese "trotting horse lamp" was a rotating shadow lamp). The European magic lantern dates from the 1650s.
- The pound lock (Qiao Weiyue, 984), paddle-wheel boats and the sternpost rudder (1st century AD, Europe about 1180) are fair Chinese firsts.
- Biological pest control with citrus ants is recorded in 304 AD, about 16 centuries before the West.
- The monk Huaibing raised sunken iron oxen with boats as floats in the 11th century.
- Baden Baden-Powell was lifted by a kite in 1894, and Otto Lilienthal's gliders opened the way to powered flight.
- Kites date to about the 5th–4th century BC, and kites were flown for help during the siege of Nanjing (549).
- Emperor Gao Yang (Wenxuan of Northern Qi) launched prisoners on kites in 559. Yuan Huangtou flew about 2.5 km and survived, around 1,335 years before Baden-Powell.
- Bamboo-cable suspension bridges in Sichuan (the Anlan bridge, misheard as "mainland bridge") are traditionally dated to the 3rd century BC, though the date is uncertain. China's iron-chain bridges came about 1,500 years before Europe's (1801).
- The show rightly corrects the popular idea that Leonardo invented the parachute.
- Writing appeared on Sumerian clay tablets, though about 5,200 years ago (Uruk, around 3200 BC) rather than 6,000. Egyptian writing came at roughly the same time.
- Paper dates to the 2nd century BC from archaeology (Fangmatan, Baqiao), well before the traditional Cai Lun date of 105 AD. The show is ahead of most popular books here.
- Paper armour, paper money (Sichuan *jiaozi*, early 11th century), death for counterfeiting (printed on Yuan notes) and Europe's first banknotes (Stockholm, 1661) are all correct.
- The Diamond Sutra (868) is the oldest dated printed book. Undated earlier prints exist (Korea and Japan, 8th century).
- The Mongols reached Silesia and Hungary in 1241, and Gutenberg's press followed about two centuries later. Any transmission of printing from China to Europe is unproven.
- Gunpowder came from Daoist alchemists searching for an elixir (9th century), and rockets reached Europe by the 13th century.
