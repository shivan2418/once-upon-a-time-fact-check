# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e23: Simón Bolívar
The episode tells Bolívar's life as a flashback from his death at Santa Marta in 1830: his education under Simón Rodríguez, his brief marriage and his time in Paris, and the first Venezuelan republic under Miranda and its collapse. It then follows the Admirable Campaign, the war against Boves, help from Haiti, the Congress of Angostura and the crossing of the Andes. It ends with the founding of Gran Colombia and Bolivia, the meeting with San Martín, the attempt on his life and his bitter end as the union breaks apart.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Apparently I'm not needed. I relinquish all this power that you so covet."
  **Today:** The episode shows Bolívar as the only selfless man among greedy rivals. In fact he wanted power too. He wrote the 1826 Bolivian constitution with a president for life, and he ruled Gran Colombia as dictator from August 1828 under his Organic Decree. Fear of that dictatorship, and of rumoured plans for a monarchy, drove much of the opposition, including the September 1828 plot. Historians since Salvador de Madariaga (1951) and John Lynch (*Simón Bolívar: A Life*, 2006) describe a great but authoritarian leader.
  **Why it was believed:** An official cult of Bolívar in Venezuela and Colombia shaped schoolbooks and popular biographies.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The traitor who organized this plot, General Santander, is condemned to death."
  **Today:** Santander was condemned to death after the attempt of 25 September 1828, and Bolívar commuted the sentence to exile, as the episode shows. But Santander's role is disputed. He knew a conspiracy was being planned and did not report it. Whether he organised it was never proven, and the trial was hasty and political.
  **Why it was believed:** The Bolivarian version of events, and the verdict itself, made Santander the villain in popular accounts.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "Wellington inflicted a stinging defeat on the French at Waterloo,"
  **Today:** The scene is set in 1813–14, after Bolívar entered Caracas (August 1813). Waterloo was in June 1815. The defeat that drove the French out of Spain was **Vitoria** (21 June 1813), and Ferdinand VII returned to Spain in March 1814.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "and creoles, whose frustrations and misery he knew how to play on."
  **Today:** Boves's llaneros ("Leoneros" is a mishearing) were mostly pardos (people of mixed African, European and Indigenous descent), free Black people and enslaved people, with some Indigenous people. "Creoles" (criollos) meant American-born whites, the class Boves turned his followers against, as his own line "kill the whites" shows. This may be a translation slip.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The Congress declares unanimously the Republic of Venezuela free and independent."
  **Today:** The vote of 5 July 1811 was not unanimous. Most accounts give 40 deputies for. At least one, the priest Manuel Vicente Maya, voted against, and some counts give more dissenters.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "When the man who wanted to unite all of Latin America died at 47, the region had no fewer than 16 countries."
  **Today:** The age is right: born 24 July 1783, died 17 December 1830. But in 1830 Latin America had about 13 independent states: Mexico, the Central American Federation, Venezuela, New Granada, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, the United Provinces (Argentina), Paraguay, Uruguay, Brazil and Haiti. Sixteen or more came only after the Central American Federation broke up (1838–41) and the Dominican Republic was founded (1844).
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- His death at Santa Marta (1830). Simón Rodríguez as his tutor (1790s) teaching Rousseau-style ideas of liberty and against slavery.
- Marriage to María Teresa del Toro (Madrid, 1802), and her death from fever (yellow fever, January 1803). She was not his "only great love": Manuela Sáenz, met in Quito in 1822, also appears in the episode.
- Paris in 1804, meeting Rodríguez again and disillusion with Napoleon's coronation. Their oath on Monte Sacro in Rome (1805) is left out.
- 1810: Joseph Bonaparte king in Madrid, the Caracas junta (19 April), and Bolívar leading the mission to London (with López Méndez and Andrés Bello), where he persuaded Miranda to return.
- Miranda as a Spanish officer, a Russian colonel by Catherine II's favour and a general of the French Revolution. His capitulation to Monteverde (July 1812) and death in a Spanish prison (Cádiz, 1816). The episode leaves out that Bolívar was among the officers who arrested Miranda at La Guaira and left him to the Spaniards.
- The Magdalena campaign from Barrancas (Cartagena), Tenerife, Cúcuta ("Guenta" is a mishearing), Mérida, and the entry into Caracas on 6 August 1813. The Admirable Campaign from Cúcuta covered about 1,500 km, so "over 2,000 kilometers" counts generously from Barrancas.
- "Merciless war": the War to the Death decree was issued at Trujillo on 15 June 1813, before Caracas, not after. To its credit, the episode shows Bolívar ordering prisoners killed. About 800–1,000 Spanish and Canarian prisoners were executed at La Guaira and Caracas in February 1814.
- Bolívar's speech of January 1814 handing power to the people, and being kept as dictator (he was proclaimed Liberator in October 1813).
- San Mateo (February–March 1814), then disaster against Boves at La Puerta (June 1814).
- To its credit, the episode shows Haitian President Pétion's aid (1815–16) given on condition that Bolívar abolish slavery. Bolívar did decree emancipation in 1816, but in practice Venezuela fully abolished slavery only in 1854.
- Páez winning over Boves's former llaneros, and the cavalry seizing Spanish gunboats on the Apure (1818).
- The Congress of Angostura (February 1819). The speech's themes (popular sovereignty, separation of powers, abolition of slavery, the people held under "ignorance and tyranny") paraphrase the real address.
- The crossing of the Andes in the rainy season (1819), Santander's rendezvous at Tame ("Tamay"), Páez's diversion on the Apure, Tunja and Bogotá, and the villagers clothing the ragged army (the Socha tradition). The decisive battle of Boyacá (7 August 1819) is not named.
- The Republic of Colombia (17 December 1819) at first united Venezuela and New Granada. Quito joined only in 1822, after Sucre's victory at Pichincha.
- The Guayaquil meeting with San Martín (July 1822): no record was kept, so the dialogue is imagined. San Martín was "Protector" of Peru and the liberator of Argentina, Chile and Peru, not of South America as a whole.
- Junín and Ayacucho (1824), with Ayacucho won by Sucre. The founding of Bolivia (1825), with Sucre its first constitutional president (Bolívar held the office briefly first). Rodríguez as director of public education, with co-educational schools, before he left after clashing with officials.
- Congress withdrawing Bolívar's command in 1824 under Santander's influence, the attempt on his life on 25 September 1828, Sucre's murder (4 June 1830) and "plowing the sea" (a letter to Flores, November 1830) are all accurate.
- "In the days of Pericles, the ancient Greeks said, man is the measure of all things": the saying is Protagoras's, who lived in Pericles's day, so the line is fair.
