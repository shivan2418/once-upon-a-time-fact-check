# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e13: The Age of the Conquistadores
The episode surveys the Spanish expeditions into what is now the southern United States: Balboa reaching the Pacific (1513), Ponce de León naming Florida, Narváez's disastrous 1528 expedition and Cabeza de Vaca's eight-year odyssey, Hernando de Soto's march to the Mississippi (1539–42) and Coronado's search for the Seven Cities of Cíbola and Quivira (1540–42). It ends with a roll-call of the conquistadors' violent or miserable deaths. It is sympathetic to Cabeza de Vaca and clear-eyed about why Native peoples resisted.

## Outdated (since 1991)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Well, he believed there was a fountain of youth, and he even became governor of Bimini for that."
  **Today:** Ponce de León's royal contracts (1512–14) for Bimini and Florida talk about land, gold and slaves, and never mention a fountain. The fountain story was first attached to him after his death, mockingly by Oviedo (1535), then by Fontaneda and Herrera (1601). Historians now treat the "quest for the Fountain of Youth" as a legend (see, e.g., J. Michael Francis's work, 2010s).
  **Why it was believed:** Herrera's widely read history and 19th-century American textbooks made the fountain the standard story of Florida's discovery.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "Imagine, he was 53."
  **Today:** This uses the traditional birth year of 1460. Most modern biographers prefer about 1474, based on his own testimony, which would make him about 39 in 1513. The exact year is uncertain.
  **Why it was believed:** The 1460 date appeared in older reference works.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "who was already abandoned by everybody."
  **Today:** Columbus died in Valladolid in 1506, ill and bitter about his lost titles, but not poor or alone. He had a large income from his share of the Indies trade, his sons were at court, and his son Diego became governor of Hispaniola in 1509.
  **Why it was believed:** Romantic biographies, especially Washington Irving's (1828), made the forgotten, poverty-stricken Columbus a set piece.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "before whose armies enemy forces parted, as the Red Sea before Moses."
  **Today:** Cortés and Pizarro did not succeed by miracle. They had tens of thousands of Native allies (Tlaxcalans and others in Mexico; Cañari, Chachapoya and Huáscar's supporters in Peru), and epidemics and civil wars had already weakened both empires. The show's own Episodes 10 and 11 partly show this.
  **Why it was believed:** The 19th-century Prescott story of tiny bands of heroes was still the popular version in 1991 (see Restall, *Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest*, 2003).
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "who after a troublesome voyage caused by a false maneuver"
  **Today:** Balboa reached the Pacific on an overland march across the Isthmus of Panama in September 1513, guided by Native people. He first saw the ocean from a ridge on 25 September. No sea voyage or mistake by Pizarro (who was one of his men) was involved.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "He was an old soldier who had been companion to Columbus and to Cortez."
  **Today:** Ponce de León sailed on Columbus's second voyage (1493), but he never served with Cortés. Cortés's conquest of Mexico (1519–21) came after Ponce had already explored Florida.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "My forefathers earned it by charging the enemy with bullhorns having no weapons."
  **Today:** According to the family legend, an ancestor, the shepherd Martín Alhaja, marked a hidden mountain pass with a cow's skull before the Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa (1212). That let the Christian army surprise the Almohads. The show substitutes a different (invented) legend.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "And suddenly, they reached their objective, Tampa Bay in Florida, the very spot where Ponce de Leon had been mortally wounded."
  **Today:** Narváez's grant covered the coast from Florida to the Río de las Palmas in Mexico, and his pilots thought they were near the Pánuco. He landed near Tampa Bay in April 1528 more or less by accident. Ponce de León was wounded in 1521 further south, probably among the Calusa around Charlotte Harbor or the Caloosahatchee River.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "they cross into Alabama and then into Mississippi and Louisiana,"
  **Today:** Narváez's men went along the Gulf Coast past Alabama, Mississippi and Louisiana in makeshift boats (late 1528) and were wrecked on the Texas coast. Cabeza de Vaca's walk began in Texas. From there he and three companions (Dorantes, Castillo and the enslaved Moroccan Estebanico, all left out) crossed Texas and northern Mexico, reaching Spanish slavers in Sinaloa in 1536.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "who himself is stuck in alabama while waiting he raises an even bigger army"
  **Today:** Mendoza did not wait for de Soto. Coronado's expedition left in February 1540, at the same time as de Soto was marching through the Southeast. It was sparked by Fray Marcos de Niza's 1539 report and Estebanico's death at Zuni. Its 1540 muster lists about 290 Europeans (226 horsemen, 63 foot), with about 80 more joining later, plus about 1,300 Native allies from Mexico. It did not have the "1,000 infantry soldiers" the show gives.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Balboa as Pedrarias's son-in-law (by proxy betrothal to his daughter María) and his beheading at Acla in 1519, after Pizarro arrested him.
- The legends that drew explorers (one-legged men, giants, El Dorado's "golden man", Columbus's "mermaids", which were manatees). The show presents these as beliefs, not facts.
- Florida named for Pascua Florida (Easter 1513); the Gulf Stream noted on that voyage (by his pilot Antón de Alaminos); Ponce's fatal arrow wound in 1521 and death in Cuba.
- "the first European to set foot on the future USA": the first documented one on the mainland. Columbus had landed on Puerto Rico, now a US territory, in 1493, and unrecorded slaving voyages may have reached Florida before 1513.
- Narváez (Cortés's rival from Episode 10) as governor; desertions at Santo Domingo (about 140); hurricane losses at Trinidad, Cuba; the pilot Miruelo; 300 men marching inland to Apalachee; horsehide boats; Narváez lost at sea in November 1528. The women left on the ships agreeing to take new husbands comes straight from Cabeza de Vaca's own *Relación*.
- Cabeza de Vaca as treasurer, the survivors working as healers, and the eight-year journey (1528–36). Later, as governor of Río de la Plata, he was deposed and sent to Spain in chains (1544–45), partly because he restrained abuses of the Guaraní.
- De Soto: his horsemanship display before Atahualpa; about 620 men and 220 horses (plus pigs) landing near Tampa Bay in 1539; Juan Ortiz, a Narváez-era captive (he had come with a search party) after about 11 years; Maldonado's ships waiting at Ochuse (Pensacola Bay) in 1540; the battle of Mabila, where Native deaths far exceeded the 20-odd Spaniards killed; de Soto's death in 1542 and burial in the Mississippi.
- The show's explanation of Native "unpredictability" ("they will attack us if we try to steal from them or take them as slaves") is fair and fits the sources.
- Coronado: Cíbola (the Zuni pueblos), the guide "El Turco" leading them to Quivira (Kansas) and being garrotted, copper rather than gold.
- Later explorers: Cabrillo, Unamuno, Vizcaíno, Oñate, Menéndez de Avilés (the names are mangled in the transcript).
- The fates: Pizarro murdered by Almagro's followers (1541), Juan Pizarro killed by a stone at Sacsayhuamán (1536), Gonzalo beheaded (1548), Almagro garrotted (1538) and his son executed in Cusco (1542), Narváez lost at sea, de Soto dead of fever. Alvarado was crushed when a horse fell on him in a slope accident during the Mixtón War (1541), rather than riding "off a precipice". Hernando Pizarro, who lived to a great age, does not fit the "curse".
