# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e17: The Indians in the 18th Century
In the early 1700s a Plains band that still hunts on foot with dogs first meets mounted Apaches. The band's boys go through vision quests, and the band then captures and trades for horses, which changes buffalo hunting and travel. The episode closes on rifles, whiskey and European diseases reaching the Great Plains as settlers push Native peoples ever farther west. ## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "I want you to kill the number we need, no more."
  **Today:** Communal hunts were tightly controlled by hunt police (akicita among the Lakota). But the idea that Plains peoples always took "only what they needed" has since been challenged. Shepard Krech's *The Ecological Indian* (1999), Dan Flores (1991) and Andrew Isenberg (*The Destruction of the Bison*, 2000) document buffalo jumps that killed more animals than could be used, and Native market hunting for the hide and robe trade in the 1800s. Historians agree the main cause of the bison's collapse was non-Native commercial hunting, but the "ecological Indian" image is now seen as simplified.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Another one if you kill an enemy, and especially one if you scalped one."
  **Today:** Among Plains peoples the highest war honour was usually counting coup: touching or striking a living enemy, or taking his weapon or horse. That outranked killing him. Scalping carried relatively little prestige in most coup systems, so the show reverses the order. Eagle-feather awards did vary from nation to nation.
  **Why it was believed:** Western films and pulp fiction made scalping the defining act of "Indian warfare".
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Horses reaching the northern and central Plains in the early-to-mid 1700s (Shoshone around 1700, Blackfoot around 1730, Lakota around 1750), spreading north from Spanish New Mexico after the 1680 Pueblo Revolt. The Apaches were mounted earlier, so the opening encounter is plausible. The show does not project the horse culture back into earlier times, which is better than most popular portrayals.
- The "big dog" name echoes real names for the horse: Lakota *šuŋkawakaŋ* ("sacred/mysterious dog") and Blackfoot "elk-dog". Before horses, dogs pulled the travois.
- Lakota, Cheyenne and others moving west onto the Plains in the 1700s, pushed by better-armed neighbours (Ojibwe, Cree) and the pressure of the fur trade. The Comanche arrived on the southern Plains around 1700 and fought the Spanish through much of the century (peace in 1786).
- Communal buffalo hunts under a leader's discipline, with no hunting for individual glory; riding up close to kill with arrows; wounded bulls goring horses.
- Vision quests with fasting and sweat lodges in search of the "great mysterious" power (Lakota *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka*); medicine bundles and charms; eagle feathers earned in war.
- The chief who is also shaman and healer is played for comedy. Political and spiritual leadership were often separate roles, though some leaders held both.
- Guns, whiskey and epidemics (such as the 1780–82 smallpox epidemic on the Plains) reaching the Plains peoples ahead of white settlement.
