# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e26: Towards the 20th Century
The episode follows an escaped slave, Nat, who finds refuge in Pennsylvania and then fights in the Civil War. Around his story it covers Lincoln's election, secession, Fort Sumter, the Monitor and the Merrimack, Appomattox, Lincoln's assassination and the rise of the Ku Klux Klan. It then surveys late-19th-century America: mass immigration, the Homestead Act, railroad land grants, robber barons, child labour, unions, imperialism, inventions, and a closing tribute to the United States as a "beacon for brotherhood".

## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "There were 600,000 dead, 400,000 wounded."
  **Today:** The long-standing figure of about 620,000 deaths comes from Fox and Livermore's 19th-century counts. In 2011 J. David Hacker used census data to estimate about 750,000 deaths (range 620,000–850,000), and this is now widely accepted. Wounded are usually put at about 475,000 or more.
  **Confidence:** high

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "No, they were just too far outnumbered."
  **Today:** This is the "Lost Cause" explanation: a flawless Lee beaten only by numbers. Historians point to Lee's own costly choices (Malvern Hill, Pickett's Charge), the Confederacy's strategic and economic weaknesses, desertion, enslaved people fleeing to Union lines, and Union generalship by 1864–65. Numbers mattered, but they were not the only reason.
  **Why it was believed:** Lost Cause writers (Jubal Early and others) built Lee's image after 1865, and it ruled popular history for a century. Critiques such as Connelly's *The Marble Man* (1977) and Nolan's *Lee Considered* (1991) were only beginning to spread.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Yes, and I tell you that the institution of slavery is evil in itself,"
  **Today:** Lincoln did call slavery a "monstrous injustice", but in 1858–60 his platform was to stop slavery from **spreading**, not to abolish it in the states where it existed. In the 1858 debates he also said he did not favour political or social equality between the races. The scene, and the line "Our Constitution guarantees liberty and justice for all", makes him sound like a straightforward abolitionist. ("Liberty and justice for all" comes from the 1892 Pledge of Allegiance, not the Constitution, which then protected slavery.)
  **Why it was believed:** The "Great Emancipator" image of Lincoln dominated school and popular history.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "This is an anti-slave state."
  **Today:** Being in a free state did not protect a fugitive. Under the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 (and *Prigg v. Pennsylvania*, 1842), slaveholders could recapture escaped people anywhere in the North with federal help, and anyone who hid them could be fined and jailed. That is why the Underground Railroad led on to Canada. Pennsylvanians who resisted, as at Christiana in 1851, were prosecuted for treason. The homeowner's defiance is admirable, but it had no legal force.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "A few weeks later, all the other slave states followed except for Kentucky, Missouri, and Maryland."
  **Today:** Six more Deep South states seceded by 1 February 1861. Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee and North Carolina left only after Fort Sumter and Lincoln's call for troops (April–May 1861). Delaware, a fourth slave state, also stayed in the Union, as did West Virginia, which split from Virginia.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Then I will enlist, too. I will fight to defend our liberty."
  **Today:** Black men were turned away from the Union Army in 1861. They could enlist only from the second half of 1862, and in large numbers only after the Emancipation Proclamation (1 January 1863), in segregated United States Colored Troops regiments under white officers. A formerly enslaved sergeant commanding white soldiers ("It's the Negro slave commanding us now") would not have happened. Black soldiers also got lower pay until 1864.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Forces may retain their weapons and all men keep their horses."
  **Today:** At Appomattox (9 April 1865) Confederate soldiers stacked and surrendered their arms. Only officers kept their side-arms, and men who owned their horses or mules could take them home. The generous spirit of the terms is shown correctly.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "At the end of this 19th century, railroad tracks would stretch across the continent 400,000 kilometers."
  **Today:** In 1900 the US had about 193,000 miles (about 310,000 km) of track. The network peaked at about 254,000 miles (about 409,000 km) in 1916.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "But that was child's play compared to the 6,000 hectares granted to the railroads for every kilometer of rail lane."
  **Today:** The Pacific Railway Acts gave 10 sections per mile of track (1862), doubled to 20 in 1864: about 3,200 ha per km. Only the Northern Pacific, in the territories, got 40 sections per mile (about 6,400 ha per km). The total of about 53 million ha in federal grants, "the size of France", is about right.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "One percent of the population would hold seven-eighths of the country's wealth."
  **Today:** The famous figure comes from Charles B. Spahr (1896), who said that 1% of families held **more than half** the wealth, while seven-eighths of families held only one-eighth. Modern estimates (Lindert and others) put the top 1%'s share at roughly 25–45% around 1890–1910. The inequality was huge, but not 7/8.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Thomas Edison was to change everyday life for most people, inventing the"
  **Today:** The list continues "telegraph, the phonograph, the incandescent light bulb, movies". Edison did not invent the telegraph. Morse and Vail (1837–44), and Cooke and Wheatstone in Britain, did. Edison improved it (for example the quadruplex, 1874). He invented the phonograph (1877). He developed a practical incandescent bulb (1879) at the same time as Joseph Swan. Motion pictures came from his lab (Dickson's Kinetograph and Kinetoscope, 1891–93) alongside the Lumières and others.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Opened in 1813 the Brooklyn Bridge, the massive suspension bridge that stayed up."
  **Today:** The Brooklyn Bridge opened on 24 May 1883. This may be a speech-recognition error.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "And New York is already the biggest city in the world."
  **Today:** Around 1900, London (about 6.5 million in Greater London) was clearly the world's largest city. New York, even after it merged with Brooklyn in 1898, had about 3.4 million. New York only overtook London around 1925.
  **Confidence:** high

## Simplified but fine
- 1860: 33 states (Kansas became the 34th in January 1861) and about 31 million people. The 15 slave states held about 12 million, nearly 4 million of them enslaved. The 11 Confederate states had about 9 million, 3.5 million of them enslaved. The North's 22 million also included about 250,000 free Black people.
- A young enslaved man valued at about $1,000; escaped slaves heading north to Pennsylvania.
- Lincoln elected on 6 November 1860 and inaugurated in March 1861. The "house divided" (1858) is paraphrased loosely, and nearly reverses Lincoln's "it will become all one thing or all the other".
- Fort Sumter: the scene matches history. Roger Pryor declined to fire the first shot and Captain George S. James fired the signal gun (Pryor was a former congressman and volunteer aide, not a lieutenant). It also avoids the popular Edmund Ruffin legend.
- 75,000 volunteers and the blockade (April 1861). Monitor against Merrimack (CSS Virginia) on 9 March 1862, the first battle between ironclads, a draw. Washington feared the ship would shell the capital. Franklin Buchanan commanded the Virginia on 8 March but was wounded before the duel with the Monitor.
- Lincoln assassinated by Booth (April 1865).
- The Ku Klux Klan (1865–66) with ranks such as Grand Wizard, Grand Dragon and Grand Titan ("great sage" is probably a mishearing of Grand Cyclops). The Enforcement Acts of 1870–71 did act against it, and Grant prosecuted the Klan in South Carolina in 1871. "As a rule, they turned a blind eye" is fairer for the years after 1873. The episode also leaves out Reconstruction, the 13th–15th Amendments, Black officeholders and Jim Crow.
- Alaska bought in 1867; Gilded Age scandals; the population rising from about 5.3 million (1800) to 76 million (1900).
- Homestead Act: 160 acres (about 65 ha), proved up after 5 years; speculators and railroads took much of the best land. Immigrants from Poland, Ireland, Russia and Sweden. New York's arrival depot was Castle Garden until 1890 and Ellis Island from 1892.
- Indian treaties broken for the railroads. The Statue of Liberty dedicated in 1886. Vanderbilt, Gould ("Ghouls" in the transcript), Carnegie, Rockefeller and J.P. Morgan as "robber barons".
- Child labour: the 1900 census counted about 1.75 million children aged 10–15 working. About a fifth of women were in paid work. Unions, labour violence and falling farm prices.
- Imperialism in Hawaii and Cuba (1898). The Philippines are not mentioned.
- Bell's telephone (1876); Chicago's first skyscraper (Home Insurance Building, 1885); the Wright brothers (1903).
- Henry Ford's assembly line (1913) is put before Theodore Roosevelt, who became president in 1901 when McKinley was killed and was first elected in 1904.
- "Canada and Mexico, which would never pose a genuine threat": an opinion. It skips the War of 1812 (British Canada) and the US–Mexican War, though in both the United States was largely the aggressor.
