# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e21: Ebony Wood
The episode follows the Atlantic slave trade ("ebony wood" was the traders' euphemism for captives) from an African kingdom where a king's raiders capture whole villages and his trade minister bargains with a European captain, through the Middle Passage, to a slave auction and plantation in the Americas. A family plans to escape north, and the episode closes with the abolition of the trade and an estimate of how many Africans were deported.

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "She's with Mokhtar in a beautiful coffee plantation."
  **Today:** The escape plan ("up north, in those states, there's no slaves") puts the story in the United States, where coffee was never a plantation crop. US plantations grew tobacco, rice, sugar and, after 1793, cotton. Coffee plantations worked by enslaved people were in Saint-Domingue, Cuba, Jamaica and above all Brazil. (The setting is only implied, so this may be a deliberate composite.)
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "You will purchase all you've got of rubber, an ivory, pepper,"
  **Today:** Ivory and pepper (melegueta, the "grains of paradise" of the Grain Coast) were real West African exports. Rubber became a significant African export only in the late 19th century. The French original probably said *gomme* (gum arabic, a major Senegal export), which the dub translated as "rubber".
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- African rulers raiding to capture and sell people, and trading with Portuguese, English, French and Dutch ships, is accurate. "Great Yavogan" is the *yovogan* ("chief of the white men"), Dahomey's official who handled European trade at Ouidah. The episode shows African participation without excusing the European buyers, which is more honest than many 1990s accounts.
- The goods traded (brandy, cloth, gunpowder, guns) are right. "Strings of pearls" is a literal translation of French *perles*, which here means glass beads.
- Ships coasting along Africa until full, shipboard revolts (about one voyage in ten saw one, and most failed), captives forced to "dance" on deck for exercise, and bodies oiled before sale are all accurate.
- The auction in lots, children sold away from their mothers, flogging, and slave drivers chosen from among the enslaved are accurate. Lines such as "only Africans can work well in this hot climate" are spoken by slavers, not the narrator.
- "now it's illegal to import slaves" fits the US ban of 1808, while the domestic trade carried on. The northern states had abolished or were gradually ending slavery (Vermont 1777 to New Jersey's gradual law of 1804). The episode rightly says that escaping north did not end the danger (Fugitive Slave Acts of 1793 and 1850).
- The French continued trading illegally after the 1815–18 bans, until the law of 1831 and the 1831/1833 right-of-search treaties with Britain. Brazil (1850) and Cuba (1860s) went on longer. US slavery ended with the 13th Amendment (December 1865).
- "10 to 14 million people were taken from Africa": this matches today's Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (Eltis & Richardson, 1999/2008), which counts about 12.5 million embarked and 10.7 million landed. It is better than both the low and the inflated popular figures of 1991. The episode doesn't say that only about 400,000 of them went to mainland North America, while most went to Brazil and the Caribbean.
