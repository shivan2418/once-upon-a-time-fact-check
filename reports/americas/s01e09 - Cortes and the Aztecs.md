# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e09: Cortes and the Aztecs
The episode opens with a quick run through Magellan's voyage (1519–1522), then moves to Cuba in 1518, where Governor Velázquez hands Hernán Cortés an expedition that Cortés takes off without permission. It follows Cortés along the Gulf coast: he picks up Aguilar and Malinche (Doña Marina), wins over the Totonacs of Cempoala, scuttles his ships, fights and then allies with Tlaxcala, sacks Cholula and reaches the Valley of Mexico. It is told largely through Bernal Díaz, and it rightly shows that the Aztec empire was riven by subject peoples who sided with the Spaniards, but it repeats the "white god" legend.

## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "Just as the prediction had predicted the great white god, Quetzalcoatl."
  **Today:** The story that Moctezuma and the Aztecs expected a returning pale, bearded Quetzalcoatl and took Cortés for him is now widely seen as a post-conquest invention. It does not appear in Cortés's letters. It took shape decades later in Franciscan and Nahua writings that tried to explain the defeat after the fact. Susan Gillespie (*The Aztec Kings*, 1989) first argued this. Camilla Townsend ("Burying the White Gods", *American Historical Review*, 2003) and Matthew Restall (*Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest*, 2003) made it the mainstream view. Around 1991 most narrative histories still told the story; Hugh Thomas's *Conquest* (1993) is one example. No pre-conquest image shows Quetzalcoatl as white.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "he was facing a great empire waiting for a god"
  **Today:** This rests on the same post-conquest legend (see above). Moctezuma's gifts and his hesitation are better read as normal Mesoamerican diplomacy toward a powerful, unknown force (Restall, *When Montezuma Met Cortés*, 2018). They were not religious paralysis.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "He says you may well be white god."
  **Today:** The "white god" idea comes from the same post-conquest legend. The Tlaxcalans allied with Cortés for practical reasons: they had lost the battles, and they wanted to use the Spaniards against their Aztec enemies. Spanish and Tlaxcalan sources describe it this way, and so do Ross Hassig (*Mexico and the Spanish Conquest*, 1994) and Restall (2003).
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "With an audacity unparalleled in history, Captain Cortez burnt his boats behind him."
  **Today:** Cortés had his ships stripped of their gear and run aground or scuttled (holed and sunk) at Villa Rica in 1519. He did not burn them. Bernal Díaz and Cortés's own letter both say this, and the show's own dialogue just before says "all our ships be sunk".
  **Why it was believed:** "Burning his boats" became a proverb. Later chroniclers such as Cervantes de Salazar told it that way, and popular histories repeated it.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Everything seemed peaceful, but Cortez had been warned they were plotting a massacre and ordered the attack."
  **Today:** The claim of a Cholulan plot comes only from the Spaniards and their Tlaxcalan allies, who were enemies of Cholula. Many historians doubt it or think the Tlaxcalans stirred it up. The "attack" was a massacre of thousands of mostly unarmed Cholulans gathered in the temple courtyard (estimates run from about 3,000 to 6,000) in October 1519. The episode gives the Spanish justification and leaves out who died.
  **Why it was believed:** For centuries histories relied on Cortés's and Bernal Díaz's accounts, which presented the massacre as a pre-emptive strike.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "Yes, but I'm afraid I'm the only survivor."
  **Today:** Gerónimo de Aguilar was one of two survivors of the 1511 shipwreck who were still alive in 1519. The other, Gonzalo Guerrero, had married into Maya society and refused to rejoin the Spaniards. Bernal Díaz tells this story in detail.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The city of Cholula was on their route with its 400 temples and pyramids higher than those in Egypt."
  **Today:** The "400 temples" matches Cortés's report of about 430 towers. But the Great Pyramid of Cholula (Tlachihualtepetl) is about 55–66 m high, against about 146 m for the Great Pyramid of Giza. Cholula's is the largest pyramid in the world **by volume**, not by height.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "sandoval you will stay here with a hundred men"
  **Today:** When Cortés marched inland in August 1519, he left **Juan de Escalante** in command at Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz, with roughly 150 men. Gonzalo de Sandoval replaced him only after Escalante was killed later that year.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "You're a cultured man. You will tell the story of our adventures."
  **Today:** Nobody commissioned Bernal Díaz del Castillo to write the expedition's history. He was an ordinary soldier who wrote his *Historia verdadera* decades later (finished around 1568), partly to answer Cortés's official chronicler, López de Gómara. He called himself an "idiota sin letras" (an unlearned man). Cortés wrote his own account in his letters to Charles V.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "1,500 meters. I want volunteers to climb it."
  **Today:** Diego de Ordaz did climb the volcano Popocatépetl in 1519, but it stands about 5,400 m high, roughly 3,000 m above the valley floor. The figure may be a mishearing.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "September 10th, 1519, we set our course, heading for, uh..."
  **Today:** The fleet left Seville on 10 **August** 1519 and put to sea from Sanlúcar de Barrameda on 20 September 1519.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "2nd of November, the fleet came in sight of the northeastern coast of Brazil."
  **Today:** The fleet reached Cabo de Santo Agostinho (near Recife) on 29 November 1519 and entered the bay of Rio de Janeiro on 13 December. Whisper may have garbled the date.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- "It's agreed that the world is round": the show rightly avoids the myth that Magellan or Columbus had to prove the earth was not flat. Educated Europeans had accepted a round earth since antiquity.
- Magellan as a Portuguese navigator serving Charles V. In 1519 Charles was King Charles I of Spain and was elected Emperor that June.
- The Río de la Plata mistaken for the passage (early 1520), the Easter mutiny at San Julián, the wreck of the *Santiago*, the strait found in October 1520 ("201520" is probably a garbled "21 October 1520") and the "Islands of Thieves" (Guam, March 1521).
- Magellan killed at Mactan (27 April 1521) while helping Rajah Humabon of Cebu, and Humabon's later massacre of about 27 officers and men.
- Elcano returning with 18 men out of about 265–270 men and five ships. The *Victoria* reached Sanlúcar on 6 September 1522, not the 4th.
- Santiago de Cuba in 1518 as the base for expeditions. Velázquez had sailed on Columbus's second voyage. "Criada" is probably a mishearing of Juan de Grijalva, his kinsman, whose 1518 expedition displeased him.
- Moctezuma could not be looked in the face, and envoys reported the earlier Spanish visits (Córdoba 1517, Grijalva 1518).
- Cortés slipping out of Santiago on 18 November 1518, with about 500 soldiers, about 100 sailors, about 200 Cuban islanders, 14 guns and 16 horses (Bernal Díaz's figures). He was 33 or 34.
- Aguilar wrecked in 1511 on a ship from Balboa's colony at Darién, then Maya-speaking Aguilar and the Nahuatl- and Maya-speaking Malinche forming the chain of interpreters. The episode says Malinche was given to Cortés as a slave after the Battle of Centla (Tabasco, March 1519), but its line about treating her "with respect" glosses over how she was handed between Spanish men.
- Moctezuma's gifts, the high value Mesoamericans placed on green stones (chalchihuitl), and the "cracks" in an empire of resentful tributaries. That emphasis on Indigenous allies is better than the old "handful of Spaniards" myth.
- The "Fat Cacique" of Cempoala, Totonac complaints about Aztec tribute in people for sacrifice, the arrest of Moctezuma's tax collectors and the smashing of Cempoala's idols all follow Bernal Díaz.
- Cortés hanging conspirators who wanted to return to Cuba (Escudero and Cermeño).
- Leaving the coast in August 1519 with about 400 Spaniards, 15–16 horses, a few guns and Totonac warriors and porters. "Hundreds of thousands" of Aztec warriors is a loose round figure.
- The Tlaxcala battles under Xicotencatl the Younger (the fighting lasted about two weeks, not four days), the Spaniards secretly burying their dead to seem immortal (as Bernal Díaz records), and the alliance that gave Cortés thousands of Tlaxcalan warriors. The Tlaxcalans were allies pursuing their own war, not simply troops under Cortés's command.
