# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e15: England and the Thirteen Colonies
The episode follows English colonisation of North America from Drake's 1579 claim and the lost Roanoke colony to the founding of Georgia in 1732. It covers Jamestown, tobacco, Pocahontas, the first Africans in Virginia, the Pilgrims and the first Thanksgiving, the Puritan Great Migration and its dissenters (Hooker, Williams, Hutchinson), New Amsterdam becoming New York, Carolina, Pennsylvania and Georgia.

## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "They had vanished."
  **Today:** The Roanoke colonists' fate is still unproven, but since the 1990s archaeology has strengthened the old theory that survivors moved in with Native neighbours. The Croatoan Archaeological Project on Hatteras Island (from 1998, mainly 2010s) has found late-16th-century English objects, and the First Colony Foundation's "Site X" work inland (from 2012) points to a possible move up the Albemarle Sound. The show's "vanished" framing reflects the older popular mystery.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "an expedition has just arrived, commanded by Sir Walter Raleigh."
  **Today:** Raleigh never went to North America. He sponsored the Roanoke voyages: the 1584 scouting trip under Amadas and Barlowe, the 1585 colony under Grenville and Lane, and the 1587 colony under John White. "Oak Island" is almost certainly a mishearing of Roanoke Island.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular history credited Raleigh as Virginia's "founder" because he held the royal patent.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Me, Pocahontas, your own daughter."
  **Today:** The rescue rests only on John Smith's own account, written in 1624 (his 1608 report does not mention it). Many historians doubt it or read it as an adoption ceremony that Smith misunderstood. Pocahontas was about 11 in December 1607. Smith was also captured by Opechancanough while exploring the Chickahominy River, not put on trial for cheating on land deals as the show has it.
  **Why it was believed:** The rescue was a fixture of American schoolbooks and folklore from the 19th century on.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Slaves arrive in Virginia. This was the beginning of slavery in America."
  **Today:** The "20 and odd" Africans who reached Point Comfort in August 1619 were the first recorded in English Virginia. Slavery in the Americas was a century older. Spain and Portugal had shipped enslaved Africans to the Caribbean and Brazil since the early 1500s, and Native Americans were enslaved from Columbus onward. Whether the 1619 Africans were treated as slaves or as indentured servants is still debated. Virginia's slave laws came later (1660s).
  **Why it was believed:** US history was told from the English colonies outward, which made 1619 "the start".
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "We might name it Thanksgiving Day."
  **Today:** The 1621 harvest feast was not called a "thanksgiving". To the Pilgrims a thanksgiving was a religious day of prayer. The only eyewitness (Edward Winslow) mentions fowl, five deer brought by about 90 Wampanoag under Massasoit, and recreation. Turkey is possible, but cranberry sauce is unlikely because there was no sugar. The show also leaves out the Wampanoag guests, who were the majority.
  **Why it was believed:** The turkey-and-cranberry Thanksgiving was created in the 19th century (Sarah Josepha Hale's campaign, Lincoln's 1863 proclamation) and projected back onto 1621.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Great Pilgrim's Festival lasted three days and now every November Americans celebrate Thanksgiving Day."
  **Today:** The feast did last about three days, but it was a one-off in autumn 1621 (probably late September to mid-November), not the start of an annual tradition. The national November holiday dates from 1863.
  **Why it was believed:** Same 19th-century origin myth as above.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "And that was that. The island of Manhattan was bought for $24 worth of junk jewelry."
  **Today:** The only contemporary source (Pieter Schaghen's 1626 letter) gives the price as trade goods worth 60 guilders and does not list beads. The "$24" is a conversion made in the 1840s and is worth about $1,000 or more today. The Lenape probably understood the deal as shared use, not an outright sale. The show gets credit for having the Native characters dispute who owns the land.
  **Why it was believed:** The $24 figure was a staple of schoolbooks.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "virginia dare the first child born in the new england colony"
  **Today:** Virginia Dare (born 18 August 1587) was the first English child born in the Americas, on Roanoke Island in what was then "Virginia" (now North Carolina), not New England. Her grandfather John White, the governor, was not a knight ("sir john white"). This may be a dub slip.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "colony mysteriously disappeared for 12 years they searched for survivors but in vain"
  **Today:** White left for supplies in 1587 and could not return until August 1590, three years later. He found the settlement abandoned and "CROATOAN" carved on a post. Raleigh sent searches in the 1600s and Jamestown made inquiries after 1607, but the "12 years" does not match either event.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "And 200 million pounds, a thousand times more, 50 years later."
  **Today:** A thousand times 20,000 lb is 20 million lb, which is about right for Chesapeake exports in the late 1660s–1670s. Exports never reached 200 million lb in the colonial period; they peaked around 100 million lb in the 1770s. The dub probably mistranslated "20 millions".
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Here in Chesapeake Bay, you found a splendid place to settle."
  **Today:** John Smith did not lead the founding of Jamestown in 1607. Christopher Newport commanded the voyage, Edward Maria Wingfield was the first council president, and Smith arrived under arrest. He became president only in September 1608. The site was a malarial, brackish swamp chosen for defence, and was a major reason so many died.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "30 pounds of tobacco surely isn't too high a price to pay for a charming girl."
  **Today:** This seems to refer to the Virginia Company's "tobacco brides" (1619–1621). A colonist who married one paid her passage, set at 120 lb of tobacco, later 150 lb. The women chose their husbands and were not sold at auction.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "That 147 of them died in this way."
  **Today:** About 189 Catholics were executed under Elizabeth I (roughly 130 priests and 60 lay people). Only a handful of them were Jesuits, the best known being Edmund Campion (1581). The figure and the "Jesuits" label do not match. The fine (£20 a month for not attending church) and the 1581 treason law for reconciling with Rome are correct.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Now, 20,000 pilgrims who disagreed with this,"
  **Today:** The Separatists who went to Holland (the Scrooby congregation, 1607–08) numbered a few hundred at most, and they left under James I, not Elizabeth. They were radical Protestants fleeing pressure from the Church of England, not people objecting to anti-Catholic laws. 20,000 is the size of the later Puritan Great Migration to New England (1630–1640), which the episode also cites correctly further on.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "First, our leader will sign Captain Bradford."
  **Today:** John Carver signed first and was the colony's first governor. William Bradford became governor in 1621 after Carver died, and he was never a captain.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Then, Captain Lionel Standish, you will be responsible for our safety."
  **Today:** The colony's military leader was Captain **Myles** Standish. This is probably a dub or transcription error.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "The Quakers are banned in Boston and leave the city."
  **Today:** The scene before this one is the Antinomian controversy of 1636–38 (Hutchinson, Wheelwright). Quakers first reached Boston in 1656. Massachusetts did more than ban them: it whipped and mutilated them, and hanged four between 1659 and 1661, among them Mary Dyer.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "To the north he founds New Hampshire."
  **Today:** John Wheelwright founded the town of Exeter (1638). New Hampshire grew out of the Mason and Gorges grants (1622, 1629), with settlements at Dover and Portsmouth from 1623, years before he arrived.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "And a group of 300 Catholics came and founded Maryland."
  **Today:** The Ark and the Dove brought about 140–150 settlers in 1634. The Calverts and the gentlemen leaders were Catholic, but most of the settlers, especially the servants, were Protestant. Maryland was meant as a refuge for Catholics, not a Catholic colony.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "May I just point out that we are at war, but surrendering will treat you well, well?"
  **Today:** England and the Dutch Republic were at peace when Richard Nicolls's fleet took New Amsterdam in August–September 1664. The Second Anglo-Dutch War was declared only in March 1665, and the seizure was one of its causes. The wet-powder joke stands in for a real shortage of powder. In reality the townspeople petitioned the stubborn Stuyvesant to surrender.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Then there will be land gravenes who will hold 15,000 hectares each."
  **Today:** The Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina (1669) gave each landgrave 48,000 acres (about 19,400 ha) and each cacique ("cassock") 24,000 acres (about 9,700 ha), not "a thousand". "Land gravenes"/"khakis" are mishearings of landgraves/caciques.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "My son William would like to go to America, sire."
  **Today:** Admiral Sir William Penn died in 1670. Charles II granted Pennsylvania in 1681 to William Penn the younger to pay off a debt (about £16,000) owed to his late father. The father could not have asked for it. Penn also wanted the name "Sylvania"; it was the king who added "Penn" to honour the admiral.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "The colony of Georgia in honor of our King George III!"
  **Today:** Georgia was chartered in 1732 and named after **George II**. George III became king in 1760. Oglethorpe was a colonel when he founded Savannah in 1733 and became a general later.
  **Confidence:** high

## Simplified but fine
- Drake claiming land for Elizabeth in 1579 ("Nova Albion", on the California coast). His account says he left a plate with a sixpence showing her portrait, so the "picture" joke is accurate.
- The tobacco boom: about 20,000 lb exported in 1618 and about 500,000 lb by the late 1620s. King James's 1604 *Counterblaste* attacked smoking in similar terms.
- "only 32 survive the first winter": about 38 of the 104 men were alive when supplies arrived in January 1608. Smith's 1608 "he that will not work shall not eat" rule is real.
- The Mayflower Compact wording and its "northern parts of Virginia" target; Cape Cod/Plymouth instead; about half the colonists dead by spring 1621.
- Squanto (Tisquantum) teaching the colonists to plant corn with fish. Whether fish fertiliser was a Native practice or one Squanto learned in Europe is debated (Ceci 1975).
- The Great Migration: about 20,000 colonists on about 200 ships (1630–1640).
- "Reverend Colton" is John Cotton, who did call democracy unfit "for church or commonwealth" (1636). Thomas Hooker's "foundation of authority is laid… in the free consent of the people" (1638); Hartford founded 1636.
- Roger Williams insisting that Native land be bought, his banishment, and Providence (1636). The show gives him fair credit here. Anne Hutchinson's trial (1637–38) and move to Rhode Island (she had 15 children; not all went with her).
- "Miles Dennis" is a garble of Myles Standish arresting Thomas Morton of Merrymount (1628), who was banished for selling guns to Native people and for his maypole revels.
- Peter Minuit as the colony's director (governor); the wall that gave Wall Street its name; Stuyvesant's surrender in 1664; New Jersey and Delaware coming with it.
- Anthony Ashley Cooper and John Locke drafting Carolina's aristocratic constitution, which gave masters "absolute power" over enslaved Africans.
- Penn's Frame of Government: religious toleration, and only murder and treason punished by death. The "perpetual brotherhood" treaty is the Shackamaxon treaty (1682–83), known from tradition rather than a surviving document.
- Georgia as a buffer against Spanish Florida, the last of the thirteen colonies (1732).
