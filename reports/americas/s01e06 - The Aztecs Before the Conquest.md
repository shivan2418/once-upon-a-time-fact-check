# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e06: The Aztecs Before the Conquest
The episode is set in 1486, at the start of Ahuitzotl's reign. It shows the Triple Alliance (Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, Tlacopan) forcing a town to pay tribute, then follows Mexica schoolchildren as a priest teaches them about sacrifice to the sun, warrior training, the founding legend of Tenochtitlan and the laws of daily life. It ends with the legend of Quetzalcoatl's promised return and the arrival of Cortés.

## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "But one day soon, he will return. Believe it, all the oracles tell us this."
  **Today:** The story that the Aztecs expected Quetzalcoatl to return from the east, and then took Cortés for him, is now widely seen as a post-conquest invention. It does not appear in Cortés's letters. It took shape decades later in Franciscan and Nahua writings that tried to explain the defeat after the fact. Susan Gillespie (*The Aztec Kings*, 1989) first argued this. Camilla Townsend ("Burying the White Gods", *American Historical Review*, 2003) and Matthew Restall (*Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest*, 2003) made it the mainstream view. Around 1991 most narrative histories still told the story.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Taken for the god Quetzalcoatl came Cortes, the conquistador."
  **Today:** This rests on the same post-conquest legend (see above). Moctezuma's gifts and his caution toward Cortés are now read as normal Mesoamerican diplomacy toward a powerful unknown force, not as religious paralysis (Restall, *When Montezuma Met Cortés*, 2018).
  **Confidence:** high

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "Homosexuality? It depends on the sex of the couple. For women, it is death. For men, well, nobody's perfect."
  **Today:** The colonial sources on Aztec and Texcocan law (Motolinía, Las Casas, Ixtlilxochitl's account of Nezahualcoyotl's laws) all say sex between men was punished by **death**, and some describe especially cruel deaths. They say the same for women. These sources were written by Spaniards and Christianised Nahuas and may exaggerate, but none of them says men were tolerated. The joke reverses what the sources say.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "In sacrifices, he took only the blood of serpents and birds, hence his name."
  **Today:** The name Quetzalcoatl means "quetzal(-feathered) serpent", the plumed serpent, a deity shown that way since Teotihuacan (about 200 AD). It does not come from what he was offered. The legend of the Toltec priest-king Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl does say he offered snakes, birds and butterflies instead of humans (Anales de Cuauhtitlan). But Quetzalcoatl as a god did receive human sacrifice, for example at Cholula, so "enemy of all violence" is also a legend.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Fate awaits anyone who's caught using peyotl."
  **Today:** No known Aztec law punished peyote use. Peyote came mainly from the northern deserts and was linked to the Chichimecs. Hallucinogenic mushrooms (teonanacatl) and similar plants were taken openly at feasts by merchants and nobles, as Sahagún describes. The surviving laws punish drunkenness, not ritual drugs.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Now, they have the same monuments, the same pyramids, same lifestyle as their cousins farther north."
  **Today:** The mound-building peoples of the Mississippi valley (episode 05) built earthen platform mounds. They lived in chiefdoms, and their greatest centre, Cahokia, was largely abandoned by about 1350. The Aztecs built stone pyramid-temples and lived in large cities under a tribute empire with writing and markets. A Mesoamerican influence on the Mississippians is possible but unproven. The two were not "the same".
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- 1486 is the year Ahuitzotl came to the throne, and the Great Temple's enlarged rebuilding was dedicated in 1487 ("tomorrow, we will consecrate our great temple"). The dates fit.
- The tribute war is well researched and to the show's credit. It follows Durán's account: merchants being attacked as the cause of war, then three embassies (Mexica, Texcoco, Tlacopan) each giving 20 days. The envoys gave the enemy shields and weapons and anointed the ruler with a sacred ointment, so that it could not be said the town was attacked unarmed. The demands for tribute in gold, feathers and cloth, a place for Huitzilopochtli ("Vici de Polchi" is a mishearing), and some captives are also true to the sources. "Clacopans" means Tlacopan.
- Months of 20 days: the Aztec solar year had 18 veintenas of 20 days, plus 5 extra days.
- Blood as "precious water" (chalchiuhatl, a real Nahuatl metaphor), and the sun needing it to keep moving, is fair to Mexica belief. So is the idea that warriors who died in battle or on the sacrificial stone joined the sun. (Women who died in childbirth did too.)
- The warrior training follows the Codex Mendoza closely: hair cut at 10 leaving a lock, the lock cut at the first captive, captives taken by two or three youths together, a return to commoner life after failing for several campaigns, and promotion after four captives to the eagle and jaguar orders. What counted was **capturing** enemies, not killing them.
- "20,000 years after the first tribes crossed the Bering Straits" and hunting mammoths 15,000 years ago fit current estimates (about 16,000–23,000 years for the first arrivals; mammoth kill sites in the Basin of Mexico). People crossed by the Beringian land bridge or along its coast, not across the strait.
- Writing, temples and pottery before the Aztecs: Mesoamerican writing goes back to about 600 BC (Zapotec and Olmec-area inscriptions).
- The founding legend (Huitzilopochtli, the eagle on a cactus) is presented as a story the priest tells. The snake in the eagle's beak is a mainly colonial-era version of the sign.
- Itzcoatl ("Itzhakotl") forming the Triple Alliance with Texcoco and Tlacopan (1428).
- The drunkenness laws follow the sources: drinking allowed for the old, commoners shaved and beaten, nobles executed. Execution of a thief who was a noble, stoning for adultery and death for moving boundary markers are also in the sources. Theft was mostly punished by slavery to the victim until the loss was repaid.
- Slavery rules: the Aztec slave (tlacotli) had rights, could only be sold after repeated bad conduct, and became free by escaping into the royal palace. Their children were born free, which the show does not mention.
- Confessing once in a lifetime and being let off: this was confession before a priest of Tlazolteotl (Sahagún), mainly for sexual offences.
- Heavy tribute and labour service on the dikes and the aqueducts (Nezahualcoyotl's dike, 1449; Ahuitzotl's Acuecuexco aqueduct, 1499). Actual tax rates are not known, so "half the corn" is a cartoon exaggeration.
- Marriage arranged by parents through elderly women matchmakers, with ritual refusals at first.
- The table manners and the priest's advice paraphrase the huehuetlahtolli (elders' speeches) collected by Sahagún.
- "30 years later ... came Cortes": he landed in 1519, 33 years after 1486.
