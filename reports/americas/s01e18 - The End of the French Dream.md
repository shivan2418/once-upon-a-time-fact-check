# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e18: The End of the French Dream
The episode follows René-Robert Cavelier de La Salle from his arrival in New France in the 1660s, through the Iroquois destruction of the Huron, Bishop Laval's church quarrels, Talon and the King's Daughters, Frontenac and Fort Frontenac, the Griffon and Fort Crèvecoeur, to his 1682 descent of the Mississippi and claim of "Louisiana". It ends with his failed 1684–87 Gulf colony, his murder in Texas, and a fast run through the Seven Years' War (Braddock, the Acadian expulsion, Louisbourg, Quebec) to the loss of New France in 1763.

## Outdated (since 1991)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "But now, well, those 400 Dutch rifles tipped the balance."
  **Today:** Dutch guns mattered, but the Huron (Wendat) collapse of 1649 had deeper causes. Smallpox and other epidemics in 1634–40 killed about half the Wendat. Christian and traditionalist Wendat factions had also split. Historians since Bruce Trigger (*The Children of Aataentsic*, 1976) have made this point. Gunfire alone did not decide the war.
  **Why it was believed:** The "Beaver Wars" story, in which Iroquois firepower crushed the Huron, was the standard textbook account from Parkman to the mid-20th century.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Alone, exhausted, and with no food in a hostile land 2,000 kilometers from home."
  **Today:** Nobody knows where La Salle was between leaving the Sulpicians in 1669 and 1671. The claim that he spent two heroic years alone, or reached the Ohio, comes from a doubtful anonymous memoir. In 1671 Nicolas Perrot reportedly met him on the Ottawa River hunting with a party of Iroquois, so he was not alone.
  **Why it was believed:** Francis Parkman's romantic biography (1869) and later popular accounts filled the gap with heroics.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The Jesuits wanted credit for most of the big discoveries being made, and that's why."
  **Today:** The Jesuits plotting against La Salle comes mainly from La Salle's own letters, which often blamed enemies for his failures. Colbert did distrust Jesuit influence, and the Jesuits did oppose La Salle's fur-trade monopoly. But modern biographers such as Céline Dupré (*Dictionary of Canadian Biography*, 1966) treat the plot story with caution. The show presents one side of a feud as fact.
  **Why it was believed:** Parkman's strongly anti-Jesuit telling shaped popular accounts of La Salle for a century.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "He has under his command a certain ship's captain, who was to be no help at all, Captain Beaujeu."
  **Today:** La Salle and Beaujeu did quarrel bitterly. However, the 1684–85 expedition overshot the Mississippi mainly because of La Salle's own mistakes: a wrong longitude for the river's mouth and wrong assumptions about the Gulf currents. Robert Weddle (*The French Thorn*, 1991) and other historians blame La Salle at least as much as Beaujeu. Even the episode's dialogue has La Salle steering west.
  **Why it was believed:** The heroic-La Salle story, based on his own letters and Parkman, needed a villain.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "You realize, one-third of the USA territory today was controlled by France?"
  **Today:** France *claimed* the Mississippi basin, roughly a third of the lower 48 states. It controlled only a few forts, missions and trading posts, and a few thousand colonists lived there. The land was held and governed by Native nations: Illinois, Osage, Chickasaw, Natchez, Choctaw, Sioux and many others. The closing line, "by the sheer willpower of the Cavalier de La Salle, it was French", repeats this "empty continent" framing.
  **Why it was believed:** Maps coloured by European claims were the normal way to teach colonial North America.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "Robert Cavalier de La Salle was 25 when he saw Canada for the first time"
  **Today:** La Salle was born on 22 November 1643 and reached Canada in 1667, so he was 23. A small slip.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The Jesuit, Monseigneur de Laval, who was to emerge the winner, consecrated a bishop."
  **Today:** François de Laval was not a Jesuit. He was a secular priest, educated by the Jesuits and backed by them. Rome made him vicar apostolic and titular bishop of Petraea in 1658, and bishop of Quebec in 1674. The episode does get the rivalry right: the Archbishop of Rouen's Sulpician vicar-general, Gabriel de Queylus, against Rome's appointee.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Monsieur, with your permission, I would present the brothers Marquette and Joliet."
  **Today:** Jacques Marquette (a Jesuit priest) and Louis Jolliet (a layman, a former seminarian) were not brothers, either by birth or in a religious order. Their Mississippi voyage was in 1673, not before La Salle's 1669 expedition. The episode never says that they reached the Mississippi nine years before La Salle. ("Brothers" may be a dubbing slip for *Père*/Father.)
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Listen, the Abbé Jolier has decided to go on all the way to Lake Erie."
  **Today:** In 1669 it was the Sulpicians Dollier de Casson and Galinée who decided to follow Jolliet's route west by Lake Erie. Jolliet was not an abbé. This is probably a dub or transcription mix-up of the names.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In a word, returning to Creve Coeur on January 25, 1681,"
  **Today:** The expedition down the Illinois and Mississippi left in December 1681 and January **1682**. It reached the Mississippi on 6 February 1682 and the Gulf in early April. La Salle claimed Louisiana on 9 April 1682.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Once again, de la Salle missed it, but one year after his departure and after endless searching,"
  **Today:** The fleet left France in August 1684 and landed in Matagorda Bay, Texas, in February 1685, about six months later. Most of the "endless searching" came after the landing, from Fort Saint-Louis in 1685–87.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Rule 1687, Robert de La Salle is assassinated together with two of his men, by one of his own bandits, said."
  **Today:** On 19 March 1687 Pierre Duhaut shot La Salle in an ambush near the Trinity River, with the surgeon Liotot as his accomplice. La Salle was killed alone. His nephew Moranget, his servant Saget and the Shawnee hunter Nika had been murdered a few days earlier. ("said" is probably a mishearing of "Duhaut".)
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In the English offensive in Arcadia, the inhabitants were deported or shot."
  **Today:** Acadia had been British Nova Scotia since 1713, so this was not an offensive into French territory. From 1755 the British deported Acadians who refused an unconditional oath of loyalty: about 11,000–12,000 people up to 1764, and thousands died of disease or at sea. Some were killed in raids, but the policy was deportation and burning farms, not shooting. "Great disturbance" translates *le Grand Dérangement*, usually called the Great Upheaval.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "the English send their 30,000 soldiers to meet the French's 300."
  **Today:** By 1758 the British had about 20,000+ regulars and a similar number of colonial provincials in North America. The French had about 6,800 regulars (troupes de terre and troupes de la marine), plus Canadian militia and Native allies, together well over 10,000. "300" is absurd and may be a dubbing error for 3,000 or for regiments. The British advantage was real but nothing like 100 to 1.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major

## Simplified but fine
- La Salle had left the Jesuits in 1667. His father's death in 1666 left him without an inheritance, since his vows barred him from inheriting. The Sulpicians, seigneurs of Montreal, granted him land at Lachine.
- About 3,000 French in the 1660s, against roughly 3:1 Dutch and 20:1 English, is about right.
- The Dutch sold about 400 guns (muskets, not rifles) to the Mohawk in 1648 under Stuyvesant's administration. "Give" is loose wording.
- Stuyvesant took New Sweden (1655) and England took New Netherland (1664).
- Governor Courcelle and Intendant Talon arrived in 1665 with the Carignan-Salières regiment, about 1,200 men sent from France rather than raised locally.
- About 770–800 King's Daughters (filles du roi) came in 1663–73, many of them orphans, with royal dowries. "Excellent families" is a recruiter's pitch in the dialogue: most were poor. To its credit, the show avoids the "brides were prostitutes" myth.
- Talon's plan to send New France grain and fish to the French Antilles is accurate.
- The July 1669 expedition with the Sulpicians Dollier and Galinée (misheard "Galinet and Dolier") took place, and La Salle pleaded illness and left them.
- Frontenac arrived in 1672, Fort Frontenac (Cataraqui) was built in 1673 with an Iroquois council, and "Father de Blon" is probably the Jesuit Claude Dablon. Fears of a Dutch attack in 1673 are real, since the Dutch retook New York that year.
- La Salle's trips to France: in 1674–75 he got Fort Frontenac and noble rank, and in 1678 a commission to explore west with the Prince de Conti's support ("The 14th of France" = Louis XIV).
- The Griffon (1679) vanished with its cargo, and La Salle suspected the pilot. Fort Miamis, Fort Conti and Fort Crèvecoeur (1680), and the Mascouten chief Monso's night-time gifts of kettles, hatchets and knives to turn the Illinois against La Salle all match the sources.
- La Salle's winter trek in 1680 to Fort Frontenac (about 1,600 km rather than 2,000), the revolt at Crèvecoeur, and his capture of the deserters on Lake Ontario.
- The 1682 party: the sources give 23 Frenchmen (not 22), 18 Native men, 10 women and 3 children. La Salle claimed Louisiana for Louis XIV.
- Governor La Barre ran down the discovery and seized Fort Frontenac, and the king briefly called it "very useless". La Salle went back to France and was given command of Louisiana.
- The 1684 fleet had four ships and about 300 people. It stopped at Saint-Domingue, where one ship was lost to Spanish privateers ("Little Gold" = Petit-Goâve). They landed at Matagorda Bay, where the Texas Colorado flows in, roughly 500–650 km west of the Mississippi.
- France held the Mississippi claim for about 80 years (1682–1762) and was outnumbered about 20:1 by the British colonies in the 1750s. Braddock's defeat (1755) was at the Monongahela, near Fort Duquesne, before war was formally declared (1756).
- Louisbourg (1758), Wolfe's climb to the Plains of Abraham, the deaths of Wolfe and Montcalm (1759), Montreal (1760). In 1762–63 western Louisiana and New Orleans went to Spain (Fontainebleau) and eastern Louisiana to Britain, not all of Louisiana to Spain.
- French help from Lafayette, de Grasse and Rochambeau was decisive at Yorktown.
