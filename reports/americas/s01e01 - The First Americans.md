# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e01: The First Americans
A band of Ice Age hunters in Siberia follows the game herds east, generation after generation, across the land bridge that joined Siberia and Alaska when sea levels were low. The episode ends with them waiting thousands of years for a wall of ice to melt before they can move south into the new continent. It gives no dates, but it follows the standard 1991 model: one overland crossing, then a trip south through an opening in the ice sheets.

## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "Thousands and thousands of years"
  **Today:** The episode follows the 1991 standard model: people crossed Beringia and then waited for the "wall of ice" to open into an ice-free corridor down the Canadian interior (the "Clovis-first" scenario, about 13,000 years ago). Later evidence changed this in three ways. Sites older than the corridor are now accepted: Monte Verde in Chile (about 14,500 years, accepted in 1997) and the White Sands footprints in New Mexico (about 21,000–23,000 years, published 2021, confirmed 2023). Studies of plant and animal remains show the corridor could not support travellers until about 12,600 years ago (Pedersen et al., *Nature*, 2016). Most researchers now favour a route down the Pacific coast, often in boats. Genetic studies since 2007 (the "Beringian standstill") suggest the ancestral population lived in Beringia for several thousand years before spreading south.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "the great barrier that blocked their way"
  **Today:** The ice sheets did block the interior route for a long time. But as noted above, the first people south of the ice probably went around it along the coast rather than waiting for the interior to melt. The episode's single-path story is the pre-1997 version.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "A period of cold back then transformed the sea into ice and snow,"
  **Today:** The Bering land bridge was not frozen sea. The sea level fell because huge amounts of water were locked up in ice sheets on land. That exposed the shallow seabed as dry land, mostly a cold, dry grassland (the "mammoth steppe") that was largely free of glaciers. Animals and people could live there.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular books pictured an "Ice Age" as ice everywhere, and they mixed up the land bridge with walking across sea ice. Geologists (e.g. Hopkins, *The Bering Land Bridge*, 1967) had described Beringia as an unglaciated steppe long before 1991.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "the deep cold had frozen it into ice and snow,"
  **Today:** Same point: the "earth bridge" the show mentions next was exposed seabed, a dry grassland, not a sheet of frozen sea.
  **Why it was believed:** The same Ice Age imagery as above.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "And with the guts, we can make bow strings,"
  **Today:** Ice Age hunters in Beringia and the first Americans hunted with thrusting spears and spear-throwers (atlatls). The bow reached the American Arctic only with the Arctic Small Tool tradition, about 4,500–5,000 years ago, and most of North America adopted it about 2,000–1,500 years ago. This was in standard archaeology textbooks by 1991.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Siberia and Alaska joined by a land bridge when the sea was lower. The "100 meters, 300 feet" fall in sea level is in the right range (about 120 m at the glacial maximum; the strait is only 30–50 m deep).
- The migration as a slow drift over generations, following the game, with no plan to reach a "new continent". This matches modern thinking.
- Hunters using bone needles, hides and gut, killing big game together, and travelling in small bands.
- The sabre-toothed cat in the elder's story: scimitar-toothed cats (*Homotherium*) did live in Beringia.
- The sea flooding the bridge behind them (about 11,000 years ago).
