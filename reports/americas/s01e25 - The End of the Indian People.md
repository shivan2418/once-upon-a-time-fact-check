# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e25: The End of the Indian People
The episode (in French) says outright that Native Americans were treated unjustly and nearly wiped out. It goes from Lewis and Clark through Jackson's removal of the Cherokee, Manifest Destiny and Frémont to the Apache wars (Cochise, Geronimo) and the Plains wars: Sand Creek, the Bozeman Trail and Fetterman, Fort Laramie 1868, the bison slaughter, Little Bighorn, the deaths of Crazy Horse and others, and Chief Joseph. It ends with Native peoples confined to reservations and about 250,000 remaining. It is much more honest than the "cowboys and Indians" myths of its time, but some details are wrong.

## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "Je me fais fort de battre les sioux à plate couture et traverser le pays de long..." [I'm confident I can thrash the Sioux and cross the country from end to end...]
  **Today:** This is the famous Fetterman boast ("Give me eighty men and I'll ride through the whole Sioux nation"). Shannon Smith's *Give Me Eighty Men* (2008) showed it rests on later, hostile testimony, mostly from Carrington's circle shifting the blame. The image of Fetterman as an arrogant hothead who disobeyed orders is now widely doubted, though it is still uncertain why he crossed Lodge Trail Ridge.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Un génie militaire qui, pour fuir la réserve, fait faire au sien homme, femme et enfant une retraite de 2700 kilomètres en 100 jours." [A military genius who, to escape the reservation, led his men, women and children on a 2,700-kilometre retreat in 100 days.]
  **Today:** Chief Joseph was mainly a civil and camp leader during the 1877 flight. The fighting was led mostly by Looking Glass, Joseph's brother Ollokot, White Bird, Toohoolhoolzote and Lean Elk (Poker Joe). The trek is usually given as about 1,170 miles (about 1,900 km) over about 110 days (June to 5 October 1877), though some accounts give longer distances.
  **Why it was believed:** Newspapers and army officers in 1877 made Joseph the "Red Napoleon" because he was the spokesman at the surrender, and his "I will fight no more forever" speech fixed that image in popular history.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Emmenez-les de l'autre côté du Mississippi, dans le désert de l'Oklahoma." [Take them across the Mississippi, into the Oklahoma desert.]
  **Today:** Indian Territory (eastern Oklahoma), where the Five Tribes were sent, is mostly wooded, well-watered and farmable. The Cherokee, Choctaw and Creek rebuilt farms and plantations there. It was the forced removal that was disastrous, not the land being desert.
  **Why it was believed:** The 19th-century idea of a "Great American Desert" west of the Mississippi lasted a long time in popular history.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "C'était hier ou plus avant-hier et en ce milieu du XVIIIe siècle, alors que les États-Unis ont déjà 23 millions d'habitants" [It was yesterday, or rather the day before, in the middle of the 18th century, when the United States already had 23 million inhabitants]
  **Today:** The figures (23 million Americans, France with about 36 million) are for the 1850 census, the middle of the **19th** century. This may be a speech-recognition slip for "XIXe".
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Manhattan, Brooklyn, ce sont quelques maisons de campagne difficiles d'accès." [Manhattan, Brooklyn are a few hard-to-reach country houses.]
  **Today:** In 1850 Brooklyn was a city of about 97,000 people, one of the largest in the US, linked to Manhattan by frequent ferries. Upper Manhattan (Harlem) was indeed still rural.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Chicago a environ 10 000 habitants." [Chicago has about 10,000 inhabitants.]
  **Today:** The 1850 census counted about 30,000 people in Chicago. The 10,000 figure belongs to the mid-1840s.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Ces terres sont immenses, mais elles sont inhabitables et inexploitables, président Jefferson." [These lands are immense, but they are uninhabitable and unusable, President Jefferson.]
  **Today:** Lewis and Clark (who left in May 1804) reported rich, game-filled country. The "Great American Desert" label came later, from Zebulon Pike (1810) and Stephen Long's expedition (1820). Jefferson did already favour moving eastern Native peoples west of the Mississippi from 1803.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "ils avaient 30 moulins, 10 siries, 60 échoppes de charrons, 300 charrues, 700 métiers attissés." [they had 30 mills, 10 sawmills, 60 wheelwright shops, 300 ploughs, 700 looms.]
  **Today:** The Cherokee census of 1825–26 counted 31 gristmills, 10 sawmills, 62 blacksmith shops, 762 looms, 22,000 cattle, about 7,600 horses and 46,000 pigs, all close to what the show says. But it counted about **2,900 ploughs**, not 300. The same census also counted about 1,300 enslaved Black people held by Cherokee owners, which the show leaves out.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "30 000 hommes seront déportés." [30,000 people will be deported.]
  **Today:** About 16,000 Cherokee were forcibly removed in 1838–39, and roughly 4,000 died. Counting all Five Tribes, about 60,000 people were removed in the 1830s. So 30,000 fits neither the Cherokee alone (as the context suggests) nor the total.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Voilà ce qu'écrivait en 1846 un dénommé John O'Sullivan." [That is what a certain John O'Sullivan wrote in 1846.]
  **Today:** John L. O'Sullivan used "manifest destiny" in 1845, in the July–August *Democratic Review* ("Annexation") and in the *New York Morning News* on 27 December 1845. The quotation itself is accurate.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Qu'on dépêche à leur secours 30 cavaliers épaulés par 150 fantassins !" [Send 30 cavalry supported by 150 infantry to their aid!]
  **Today:** On 21 December 1866 Fetterman led 81 men in all (about 49 infantry, 27 cavalry, 2 civilians and 3 officers), and all of them were killed. ("Fort Kenny" in the transcript is Fort Phil Kearny.)
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Trois millions de bêtes seront abattues chaque année." [Three million animals will be slaughtered every year.]
  **Today:** About 3 to 3.5 million bison were killed on the southern plains in **all** of 1872–74 (Dodge's and Hornaday's estimates), so roughly 1 to 1.5 million a year. The northern herd was destroyed later, in 1880–83. The scale of the slaughter is not exaggerated, but the yearly rate is.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Le gouvernement a décidé de construire une voie ferrée entre les Big Horn et les Black Hills, messieurs." [The government has decided to build a railway between the Big Horns and the Black Hills, gentlemen.]
  **Today:** What triggered the 1876 war was gold. Custer's 1874 expedition reported gold in the Black Hills, miners poured in against the 1868 treaty, and the Allison Commission (1875) tried to buy the Hills. The Northern Pacific's surveys along the Yellowstone (1873) also caused fighting, but they did not run between the Big Horns and the Black Hills.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "D'autres troupes partent à leur tour de Fort Lincoln et sous le commandement du général Crook." [Other troops leave in turn from Fort Lincoln under the command of General Crook.]
  **Today:** The 1876 campaign had three columns. Gibbon came from Fort Ellis (correct). Terry, with Custer's 7th Cavalry, came from Fort Abraham Lincoln. Crook came from Fort Fetterman in Wyoming. The line merges the last two, though the dub may have dropped a clause.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Major Bentin, prenez-vous aussi trois compagnies." [Major Benteen, you also take three companies.]
  **Today:** Frederick Benteen was a **captain** (Reno was the only major). He was sent scouting to the left (south-west) with three companies, not held as a reserve. Custer did take five companies, and Reno three.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Buffalo Bill obtiendra leur libération pour qu'ils puissent participer à son spectacle." [Buffalo Bill will obtain their release so they could take part in his show.]
  **Today:** Sitting Bull spent 1877–81 in Canada. He surrendered in July 1881 and was held as a prisoner of war at Fort Randall until May 1883. He was released to Standing Rock **before** he had anything to do with Cody. In 1885 the government let him tour with Buffalo Bill's Wild West for about four months. Gall surrendered in 1881 and settled at Standing Rock, and there is no record of him being imprisoned. His reported refusal to be "exhibited" fits the sources.
  **Confidence:** high

## Simplified but fine
- In 1850, five US cities with more than 100,000 people (Cincinnati made six) and New York at about 515,000. France (about 36 million) was the largest western European power, although Russia had more people.
- Lewis and Clark sent by Jefferson to explore the Louisiana Purchase; the Indian Removal Act (1830) under Jackson; the Cherokee "Americanised" with schools, churches, farms and herds. The show also leaves out the Cherokee syllabary, newspaper and constitution, and the *Worcester v. Georgia* ruling (1832) that Jackson ignored.
- The promise of land "as long as the grass grows and the waters run", the thousands who died on the Trail of Tears, and hundreds of broken treaties: all fairly stated, and better than the textbooks of the time.
- Texas after the Alamo (1836), Oregon (1846) and the Mexican Cession (1848). Frémont's 1842 climb of what he took for the highest Rocky Mountain peak (Frémont Peak, which is not the highest) and the near-drowning in the rapids with Charles Preuss. "Jessie" in that scene is odd, since Jessie Benton Frémont did not go on the expedition; it is probably a mishearing.
- Cochise: Mexicans had killed his father and relatives at a parley. In the Bascom Affair (February 1861) Lieutenant Bascom, a recent West Point graduate, met him with his family near the Butterfield station at Apache Pass. Cochise escaped, wounded, but his brother and nephews were later hanged, which the show leaves out. He then fought for about ten years (peace in 1872, died 1874).
- Homestead Act (1862): 160 acres (about 65 ha) for a small filing fee, granted regardless of treaty lands.
- Bozeman Trail from Fort Laramie to Virginia City, Montana; Red Cloud's War; the order not to pursue beyond Lodge Trail Ridge; the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 that abandoned the forts and recognised the Black Hills as Sioux land. The show rightly calls it one of the rare Native victories.
- Sand Creek (1864), "300 Cheyennes": the massacre of a camp that had been told it was under army protection is correctly shown. Estimates of the dead range from about 150 to 230 (the National Park Service says about 230), mostly women, children and old people, so 300 is on the high side.
- Buffalo Bill killing over 4,000 bison: he claimed 4,280 in about 17 months (1867–68). The general's line about hide hunters settling the "Indian question" echoes a speech attributed to General **Sheridan**, which is known only from a 1907 memoir. Army encouragement of the slaughter is well documented.
- Custer "the killer of Indian women": the Lakota nickname "Woman Killer" belonged to General Harney (Ash Hollow, 1855), but Custer's Washita attack (1868) did kill many women and children. His later image as a martyr is accurate.
- Crazy Horse: he surrendered in May 1877, was promised an agency in the north, and was bayoneted on 5 September 1877 while resisting arrest. He was about 33–37 years old, so "à peine 33 ans" is within range. The arrest followed rumours and intrigue rather than a mere "peccadillo".
- Geronimo carrying on the Apache struggle until he surrendered in 1886.
- Reservations "under army control": from the 1870s they were mostly run by civilian agents of the Interior Department's Indian Office, with the army in support.
- The population "Un million ? Dix millions ?" at contact is presented as an open question, which is fair. Most estimates for north of Mexico now run from about 2 to 7 million or more. About 250,000 at the end of the century matches the 1890 census (about 248,000).
- Omission: the Ghost Dance, the killing of Sitting Bull and the Wounded Knee massacre (all December 1890) are left out, although the episode claims resistance ended in the late 1880s. The Dawes Act (1887), which broke up reservation land, is also left out.
