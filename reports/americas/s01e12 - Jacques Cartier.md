# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e12: Jacques Cartier
The episode covers France's late start in the race for the Americas: Verrazzano's 1524 voyage along the North American coast for Francis I, then Jacques Cartier's first two voyages (1534 and 1535–36). It shows the Gulf of St. Lawrence, the first fur trading, Donnacona's sons taken to France, the trip upriver to Stadacona (Quebec) and Hochelaga (Montreal), and the scurvy winter cured by a Native cedar remedy. It closes with Roberval's later voyage and Cartier's "gold and diamonds" turning out to be pyrite and quartz.

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "He was the first to say that the New World was a new continent as well."
  **Today:** Amerigo Vespucci's *Mundus Novus* (1503) had already called the lands a "new world", and the Waldseemüller map (1507) drew them as a separate continent. Verrazzano's letter does describe a long new coastline unknown to the ancients, but he was not the first to say so.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "There was no passage to the West, and he was the first man who dared to say it."
  **Today:** The opposite is true. Verrazzano took the Outer Banks of North Carolina for a narrow isthmus and the sound behind it for the Pacific (the "Sea of Verrazzano"). Maps showed this imaginary sea for decades, and it encouraged the hope of a passage that Cartier was sent to find.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "Cartier is only 30 and the darling of the port city of Saint-Malo,"
  **Today:** Cartier was born in 1491, so he was 42 in 1534.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Today, the 13th day of August, 1535, we have entered a bay which our captain has named the Bay of St. Lawrence."
  **Today:** Cartier named the small bay (on the north shore, near Sainte-Geneviève/Pillage Bay) on **10 August** 1535, the feast of St Lawrence, which is where the name comes from. The name later spread to the gulf and the river.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Verrazzano: a Florentine sailing for Francis I in the *Dauphine* (sailing in January 1524), reaching the Carolina coast and sailing north to Newfoundland/Cape Breton. His death in 1528, killed and reportedly eaten by Caribs (probably on Guadeloupe), rests mainly on one account.
- Francis I's troubles: the Constable of Bourbon's defection (1523), Pavia and "all is lost save honour" (1525), his captivity, Chambord under construction. Pizarro's gold reaching Charles V around 1533–34. "Never bothered to look at" the reports is harsh, since the king was a prisoner in Madrid in 1525–26.
- The Saint-Malo merchants obstructing Cartier's recruitment and the royal order forcing ships to wait until his crews were complete.
- The first voyage: two ships, 61 men, leaving Saint-Malo on 20 April 1534 and sighting Newfoundland about 20 days later; Brion Island and the Îles aux Margaux; missing the river on the way home.
- July 1534 fur trading with the Mi'kmaq in Chaleur Bay. The show adds that crystals, shell and copper had religious meaning for Native peoples (George Hamell's work) and that they quickly turned to iron tools. This is better than the usual "trinkets for furs" cliché. The Mi'kmaq were already eager to trade because Basque and Breton fishermen had been coming for years, which the show leaves out.
- Donnacona's sons Domagaya and Taignoagny: Cartier's own account suggests they were taken more or less by a trick after Donnacona protested the cross raised at Gaspé. "persuade" softens this.
- The second voyage (1535–36): Grande Hermine, Petite Hermine and Émérillon, about 110 men; Stadacona and Hochelaga (2 October 1535); Donnacona's people trying to keep Cartier from going upriver to protect their trade position (a fair, modern reading).
- "a writer on board who's not exactly an unknown": who is meant is unclear. If it suggests Rabelais, the legend that he sailed with Cartier has no evidence behind it. The author of the voyage narratives is not known for certain.
- Scurvy cured with *annedda*, a white cedar (Thuja) remedy rich in vitamin C, taught by Domagaya. 25 men died, and the Petite Hermine was abandoned. All correct, and the show credits the Native knowledge.
- "We must take gifts to the king": Cartier actually kidnapped Donnacona and about nine others in May 1536. None of the adults ever came home. The show leaves this out rather than denying it.
- The third voyage under "Oberwald" (Roberval, a speech-recognition error): Cartier sailed ahead in 1541 and Roberval followed in 1542. The "gold and diamonds" were pyrite and quartz, hence the saying "faux comme les diamants du Canada".
- Early maps (about 1550) showing America mixed up with Asia: a fair point about how little was known.
