# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e11: Pizarro and the Inca Empire
The episode follows Francisco Pizarro from his obscure origins and Panama years, through the partnership with Almagro and Luque, the "line in the sand" and the first sight of Tumbes, to his royal commission from Charles V. It then shows everyday Inca life (quipus, storehouses, messengers), the civil war between Huáscar and Atahualpa, the ambush at Cajamarca, the ransom and execution of Atahualpa, Manco Inca's siege of Cusco and the civil war between the Pizarros and Almagro.

## Outdated (since 1991)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "We know with certainty he was a pig swiller, a farmhand, and a soldier."
  **Today:** Pizarro was the illegitimate son of Captain Gonzalo Pizarro and Francisca González, a servant in Trujillo. The swineherd story (and the tale of a baby abandoned at a church door and suckled by a sow, which the show also tells) goes back to López de Gómara's hostile *Historia* (1552). It is a plausible tradition, but not something "we know with certainty".
  **Why it was believed:** Gómara, then Prescott (1847), made the swineherd-to-conqueror story a set piece of popular history.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "One of those strange quirks of history with a handful of soldiers, Pizarro,"
  **Today:** About 170 Spaniards captured Atahualpa, but they did not end the empire on their own. Earlier epidemics (probably smallpox, which killed Huayna Capac and his heir around 1528) and the succession war had already broken the state. Thousands of Native allies (Cañari, Chachapoya, Huáscar's supporters) fought alongside the Spaniards, and Spanish reinforcements followed. Inca resistance went on from Vilcabamba until 1572.
  **Why it was believed:** Prescott's *Conquest of Peru* (1847) set the "handful of heroes" story. Studies of Native allies and of epidemics (Hemming 1970; later Restall 2003) were not yet standard in popular works.
  **Confidence:** high

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "He would serve many captains, including Ponce de Leon,"
  **Today:** No record puts Pizarro under Ponce de León. His known commanders were Alonso de Ojeda (Urabá, 1509–10), Balboa and Pedrarias Dávila. Ponce de León was conquering Puerto Rico and Florida at that time.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "I shall state our case personally when we see Governor Pedrarias."
  **Today:** By the Isla del Gallo episode (1527) Pedrarias had been replaced as governor of Panama by Pedro de los Ríos. It was de los Ríos who sent Juan Tafur to bring the men back and who later gave the stayers a fixed time to return.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Cortez brings back three tons of gold every year."
  **Today:** No such annual flow existed. The total gold taken from the Aztecs was a few tonnes at most, and much of it was lost on the Noche Triste. Large shipments of American bullion came later and were mostly silver, after Potosí (1545) and Zacatecas (1546).
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Almagro will become governor of Tumbez,"
  **Today:** The Capitulación de Toledo (26 July 1529) made Almagro only commander of the fortress of Tumbes, with the rank of hidalgo, a slight that soured his partnership with Pizarro. Pizarro was made governor and captain-general of Peru and a knight of Santiago (both correct). The marquisate the show adds ("and the Marquis") came only in 1537.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "you are Viracocha, the thunder god."
  **Today:** Viracocha was the Andean creator god; the thunder and weather god was Illapa. The Spaniards were called *viracochas*, but the idea that Andeans took them for gods comes mostly from post-conquest writers, much like the Quetzalcoatl story in Mexico.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "A thousand Incas killed on the spot and not one Spaniard wounded."
  **Today:** Spanish eyewitnesses (Xerez, Mena) give about 2,000 dead at Cajamarca on 16 November 1532; other accounts give up to 7,000. The one Spaniard wounded was Pizarro himself, cut on the hand while shielding Atahualpa. The show reduces the scale of the massacre.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "His brother Manco Capac to keep them quiet."
  **Today:** The puppet ruler Pizarro crowned in 1533 was Manco Inca (Manco Inca Yupanqui), after the brief reign of Túpac Huallpa. Manco Capac is the legendary founder of the Inca dynasty. This may be a slip in the dub.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "raised 400,000 men for the siege of Cusco."
  **Today:** Chroniclers estimated the 1536 besieging army at roughly 40,000–200,000, and even 200,000 is thought to be exaggerated. 400,000 goes beyond any source.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Spago was strangled while imprisoned three months later in the city of Lima"
  **Today:** Diego de Almagro was garrotted in July 1538 in **Cusco**, on Hernando Pizarro's orders, about three months after Las Salinas. Francisco Pizarro was in Lima. The transcript may have run two clauses together, but as heard it is wrong. Episode 13 itself correctly says Almagro's son was executed "in the same place as his father", which was Cusco.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Pizarro arresting Balboa on Pedrarias's orders (1519); Pascual de Andagoya's 1522 reports of a rich land to the south ("Pascal Andragoya" is a mishearing); the partnership with Almagro and the priest Hernando de Luque.
- The "line in the sand" on Isla del Gallo and the Thirteen of Fame ("A dozen men crossed the line"). The story comes from slightly later chroniclers but is widely accepted.
- Tumbes (1528): a female ruler (*capullana*, which the show treats as a name), Alonso de Molina staying behind, and Pedro de Candía ("Pedro de Cogna") sent ashore. The "Pitting cows" are spitting llamas.
- Charles V's situation: Pavia (1525), the Sack of Rome (1527), the imperial coronation (1530). Pizarro sailed in 1530–31 with about 180 men and 27 horses, not the "200 soldiers and 30 horsemen" (the contract required 250).
- Inca daily life: quipus as records, state storehouses (*qollqa*), llama loads of about 30 kg, the chasqui relay messengers. All sound.
- Child sacrifice (*capacocha*) and the chosen women (*acllas*): real institutions, later confirmed by finds such as the Ampato "Juanita" (1995) and Llullaillaco (1999) mummies. The victims were left on mountain peaks rather than "thrown into the abyss", "Wallaboo" is an unclear name, and the "5,000 concubines" figure is a guess.
- Huayna Capac's death (about 1528) and the war between Huáscar and Atahualpa. Huáscar's family was massacred by Atahualpa's generals.
- Cajamarca: about 170 Spaniards against an army of tens of thousands; Valverde's Requerimiento and Atahualpa's retort about the Pope giving away what is not his (as told by later chroniclers).
- The ransom room filled with gold to the height of his raised hand, and the melting down of the treasure.
- Felipillo's alleged love for one of Atahualpa's wives as a motive for his false translation is chroniclers' gossip, shown here as fact.
- Atahualpa's trial and execution (26 July 1533): sentenced to burning, garrotted instead after accepting baptism. The show muddles the order of the sentence but gets the outcome right. De Soto's protest that there was no uprising is accurate.
- Paullu Inca guiding Almagro to Chile (1535); Las Salinas (April 1538); Pizarro murdered in Lima and Valverde killed by Natives on Puná, both in 1541.
