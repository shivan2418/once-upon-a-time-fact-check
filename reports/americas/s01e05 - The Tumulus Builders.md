# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e05: The Tumulus Builders
The episode covers the farming cultures of the American Southwest: the Hohokam with their irrigation and ball courts, and the Anasazi (Ancestral Puebloans) at Mesa Verde and Pueblo Bonito, who build a solar observatory and later scatter in a great drought. It then follows the recurring characters east to the Mississippian mound builders and Cahokia's huge Monks Mound, where a chief settles a dispute over field boundaries. It ends with European germs and guns destroying these societies.

## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "And that was how they discovered irrigation."
  **Today:** The episode credits the Hohokam (from about 150 BC) with inventing irrigation. Excavations in the Tucson Basin since the late 1990s and 2000s, notably Las Capas, found irrigation canals dating to about 1200 BC. Maize farming in the Southwest goes back to about 2100 BC. Irrigation began in the "Early Agricultural Period", centuries before the Hohokam culture. The Hohokam did build the largest canal systems in pre-Columbian North America (hundreds of kilometres along the Salt and Gila rivers).
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "The name means old people."
  **Today:** The name "Anasazi" is a Navajo word, often rendered "ancient enemies" or "ancestors of the enemies" rather than simply "old people". Since the 1990s archaeologists and the Pueblo nations have generally preferred "Ancestral Puebloans". They were the ancestors not only of the Hopi but of all the Pueblo peoples, including Zuni, Acoma and the Rio Grande Pueblos.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Mexican influence is seen clearly here in the crops that they grow,"
  **Today:** Maize and beans did come from Mexico. But squash, sunflower and other crops were domesticated independently in eastern North America (the "Eastern Agricultural Complex"; Bruce Smith, *Science*, 1989). Mound building there is much older than Mississippian times: Poverty Point dates to about 1700 BC, and Watson Brake in Louisiana to about 3500 BC (*Science*, 1997). Most archaeologists now see Mississippian art and iconography, including the feathered-serpent-like figures, as a local development, not proof of direct Mexican influence. No Mesoamerican trade goods have been securely identified at Cahokia.
  **Why it was believed:** Mid-20th-century "diffusionist" archaeology often explained complex societies in the Eastern Woodlands as offshoots of Mesoamerica.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "In a very short time, these people were turned into a dying race."
  **Today:** Epidemics, warfare and slave raiding did cause catastrophic losses in the Southeast after de Soto (1539–43). But the descendants of the Mississippian chiefdoms (Muscogee/Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Cherokee, Natchez and others) re-formed into new nations and survive today. The Native population of the US has grown steadily since about 1900. "Dying race" repeats the 19th-century "vanishing Indian" idea.
  **Why it was believed:** The "vanishing Indian" was a long-standing theme of popular history and art.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "the descent to Mesa Verde and they need ropes."
  **Today:** Mesa Verde is a whole mesa (now a national park) with hundreds of cliff dwellings, not one building. Its best-known sites, such as Cliff Palace, Balcony House and Spruce Tree House, have been visited by ordinary tourists for over a century, using stairs and ladders. The inhabitants used carved hand-and-toe-hold trails. Most of the population lived on the mesa top, and the cliff dwellings were built only in the last century of occupation (about 1190–1280).
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "the one that is most remarkable has to be Pueblo Bonito, east of New Mexico."
  **Today:** Pueblo Bonito is in Chaco Canyon, in **northwestern** New Mexico. It was built in stages from about 850 to 1150 AD.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Just before the year 1200, we see the unfortunate fate of the Anasazi."
  **Today:** Chaco's decline came after a drought in the 1130s–1150s. The final abandonment of the Four Corners region (Mesa Verde included) came about 1280–1300, during the "Great Drought" of 1276–1299 identified from tree rings by A. E. Douglass (1929). The unfinished "temple" fits Mesa Verde's Sun Temple, built in the mid-to-late 1200s. Drought was one factor among several, along with violence, conflict and social change.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "There are at least 20 inside the city limits and a countless number of them outside."
  **Today:** Cahokia had about **120** mounds within the site, about 70 of which survive in the state historic site.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "To the east of the spot where the city of St. Louis will one day be built, Ahokia, an area about 500 acres."
  **Today:** Cahokia ("Ahokia") is east of St Louis, across the Mississippi in Illinois. But the settlement covered about 6 square miles (about 1,600 ha, roughly 4,000 acres). Only its central palisaded precinct was smaller (about 200 acres).
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "In three centuries, with the coming of Europeans, this magnificent civilization would disappear."
  **Today:** Cahokia itself was largely empty by about 1350–1400, more than a century before any European arrived. Much of the central Mississippi and lower Ohio valleys was also abandoned (Stephen Williams' "Vacant Quarter", 1990). Other Mississippian chiefdoms, such as Coosa and the Natchez, were still thriving when de Soto arrived and collapsed afterwards. The episode's own chronology also fails: the characters leave the Southwest about 1200–1300 and arrive at a Cahokia that was already past its peak (about 1050–1150).
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Hohokam ("those who have vanished", from the O'odham word) in southern Arizona, with canals and ball courts. How the ball game was played is uncertain.
- Pueblo Bonito's great houses, granaries and plazas. The solar calendar recalls Chaco's solstice and equinox markers (the Fajada Butte "Sun Dagger", 1977). The "signal" network echoes Chacoan line-of-sight sites, which are thought to have been used for signalling.
- Anasazi groups dispersing north (Hopi), into Mogollon country and to the Rio Grande, rather than "vanishing". This is a good corrective to the "mysterious disappearance" myth.
- About 10,000 mounds in Ohio (Squier and Davis's 1848 estimate). Most of these were built by the much earlier Adena and Hopewell cultures (800 BC–500 AD), not by Mississippians.
- Cahokia with "over 20,000" people (estimates run from about 10,000 to 20,000-plus), the largest city north of Mexico.
- Monks Mound ("the one of the monkey"): about 6–7 ha at the base, about 30 m high, about 620,000 m³ of earth, built from an estimated 14–15 million basket loads ("men" is probably "baskets"). Its base is comparable to the Great Pyramid's, but "equal to or exceeds the greatest monuments on Earth" overstates it (Giza is about four times its volume).
- Kin-owned fields with boundary markers and a chief judging disputes. This rightly contradicts the myth that Native peoples had no notion of property.
- The closing line "neither savages nor saints" and the direct mention of epidemics and firearms are better than the noble-or-savage stereotypes common at the time.
