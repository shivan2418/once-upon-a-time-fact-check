# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e16: The Indians in the 17th Century
A travelling Native family carries the story of the 17th-century East Coast as Native peoples saw it. It covers the Powhatan chiefdom and Jamestown (Pocahontas, the 1622 and 1644 uprisings), the Lenape between Swedes, Dutch and Iroquois, the wampum and gun trades, King Philip's War, and the Five Nations Iroquois facing French Governor Denonville. The episode is openly critical of colonial land theft and violence, which was unusual for children's TV at the time. It ends with William Penn's fairness being undone by his son's Walking Purchase.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "I do, Father, and I think the man is handsome."
  **Today:** As in episode 15, the rescue of John Smith comes only from Smith's 1624 account and is doubted. The romance hinted at here ("You know, you're a pretty girl.") has no historical basis. Pocahontas was about 11 in 1607. She was later kidnapped by the English (1613) and married John Rolfe (1614).
  **Why it was believed:** The Smith–Pocahontas romance was a staple of 19th-century plays, paintings and schoolbooks.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "They hunted them down and exterminated them."
  **Today:** The Powhatan peoples were defeated, lost most of their land and population, and were bound by the treaties of 1646 and 1677, but they were not wiped out. The Pamunkey and Mattaponi have held reservations in Virginia since the 17th century. The Pamunkey were federally recognised in 2015, and six more Virginia tribes in 2018.
  **Why it was believed:** The "vanishing Indian" idea, that East Coast peoples simply disappeared, was standard in popular history. Many Native communities in the East had no federal recognition in 1991.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "They completely massacred King Philip's whole tribe."
  **Today:** King Philip's War (1675–76) was catastrophic. Thousands of Native people were killed, and many were sold into slavery in the West Indies, Philip's wife and son among them. But the Wampanoag survived. The Aquinnah (Gay Head) Wampanoag were federally recognised in 1987 and the Mashpee in 2007.
  **Why it was believed:** Same "vanishing Indian" idea.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "They wiped out the Mohegans who were allies."
  **Today:** The Mohegans were English allies in the Pequot War and King Philip's War, and they survived. The Mohegan Tribe was federally recognised in 1994. The nation nearly destroyed in the 1637 Mystic massacre was the **Pequot**, and they also survive (Mashantucket Pequot, recognised 1983). "Same with the Delawares" is also wrong: the Lenape were pushed west but survive today as the Delaware Nation and Delaware Tribe in Oklahoma and the Munsee in Ontario.
  **Why it was believed:** Same "vanishing Indian" idea. The name Mohegan is also often confused with Mohican.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "No, Powahattans detest any violence."
  **Today:** Wahunsenacah (Powhatan) built his paramount chiefdom of about 30 tribes partly by conquest. Strachey reports that he destroyed the Chesepian people and drove out the Kecoughtan. The Powhatans were neither more nor less violent than their neighbours. This line is the "noble savage" myth stated as fact.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "On that morning, 27 white people die in the St. James River."
  **Today:** The coordinated attack on 22 March 1622 killed about **347** colonists, a quarter to a third of the English in Virginia, all along the James River settlements. "27" may be a dub or speech-recognition error.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "So the war against the Powhatans was to last for 14 years."
  **Today:** The Second Anglo-Powhatan War ran from 1622 to a peace in 1632, about ten years. The "powwow" with guaranteed safety refers to a real betrayal: at a peace parley in May 1623 the English served poisoned wine and killed about 200 Powhatans. The show only hints at this.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Tomorrow at dawn, we attack Jamestown."
  **Today:** Opechancanough's 1644 uprising (18 April 1644, indeed 22 years after 1622) struck the outlying plantations and killed about 400–500 colonists. Jamestown itself was not attacked, and the war ended in Powhatan defeat: Opechancanough was captured in 1646 and shot while a prisoner in Jamestown. Calling it an "Indian victory at Jamestown" is misleading.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "But they were able to keep it only one year."
  **Today:** New Sweden lasted 17 years, from Peter Minuit's land purchase from the Lenape and Fort Christina in 1638 until Stuyvesant conquered it in 1655. What lasted only a year was Governor Risingh's 1654 seizure of the Dutch Fort Casimir and his treaty with the Lenape that year.
  **Confidence:** high

## Simplified but fine
- Spain keeping only a "small fringe" of what became the US (Florida, New Mexico) while holding Mexico, Central America and most of South America.
- Pueblo farmers raided by Apaches in the 17th-century Southwest.
- Powhatan's chiefdom of about 30 tribes and roughly 150–200 villages. Its food saved starving Jamestown, and the colonists then took corn by force. Smith seizing Opechancanough by the hair at gunpoint (January 1609) is real, though it happened in the second winter, not the third.
- Powhatan died in 1618; Opitchapam was nominally chief, with his brother Opechancanough holding the real power. Opechancanough's claimed age of about 100 comes from colonial sources (Beverley) and is likely exaggerated.
- Europeans mass-producing wampum with steel drills and inflating it; the Swedes trading guns; the Dutch trading guns for furs; the Iroquois demanding tribute from the Lenape; Dutch and English scalp bounties (Kieft's War 1643, King Philip's War).
- King Philip (Metacom) ordered to give up his guns (Taunton, 1671); his Christian interpreter John Sassamon reporting to Plymouth, Sassamon's murder (1675), and the hanging of three Wampanoag, which set off the war.
- Iroquois adoption of captives ("mourning wars"), the Five Nations council, and False Face medicine masks.
- "François Deneuville" is Governor Jacques-René de **Denonville**. In 1687 he lured Iroquois men to Fort Frontenac and seized them, and dozens were sent to the French galleys.
- Colonists turning tribes against each other while relying on Native help to survive.
- William Penn's fair dealing, and his sons' fraudulent Walking Purchase of 1737 (Thomas and John Penn with James Logan). The show handles this accurately.
