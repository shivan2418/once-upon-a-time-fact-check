# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e20: The War of Independence
The episode follows the American Revolutionary War from Ticonderoga and Washington's appointment through the Declaration of Independence, the loss of New York, Trenton and Princeton, Saratoga and the French alliance, Valley Forge, Benedict Arnold's treason, and the French-American victory at Yorktown. It closes with the Treaty of Paris (1783) and Washington's inauguration (1789).

## Outdated (since 1991)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "oh no a third of the population remained indifferent to their war and another third"
  **Today:** The "one third patriot, one third loyalist, one third neutral" formula comes from a John Adams letter, but there he was talking about American attitudes to the *French* Revolution. Paul H. Smith (1968) estimated Loyalists at about 19–20% of the white population, and most historians now put them at 15–20%.
  **Why it was believed:** The neat "thirds" was repeated in textbooks for generations as Adams's verdict on the Revolution.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Providence arranged things very well, delaying General Howe's advance by two days."
  **Today:** This is the story of Mary Lindley Murray (Murray Hill, 15 September 1776), who supposedly delayed Howe with cake and wine while Putnam's troops escaped. Even the legend says hours, not days. Historians think Howe was halted anyway, waiting for his second wave of troops, and treat the story as embellished folklore.
  **Why it was believed:** It was told in 19th-century histories and memorials, and it is a charming, patriotic anecdote.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Monmouth was a complete rout, mostly because of the cowardice of General Lee."
  **Today:** Monmouth (28 June 1778) was tactically a draw. Lee's advance guard fell back in confusion, Washington rallied the army, the two sides fought to a standstill, and the British withdrew overnight. Lee was court-martialled for disobeying orders, an "unnecessary, disorderly" retreat and disrespect to Washington, not for cowardice. Many historians think he was treated unfairly (e.g. Lender & Stone, 2016).
  **Why it was believed:** Washington-centred histories cast Lee as the villain of the day, and his later disgrace (and the discovery in 1858 that he gave the British a plan while a prisoner) fixed that image.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "And don't forget the Battle of Bunker Hill. Our patriots fought like heroes there."
  **Today:** Congress chose Washington as commander-in-chief on 15 June 1775, two days *before* Bunker Hill (17 June). The news of the battle reached Philadelphia after he had been appointed, so it could not have been an argument for appointing him.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "To this I pledge my life, my fortune, and my sacred honor."
  **Today:** That phrase closes the Declaration of Independence (July 1776). Washington's acceptance (16 June 1775) is correctly paraphrased in the line before ("I do not think myself equal to the command"). He also refused a salary.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Richard Henry Lee will now read an article which he helped to frame."
  **Today:** Lee moved the resolution for independence (7 June 1776), but the Declaration ("We hold these truths...") was drafted by Thomas Jefferson for the Committee of Five (Adams, Franklin, Jefferson, Livingston, Sherman). Lee was not on that committee and had gone home to Virginia when it was adopted.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "James, would you bring me a lettuce leaf and one of those new fruits from the colonies?"
  **Today:** The Earl of Sandwich did not invent the sandwich during the war. "Sandwich" as a food is already recorded in Edward Gibbon's journal in 1762, and the gambling-table anecdote was printed in 1765. The tomato came from Mexico via Spain, not from Britain's colonies, and was little eaten in Britain in the 1770s. Sandwich (First Lord of the Admiralty) and Germain (Colonial Secretary) are correctly identified.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "I shall leave my 12,000 men, plus our loyalists, as well as Indians,"
  **Today:** Burgoyne set out in June 1777 with about 7,000 regulars (roughly 3,700 British and 3,000 German). With Canadians, Loyalists and about 400–500 Native allies, he had roughly 8,000–9,500 men in all.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "General Gates, General Arnold, I appreciate your extreme generosity."
  **Today:** Benedict Arnold was not at the surrender on 17 October 1777. He had been badly wounded in the leg at Bemis Heights (7 October), was recovering in Albany, and had quarrelled with Gates. Gates accepted the surrender alone.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Franklin did suggest pikes and bows and arrows (letter to Charles Lee, February 1776).
- Ethan Allen's "In the name of the great Jehovah and the Continental Congress" at Ticonderoga (10 May 1775), as told in his own memoir. Knox brought 59 of the captured guns to Boston. "a hundred cannon" is round but not far off.
- The young Washington breaking a horse echoes a family anecdote (from G.W.P. Custis), which cannot be confirmed. His career as surveyor, officer in the French and Indian War, colonel, husband (1759) and Mount Vernon planter is right. Mount Vernon's workforce was enslaved, which the episode does not mention.
- "June 1770, Battle of Bunker Hill" is very likely a mishearing of "June 17th" (1775), which is correct.
- Washington's problems in 1775: about 14,000 men, a powder shortage (about 36 barrels in August 1775), regional quarrels, officers elected by their men and short enlistments.
- Boston was freed when the British left in March 1776. Washington was in New York, not Philadelphia, in July 1776. Howe's force of over 30,000 included German (Hessian) troops.
- The New York fire (21 September 1776): Washington had wanted to burn the city and wrote that "Providence, or some good honest fellow, has done more for us than we were disposed to do for ourselves".
- Trenton (night of 25–26 December 1776, with very few American losses) and Princeton (3 January 1777).
- Burgoyne's plan with St. Leger (Mohawk Valley) and Howe (up the Hudson); "Bourgogne" is the French form of Burgoyne. His ban on scalping was partial, and the killing of Jane McCrea (July 1777) is left out. Ticonderoga fell quickly, the advance was very slow, and he surrendered at Saratoga. Gates did hand Burgoyne back his sword. The promise to ship the troops home (the Convention of Saratoga) was later broken by Congress, and the "Convention Army" was held until 1783.
- The French alliance (February 1778) with no separate peace; "Vergine" is Vergennes. Lafayette's role, Howe choosing Philadelphia over helping Burgoyne, Valley Forge (1777–78) and von Steuben's drill.
- Loyalist and Native raids that killed civilians (Wyoming Valley and Cherry Valley, 1778) are shown. The Sullivan expedition's destruction of about 40 Haudenosaunee towns (1779) and the Gnadenhutten massacre of Christian Lenape (1782) are left out, but the narrator's "each learned to hate the other" is fair.
- The fall of Charleston (May 1780, about 5,000 men lost, the worst American defeat) and Camden (August 1780).
- Arnold's £20,000 deal for West Point and Major André hanged rather than shot (October 1780).
- The Yorktown campaign: de Grasse's fleet bound for the Chesapeake, Rochambeau pressing to go south rather than attack New York, the long march, and the surrender on 19 October 1781. Giving France a decisive role is fairer than many US-centred popular accounts.
- The Treaty of Paris (1783), and Washington's inauguration (30 April 1789) shortly before the French Revolution.
