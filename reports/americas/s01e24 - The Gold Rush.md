# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e24: The Gold Rush
The episode follows a group of men from the East and from Oregon who join the California Gold Rush of 1848–49. It shows San Francisco emptying and then booming, the sea and overland routes west, panning and claims, inflated prices, gambling, shootings and claim-jumpers. It ends with California joining the Union in 1850 and a look ahead to the Civil War.

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "There were only 15 women present."
  **Today:** Women were scarce but not that scarce. About 700 women arrived by sea in 1849, out of roughly 40,000 sea-borne arrivals, and San Francisco had grown to about 25,000 people by the end of that year. The 1850 census found women were about 8% of California's non-Native population. The sentence before this one is cut off in the transcript ("out of a total population in San Francisco"), so the dub may have meant something narrower, such as women at one gathering.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- San Francisco as a sleepy Pacific town before 1848 (it had roughly 800–1,000 people), and merchants making fortunes selling pans and tools at hugely inflated prices, as Sam Brannan did.
- "In a few days san francisco was turned into a ghost town only twelve people remained": an exaggeration. By June 1848 most of the town's men had left for the diggings and the newspapers shut down, but more than twelve people stayed.
- Gold fever spreading east after 1848 and drawing people of every class. The three routes are shown correctly: round Cape Horn (often five to eight months, not three), across Panama, or overland by wagon.
- Very high prices for food and goods, and express companies charging about $1 per letter.
- "25 million Americans": the 1850 census counted about 23.2 million.
- The frog-race bet with "Calaveras" nods to Mark Twain's "The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County" (1865), which came later than 1849.
- California joining the Union in 1850 (9 September), with the Civil War beginning about ten years later (1861).
- Omission: the episode leaves out the Gold Rush's devastating effect on California's Native peoples. Their population fell from about 150,000 to about 30,000 between 1848 and 1870, through disease, starvation and state-funded killings that many historians now call genocide (for example Benjamin Madley, 2016). It also leaves out Mexican, Chilean and Chinese miners and the 1850 Foreign Miners' Tax. These are omissions, not false statements.
