# Once Upon a Time... The Americas — s01e03: The Conquerors of the Great North
The episode follows the peoples of the Arctic: early Paleo-Eskimo groups and then the Thule, the whale-hunting ancestors of today's Inuit, who spread from Alaska to Greenland and replaced the Dorset culture. It then shows Thorfinn Karlsefni's Norse expedition to Newfoundland, a friendly meeting with the locals that turns sour over a stolen sword, and the Norse withdrawal. It ends by saying that later traders and trappers brought alcohol, violence and disease, which devastated the Inuit.

## Outdated (since 1991)
- **Quote:** "Did they come by land bridges? 10 or 12,000"
  **Today:** About 10,000–12,000 years ago (the Clovis era) was the standard date in 1991 for the first people to reach the Americas. Monte Verde, Chile (about 14,500 years, accepted in 1997) and the White Sands footprints, New Mexico (about 21,000–23,000 years, 2021/2023) push the first arrival back to at least 15,000 years ago and probably earlier. By 10,000–12,000 years ago the land bridge was already flooding (about 11,000 years ago).
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "At the same time, somewhere in northern Europe, other men were also feeling"
  **Today:** The episode has the Thule expansion happen at the same time as the Norse voyages of about 1000 AD. In 1991 the Thule migration east was usually dated to about 1000 AD. Better radiocarbon dating (e.g. Friesen & Arnold, *American Antiquity*, 2008) puts the rapid spread from Alaska to Greenland at about 1200–1300 AD, two or three centuries after Leif Erikson and Karlsefni.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "their country and the Eskimos remain the soul masters of the far north."
  **Today:** The people Karlsefni met in Vinland (the sagas call them *Skrælings*) were almost certainly not Inuit. Around 1000 AD the Thule had not yet left Alaska, and the Dorset had already left Newfoundland. The locals were probably ancestors of the Beothuk or Innu (the "Little Passage" culture), forest Algonquian-speaking peoples. In Greenland, Norse and Inuit did meet later, and the Norse Greenland colonies lasted until about 1450. ("soul masters" is a mishearing of "sole masters".)
  **Why it was believed:** Older popular histories used "Skrælings" and "Eskimos" as if they were the same people, and Norse contact with Inuit in Greenland was carried over to Vinland.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "along with their more elementary civilization, absorbed by our ingenious, our courageous"
  **Today:** The Dorset (heard as "Durets") did disappear around 1300–1500 as the Thule spread. But they were not simply "more elementary". They were skilled sea-mammal hunters and produced some of the finest carvings of the prehistoric Arctic. Ancient-DNA work (Raghavan et al., *Science*, 2014) found almost no Dorset ancestry in the Inuit, so "replaced" fits better than "absorbed".
  **Why it was believed:** The idea of technologically "superior" Thule pushing aside a "simpler" people was the usual way of telling the story.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1991)
- **Quote:** "agreed that these were the very first migrants to have populated the American continent."
  **Today:** The opposite is true. The ancestors of the Eskimo-Aleut peoples (Paleo-Eskimos, about 5,000 years ago, and later the Thule) were the **last** major migration from Asia, thousands of years after the first Americans. This was standard by 1991 (e.g. Greenberg, Turner & Zegura's three-wave model, 1986). The French original may have said "the last" and been mistranslated.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "invented the harpoon with a detachable tip and waterproof jackets"
  **Today:** Toggling harpoons with detachable heads are thousands of years older than the Thule. They were used in the Pre-Dorset and Old Bering Sea cultures and in the Maritime Archaic. The Thule's real innovations were open-water whaling from umiaks with harpoon lines and floats, and wider use of dog sleds.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "They will thus cover the 12,000 kilometers that separate Beringia from Greenland,"
  **Today:** From Bering Strait to northwest Greenland is about 3,300 km in a straight line, and to east Greenland about 4,700 km. Coastal routes are longer, but 12,000 km is far too high. This may be a translation or transcription slip.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "40 years later, two ships arrive inside of Newfoundland."
  **Today:** Erik the Red settled Greenland in about 985/986. His son Leif Erikson reached Vinland in about 1000, and Thorfinn Karlsefni ("Torfinn Carl") led his expedition in about 1009–1010, roughly 25 years later. *Eiríks saga* gives Karlsefni three ships and *Grœnlendinga saga* gives him one, never two. (By coincidence, tree-ring dating at L'Anse aux Meadows shows Norse activity in 1021 AD: Kuitems et al., *Nature*, 2021.)
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Straight ahead, guys, there is land, and we are the first to set foot"
  **Today:** In both sagas Leif Erikson (and in *Grœnlendinga saga* his brother Thorvald) had already landed in Vinland before Karlsefni. Karlsefni's expedition used Leif's camp. Leif is missing from the episode.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Paleo-Eskimo skin boats and a second wave of Arctic migrants about 4,000–5,000 years ago ("light-skinned boats" is a mistranslation of "skin boats").
- The Thule coming from the northwest (Alaska), whale hunts with float harpoons, semi-underground winter houses, oil lamps ("lightning" is probably "lighting"), communal caribou hunts, and the men's house that women could not enter (the Alaskan *qargi*).
- The Thule making dog sleds central to Arctic life. The tamed-dog scene is a cartoon: dogs had come to the Americas with people thousands of years earlier, and the Thule brought their own sled-dog lineage.
- The Norse encounter closely follows the sagas: trade in food and goods, Karlsefni forbidding the sale of weapons, a native trying to take a weapon, then violence and the Norse leaving because of constant hostility. This is better than the usual "Vikings conquer" myth.
- The episode ends by blaming later traders, whalers and trappers for alcohol, violence and epidemics, and it says plainly that the Inuit were "decimated". For example, the Mackenzie Delta Inuvialuit fell from about 2,500 to about 150 people by 1910. This is franker than most children's histories of the period.
