# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e13: The Hundred Years' War
The episode covers 14th- and 15th-century Europe: famine, the Black Death and the persecution of Jews, the battles of the Hundred Years' War, and the Great Schism of the papacy. It also covers the reformers Wycliffe, John Ball and Jan Hus, and Joan of Arc. It ends in 1453 with Gutenberg, the fall of Constantinople and the coming Renaissance.

## Outdated (since 1978)
- **Quote:** "In three years, Europe lost 25 million people, one quarter of her population."
  **Today:** The classic figure was "a quarter to a third". Research since the 1990s has pushed estimates up: Ole Benedictow (2004, revised 2021) argues for about 50–60% mortality, and most historians now say at least a third and probably 40–50% or more. The starting total of about 100 million is also too high: most estimates for Europe around 1340 are 70–80 million.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "His right to the throne was attacked most viciously by Isabella of Bavaria, his own mother."
  **Today:** Isabeau did co-sign the Treaty of Troyes (1420), which disinherited the Dauphin, while her husband Charles VI was mentally ill. Her image as a vicious, scheming mother who called her son a bastard has been re-examined, by Rachel Gibbons (1996) and Tracy Adams (2010) among others. Much of it is now seen as later propaganda and legend.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "It seems that a young shepherdess rode on horseback to the Dauphin's castle at Chinon."
  **Today:** Joan was a farmer's daughter. At her trial she said she mostly did housework and only occasionally watched the animals. "Shepherdess" is a legend, pushed by her contemporaries for its biblical echo. (The story that she recognised the hidden Dauphin is in the 1456 testimonies, though the decoy on the throne is a later embellishment.)
  **Why it was believed:** "Joan the shepherdess" was a fixture of French schoolbooks and popular culture from the 15th century to the 20th.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "1343 Pisa, 1347 Prague, Pavia, Warsaw, Vienna, Erfurt, Heidelberg, Cologne, Bologna, 1088, Paris, 1120, ..."
  **Today:** The University of Warsaw was founded in 1816. The 14th-century Polish university was Kraków (1364), which is probably what was meant. Paris has no 1120 foundation either: it grew up around 1150–1200 and got its charter in 1200/1215. (Prague was 1348; Bologna's 1088 is only a traditional date.)
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Harvests were abundant, but Europe's population soon tripled, then quadrupled, until her population reached 100 million."
  **Today:** Between about 1000 and 1300, Europe's population roughly doubled, from about 35–40 million to about 70–80 million (J.C. Russell 1958; McEvedy & Jones 1978). It neither quadrupled nor reached 100 million.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "...the Black Plague, brought in by a Genoese ship from the Crimea in 1348."
  **Today:** The Genoese galleys from Caffa (Crimea) brought plague to Messina in Sicily in October 1347. It reached Marseille and other ports in late 1347 and spread across Europe in 1348.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The Archbishop of Canterbury lost no time in excommunicating him."
  **Today:** Wycliffe was never excommunicated in his lifetime. Archbishop Courtenay's Blackfriars synod (1382) condemned his teachings, and he was pushed out of Oxford. He died in 1384 as parish priest of Lutterworth, still in good standing. He was condemned as a heretic only after his death (Constance, 1415), and his body was dug up and burned in 1428.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "It was John Ball, a priest, who took Wycliffe's place... John Ball ended up in prison."
  **Today:** Ball had preached his radical message since the 1360s on his own account. Chroniclers later linked him to Wycliffe, but he was not his successor. He had been jailed **before** the Peasants' Revolt (rebels freed him in 1381), and after the revolt he was hanged, drawn and quartered in July 1381.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In the same year, in Constantinople, Justinian's ramparts gave way to the Turks."
  **Today:** The land walls breached in 1453 were the **Theodosian** Walls, built under Theodosius II in the 5th century, not walls of Justinian.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- The Avignon papacy; the Great Schism with Urban VI in Rome and Clement VII in Avignon, and correct lists of who backed each; the Council of Pisa leaving three popes.
- Crécy, Poitiers (1356) and Agincourt (1415) as repeated defeats of French cavalry; Hus burned in 1415, the same year as Agincourt.
- Jews blamed and massacred during the plague, while the Pope (Clement VI) tried to protect them.
- Hus as rector of Prague, the safe-conduct from Sigismund, and the Hussite uprising (with an anti-German element).
- Joan at Orléans, the coronation at Reims, and her burning at Rouen in 1431.
- "In 1453, Gutenberg printed the first Bible": the Gutenberg Bible was finished around 1454–55, so this is close enough.
- Byzantine scholars fleeing to Italy "helped" the Renaissance. They did, but it had already begun before 1453.
- Crécy (1346) is mentioned after the plague (1347–51), which blurs the order slightly.
