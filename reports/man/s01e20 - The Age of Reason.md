# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e20: The Age of Reason
The episode surveys the 18th-century Enlightenment through a troupe of French actors touring Europe. It covers the philosophes (Montesquieu, Diderot, Buffon, Rousseau, Voltaire) and the "enlightened despots" Frederick II, Catherine the Great and Maria Theresa, then Britain's early Industrial Revolution and the science of the 1780s. It ends in 1776–1789 with Turgot's failed tax reform and the summoning of the Estates General.

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "since the year 1765 joseph the oldest of 16 of the empress's children was heir to the throne one of his daughters the austrian marie antoinette was to come to a tragic end on the throne of France."
  **Today:** Marie Antoinette was Joseph II's younger **sister**, a daughter of Maria Theresa. Joseph had no surviving children. He was Maria Theresa's eldest son but her fourth child, and in 1765 he became Emperor and co-regent; he had been heir since birth. "His daughters" is probably a dub slip for "her daughters".
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "the Swiss Euler creates integral calculus."
  **Today:** Integral calculus was created by Newton and Leibniz in the later 17th century. Euler greatly extended and systematised it, for example in his *Institutiones calculi integralis* (1768–70). This may be a translation slip for "develops".
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "The wealthy landowners were heavily taxed."
  **Today:** Catherine II favoured the landed nobility. Nobles were exempt from the poll tax, and her Charter to the Nobility (1785) confirmed their freedom from personal taxes and service. The tax burden fell mainly on peasants and townspeople.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "To fight against the miserable condition of the proletariat, the first trade unions were formed as far back as 1776. There were the first strikes."
  **Today:** Neither unions nor strikes began in 1776. British journeymen's "combinations" are recorded from the late 1600s and early 1700s (for example, the London tailors in 1720, which prompted an Act against them in 1721), and strikes go back much further. Sidney and Beatrice Webb's *History of Trade Unionism* (1894) already traced unions to the early 18th century.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Montesquieu, following Locke, arguing for a separation of powers; Diderot favouring science; Buffon putting the Earth's age far beyond 6,000 years; the *Encyclopédie* (first volume 1751) being condemned.
- Rousseau's *Social Contract* as "the Bible of the revolutionaries". It was little read before 1789 (Daniel Mornet's research) but was revered during the Revolution.
- The quote on philosophising is genuinely Madame de Lambert's. Pompadour was Louis XV's favourite for about 19 years (1745–64), followed by du Barry.
- Abbé Nollet's electrical shows; Corsica acquired in 1768, a year before Napoleon's birth in 1769; Cugnot's steam "fardier" (1769–71).
- Frederick II: Bach's visit (1747), Voltaire's three years at Potsdam (1750–53), the seizure of Silesia (1740), keeping Silesia at Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) and pushing potato planting (from 1756).
- The 1772 partition of Poland by Prussia, Russia and Austria. Austria's share (Galicia) was the most populous, but Russia's was the largest in area.
- Catherine taking the throne in 1762 at 33 after Peter III's pro-Prussian turn. Ivan VI reigned about 13 months (1740–41), not "a few months". Diderot visited her in 1773, and the remark about him working on paper and her on human skin is the traditional one.
- Catherine secularising Church lands (1764); Maria Theresa aged 23 in 1740 facing Prussia, France, Spain, Saxony and Bavaria with Kaunitz, Daun and Lacy.
- Mozart played for the imperial family at Schönbrunn as a child (1762), not "often".
- Adam Smith and *laissez-faire* (the slogan is older and French); Hume as an empiricist after Locke; Watt's double-acting engine (1782); Hargreaves, Arkwright, Crompton and Cartwright; coke-smelted iron.
- Turgot's 1776 reforms blocked by the Parlement of Paris in the same year as the Declaration of Independence. The Declaration owed more to Locke and British thinkers than to French philosophy.
- Pitt the Younger becoming prime minister at 24 (1783); Wesley's Methodism, which only became a separate church at the end of his life.
- The "Scottish inventor" line after the Indus and Crete is garbled. It probably refers to McAdam's roads or Cumming's flush toilet (1775).
- The peace of 1783 (Treaty of Paris with the US, signed with the Versailles treaties); Herschel finding Uranus (1781); Lavoisier and Lagrange; Blanchard and Jeffries crossing the Channel by balloon (January 1785); Kant's 1784 essay.
- Voltaire, Diderot, d'Alembert and Rousseau all dead by the mid-1780s; the Estates General called for 5 May 1789.
