# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e05: The First Empires
The episode moves through the 2nd and 1st millennia BC: the Indo-European migrations, Hammurabi's laws, Abraham, the Achaeans, Ramses II at Kadesh, the Dorians, the Phoenician alphabet, Assyria and the fall of Nineveh. It then follows Persia from Zarathustra and Cyrus the Great to Darius I at Persepolis and the Persian Wars, and ends with Alexander the Great's conquest of Persia and his death in Babylon.

## Outdated (since 1978)
- **Quote:** "the Indo-Europeans for lack of a better idea of where they came from."
  **Today:** Ancient-DNA studies have largely settled the question for Europe. Indo-European languages were most likely carried by Yamnaya-related herders from the Pontic–Caspian steppe from about 3000 BC (Haak et al. and Allentoft et al., *Nature* 2015). Lazaridis et al. (*Science* 2022) also point to a possible earlier homeland south of the Caucasus. In 1978 the homeland question was still wide open.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Abraham. The Israelite left Ur and headed towards Canaan."
  **Today:** "Frowned by Abraham" just before is a mishearing of "drowned. Abraham…". Abraham is a figure of religious tradition with no historical or archaeological evidence. After Thomas Thompson (1974) and John Van Seters (1975), and the archaeology that followed, most historians treat the patriarch stories as later legends, not history. The episode presents the journey as a dated historical event.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "In 100 BC, the Dorians invaded, armed with invincible iron."
  **Today:** "100 BC" is a mishearing or slip for about 1100 BC. A "Dorian invasion" bringing iron weapons is no longer accepted. The collapse of Mycenaean Greece around 1200–1100 BC is now explained by several causes (drought, earthquakes, internal unrest, trade breakdown, the "Sea Peoples"), and iron spread gradually through trade. There is little archaeological sign of a Dorian invasion (Chadwick 1976 and later work; Cline, *1177 B.C.*, 2014).
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "For the first time in history, conquered cities were not looted, their inhabitants left unharmed, and a conqueror showed clemency."
  **Today:** Since Amélie Kuhrt's analysis of the Cyrus Cylinder (1983), historians read it as standard Mesopotamian royal propaganda in an old style, not a new humanitarian charter. The Nabonidus Chronicle also records Cyrus massacring people at Opis. Cyrus was relatively tolerant, but he was not the "first" merciful conqueror.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "the sun god Seamus handed down the civilized world's first legal code to Hammurabi"
  **Today:** "Seamus" is a mishearing of Shamash. Earlier law codes exist: the Code of Ur-Nammu (about 2100 BC), the Laws of Lipit-Ishtar (about 1930 BC) and the Laws of Eshnunna. Hammurabi's is the best-preserved and most famous, not the first.
  **Why it was believed:** Hammurabi's stele was found in 1901 and became the textbook "first law code". The Ur-Nammu tablets were published only in 1952, and popular books took decades to catch up.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Smiridus, the magus, an imposter, usurped the Persian throne. And seven of the royal princes decided to eliminate him. This horse, Winnied first, would have to carry out the task."
  **Today:** "Smiridus" is Smerdis/Gaumata, and "Winnied first" means "whinnied first". The story of Darius winning the throne because his horse neighed first is a legend told by Herodotus. Many historians also suspect that Darius made up the "impostor" story in his Behistun inscription to justify seizing the throne from the real Bardiya.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular histories of Persia retold Herodotus' colourful stories and the Behistun account at face value.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "612 bc the medes and the babylonians are now allies led by nebuchadnezzar daily siege to the city of minata"
  **Today:** In 612 BC the Babylonians who besieged Nineveh ("minata") were led by King Nabopolassar, with the Median king Cyaxares. His son Nebuchadnezzar II became king only in 605 BC.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Young Persian Prince Cyrus was educated at Zarathustra's school."
  **Today:** There is no evidence for this; it is an invention. Zoroaster's dates are very uncertain, and many specialists (already Mary Boyce in 1975) place him centuries before Cyrus, perhaps 1500–1000 BC.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "when he decided to take the throne from his dull-witted cousin as he ages the need"
  **Today:** "As he ages the need" is a mishearing of Astyages. Astyages, the Median king whom Cyrus overthrew in about 550 BC, was his grandfather (according to Herodotus), not his cousin.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "It was the birth of the world's first empire."
  **Today:** Earlier empires include Sargon of Akkad's (about 2300 BC), New Kingdom Egypt and Assyria, which this same episode shows. Cyrus' Persia was the largest empire up to its time, not the first.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "from Lebanon, teak from India, gold from Sardis, lapis lazuli from Sagdiana, … At Persopolis, Darius the Great built himself the Palace of Marvel"
  **Today:** This list of materials and craftsmen comes from Darius' foundation charter for his palace at Susa (inscription DSf, found in 1929), not Persepolis. A few items are also garbled: Egypt supplied silver and ebony, and the stone columns came from Elam ("Korea" is a mishearing).
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "There were great battles, Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis. No one was winning, so a truce was called."
  **Today:** The Greeks clearly defeated the Persian invasions at Marathon (490 BC), Salamis (480) and Plataea/Mycale (479). Fighting went on for decades before the peace of about 449 BC (the Peace of Callias), but "no one was winning" misstates the outcome.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Hammurabi's laws on debt service for three years, careless wives thrown into the river, the river ordeal and drowning for adultery, and the three classes of people: broadly accurate paraphrases (§§117, 129, 132, 143).
- The Achaeans of Mycenae taking over Minoan Crete, and Ramses II presenting the near-defeat at Kadesh as a victory: fine.
- Phoenician cities (Tyre, Sidon, Byblos) spreading an alphabet that, through Greek and Latin, became ours: fine.
- Assyrian brutality, the fall of Nineveh in 612 BC, and Nebuchadnezzar making Babylon the great city of its age: fine.
- "Nebuchadnezzar was succeeded by Nabonidus" skips three short reigns (Amel-Marduk, Neriglissar, Labashi-Marduk).
- Cyrus letting the Jews return and rebuild their Temple, and Isaiah calling him "anointed": fine.
- Babylon sending 500 boys as eunuchs (Herodotus) and "remember the Athenians" (a servant reminding Darius; "Jesus" in the transcript is a mishearing of "Darius"): fine as retellings of Herodotus.
- Alexander's dates and battles (Granicus 334 BC, misheard as "4 BC"; Issus 333; Gaugamela 331) and his death in Babylon, possibly from malaria: fine. He died at 32, just short of 33.
