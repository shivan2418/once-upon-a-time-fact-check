# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e22: The French Revolution
The episode runs from the Estates General of 1789 through the Tennis Court Oath, the fall of the Bastille, the night of 4 August and the Declaration of the Rights of Man. It continues with the flight to Varennes, war, the fall of the monarchy, Valmy and the king's trial. It ends with the Terror, Thermidor, the Directory and Bonaparte's rise to command of the Army of Italy.

## Outdated (since 1978)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "it was a split 360 votes for his death 360 votes against it then it was philip of orleans turn to vote... And it was with that one vote, his cousins, the Duke of Orleans, that Louis XVI was condemned to death."
  **Today:** The vote was not a tie broken by Orléans. Of 721 deputies voting on 16–17 January 1793, 361 voted for death without conditions and 26 more for death with a debate on delay (387 in all), against 334 for other penalties. The majority needed was 361, so the unconditional death vote had "a majority of one", but the overall vote for death was clear. A separate vote on a reprieve was defeated 380 to 310.
  **Why it was believed:** The "majority of one vote" became a royalist legend, and Philippe Égalité's vote against his own cousin made him the obvious villain of it.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "today is June the 20th, and we all met at the Jeu de Pomme... Not leave, except by force of bayonets. That's the answer given by Mirabeau to the king's forces."
  **Today:** The Tennis Court Oath was on 20 June 1789, but Mirabeau's famous retort to the master of ceremonies, Dreux-Brézé, came three days later, on 23 June, after the king's royal session. The deputies had also named themselves the National Assembly earlier, on 17 June.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- The Estates General opening on 5 May 1789, the first since 1614 (175 years); Necker dismissed on 11 July and troops gathered around Paris.
- Bastille souvenirs sold from the demolished fortress (Palloy's business). The tricolour cockade was handed to the king at the Hôtel de Ville on 17 July 1789 by Mayor Bailly, with Lafayette nearby; the tricolour flag came later.
- The night of 4 August, with feudal dues to be bought back rather than abolished outright, and the Great Fear; the main principles of the Declaration of the Rights of Man.
- The October Days bringing "the baker, the baker's wife and the baker's boy" to Paris.
- The flight to Varennes (20–21 June 1791); Mozart's death in December 1791; war in April 1792, with Louis hoping for defeat; his veto of the camp of 20,000 fédérés; the Marseillais.
- The Brunswick Manifesto and the 10 August attack on the Tuileries, then the September Massacres. The episode says the monarchy was "voted out" before Brunswick's manifesto; the king was suspended on 10 August, after it.
- Valmy (20 September 1792) was won by the regular army and volunteers, not the National Guard as such; Goethe's famous remark about it.
- "Elected this time by unanimous vote" is likely a mishearing of universal (male) suffrage. The monarchy was abolished on 21 September and the Republic dated from 22 September.
- "1893" is a mishearing of 1793. "The revolt of the masses" is the levée en masse (August 1793).
- Robespierre joined the Committee of Public Safety in July 1793 after Danton left it; "terror is an emanation of virtue" (February 1794); Olympe de Gouges on women mounting the scaffold and the rostrum; Madame Roland's "Liberty, what crimes are committed in thy name".
- Danton was executed about 16 weeks before Robespierre (5 April and 28 July 1794). "Six weeks" may be a mishearing of "sixteen weeks".
- The law of 22 Prairial (June 1794) and Robespierre's fall on 9–10 Thermidor.
- Thermidorian Paris: the waltz, the oft-quoted figure of over 600 balls, fashionable divorce (legal since 1792), Thérésa Tallien's salon and bread rations falling to about 60 grams in 1795.
- Bonaparte's cannon against the royalist rising of 13 Vendémiaire (October 1795) and command of the Army of Italy (March 1796). Barras was a Director, not "head of the army", but he did sponsor Bonaparte.
- The closing "two giants, Russia and the United States" prophecy attributed to Napoleon is hard to source. The well-known version is Tocqueville's (1835).
