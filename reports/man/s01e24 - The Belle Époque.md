# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e24: The Belle Époque
The episode opens with early motor cars and the first motoring contests (Paris–Rouen 1894, Paris–Bordeaux–Paris 1895), then surveys the world around 1900: population, colonial empires, science, art, women's progress and working-class life. It follows the alliances and nationalism that led to the First World War, the war itself and the Russian Revolution, ending with a quote from Rosa Luxemburg.

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "The Entente Cordiale was answered by the Triple Entente."
  **Today:** The Triple Entente (Britain, France and Russia, 1907) grew out of the Franco-Russian alliance (1894) and the Anglo-French Entente Cordiale (1904). It was the answer to the **Triple Alliance** of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy (1882), not to the Entente Cordiale. This is probably a dub or translation slip.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "1900, in France, the elections gave the majority rule to the Republicans."
  **Today:** France held no general election in 1900; the legislative elections were in 1898 and 1902. Republicans had held a majority since 1877–79. The 1902 election was won by the left-republican Bloc des gauches.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- The early motor-car list: Cugnot (1769–70), Gurney's steam carriage (late 1820s), Hancock's *Enterprise* (1833), Rickett (about 1858–60), Bollée's *L'Obéissante* (built 1873, famous run 1875), Panhard-Levassor (1890s).
- "1994 Paris Rouen" is a mishearing of 1894. It was a reliability trial over about 126 km with 50 km qualifying runs, and many odd propulsion systems were entered.
- The June 1895 Paris–Bordeaux–Paris race over about 1,200 km (1,178 km), often called the first true motor race.
- Drake's oil well (1859) and Rockefeller's Standard Oil (1870). Henry Ford's 1896 Quadricycle really was too wide for his workshop door.
- 1900 populations (Europe about 400 million, Asia about 900 million, Africa about 120 million) are in line with standard estimates. The Americas, at about 165 million, were a little above the figure given.
- Leroy-Beaulieu's and Joseph Chamberlain's imperialist quotes; the Berlin Conference (1884–85) setting the "rules" for claiming Africa; about 90% of Africa under European control by 1914.
- Renault's tiny first workshop (1898–99); Freud, Planck, Pavlov, Rutherford and Einstein; early Picasso.
- France's first women lawyers sworn in (December 1900); Marie Curie teaching at the Sorbonne from 1906 and becoming a full professor in 1908; the Paris Métro (1900); early cinema.
- Europe's population nearly doubling from 1850 to 1914 (about 265 million to about 450–480 million) and cities growing much faster.
- "Pope Pius X's address" probably means Catholic social teaching. The key text was Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum* (1891), though Pius X restated it in 1903.
- The French weekly rest-day law (1906) gave a Sunday off, not yet a full "weekend".
- Japan annexing Korea (1910) after defeating Russia (1905).
- Bernhardi's "war is a biological necessity" (*Germany and the Next War*, 1911–12). Houston Stewart Chamberlain was English-born but lived and wrote in Germany; "in England" is misleading.
- German advance troops coming within roughly 20–40 km of Paris before the Marne (September 1914), then trench warfare; tanks and the interrupter gear for firing machine guns through propellers.
- 1917 (US entry, Russian revolutions) and the armistice of 11 November 1918.
- "25 million dead, as many wounded" is high. Current estimates put total war deaths at about 15–22 million, with about 21–23 million military wounded.
- The 1905 revolution after Port Arthur fell, and the Duma conceded and then sidelined. "In 1717" is a mishearing of a war year; Russia's losses of over 7 million men (dead, wounded, captured) are about right.
- Rosa Luxemburg's "Freedom is always freedom for the one who thinks differently" (written 1918).
