# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e21: America
The episode opens with the Mayflower Pilgrims and a sketch of Native American origins and life, then the first Thanksgiving. It then runs through European colonisation (Columbus, Virginia, Quebec, New Amsterdam, the Carolinas, Pennsylvania, Georgia), land deals with Native peoples and the War of Independence. It ends with westward expansion, the Gold Rush, slavery and the start of the Civil War.

## Outdated (since 1978)
- **Quote:** "30 or 40,000 years before our time, the forefathers of the native people came by way of Siberia in a long migration"
  **Today:** People did come from Siberia via Beringia, but the evidence now points to about 15,000–25,000 years ago, not 30,000–40,000. Key evidence includes Monte Verde in Chile (about 14,500 years, accepted from 1997), ancient-DNA studies from the 2010s, and the White Sands footprints in New Mexico (dated about 21,000–23,000 years in 2021 and confirmed in 2023).
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "There were one million living there when the Europeans arrived."
  **Today:** About one million for North America north of Mexico was the old Mooney/Kroeber estimate (1910s–1930s). Henry Dobyns (1966, 1983), Russell Thornton (1987), Douglas Ubelaker and others have raised it, and most estimates now run from about 2 to 7 million, some higher. Disease epidemics that went ahead of the colonists had already cut many populations sharply, including the Wampanoag around Plymouth in 1616–19.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "and buffalo, 60 million buffalo."
  **Today:** The figures of 60 million or more come from the naturalist Ernest Thompson Seton (1909). Studies of how many bison the grasslands could feed, notably Dan Flores (1991) and Andrew Isenberg (2000), suggest the Great Plains held about 25–30 million, fewer during droughts.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "you want our land but what for very curious idea and why not also you buy our water and our rivers clouds in the sky... because they are for everyone"
  **Today:** This echoes the famous "Chief Seattle speech" ("How can you buy or sell the sky?"). That version was largely written by screenwriter Ted Perry in 1971, as shown by Rudolf Kaiser (1987) and others. It also suggests Native peoples had no idea of land rights. Modern ethnohistory shows that Native nations had clear territorial and use rights, though these differed from English private property, and that land deals were often misunderstood or forced.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "there was to be no easy understanding between these two divergent cultures with 10,000 years between them."
  **Today:** Native societies were not "10,000 years behind". The Wampanoag were farmers (maize, beans, squash), had political alliances and a sophisticated economy of their own, and helped the colony survive. Ranking cultures as stages on one ladder of progress has been rejected by anthropologists since Franz Boas (early 20th century).
  **Why it was believed:** 19th-century "stages of civilisation" thinking long survived in popular histories and textbooks.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "The first state was to be Virginia, discovered in 1585 by Sir Walter Raleigh."
  **Today:** Raleigh never went to North America. He **sponsored** the Roanoke voyages: the 1584 scouting trip under Amadas and Barlowe, and the 1585 colony under Grenville and Lane, on what is now the North Carolina coast. The name "Virginia" was given in 1584–85 in honour of Elizabeth. Native peoples (the Algonquian Roanoke and Secotan) already lived there.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular history credited Raleigh as Virginia's "founder" because he held the royal patent.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In 1626, a Dutch sailor, Peter Minuit, buys Manhattan Island from the Indians for $24."
  **Today:** Minuit was the director (governor) of New Netherland, not a sailor. The only contemporary source says the price was trade goods worth 60 guilders. The "$24" is a 19th-century conversion (1840s), worth about $1,000 or more today. The Lenape probably understood the deal as sharing the use of the land, not selling it outright.
  **Why it was believed:** The $24 figure became a staple of American schoolbooks and folklore.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In spite of the law in 1808 forbidding slave trading, the import slave trade is as brisk as ever."
  **Today:** After the 1808 ban, imports of enslaved Africans fell to a small fraction, probably tens of thousands over 50 years (Curtin 1969 estimated about 54,000). What boomed was the **domestic** slave trade, which moved about a million enslaved people from the Upper South to the Deep South. That trade was brutal, and Virginia and Maryland were its main sources.
  **Why it was believed:** W.E.B. Du Bois's 1896 study and abolitionist sources suggested large illegal imports, and older textbooks repeated this.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "they decided to leave for Virginia, that new land in America, where the London Company, under the guidance of John Robinson, installed its first settlers."
  **Today:** John Robinson was the Pilgrims' pastor in Leiden, and he stayed behind. The Virginia (London) Company was led by men such as Sir Edwin Sandys. It had planted Jamestown in 1607, and the Pilgrims had a patent from it.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "They come here on our land to hunt our buffalo." (Native people near the Mayflower landing)
  **Today:** There were no bison in coastal New England. The Wampanoag who met the Pilgrims farmed, fished and hunted deer. The buffalo-centred way of life belongs to the Great Plains, and the horse culture that went with it came later, after Spanish horses spread in the 1600s–1700s. The scene merges very different Native nations into one stereotype.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "To tell the truth, there were explorers before the Mayflower who came to the shores of America. The Vikings with Eric the Red in the 10th century."
  **Today:** Eric the Red settled **Greenland** (from 985/986). It was his son **Leif Erikson** who reached North America, around 1000. Tree-ring dating of wood at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland (Kuitems et al., *Nature*, 2021), places Norse activity there in exactly 1021 AD.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In 1664, an English regiment led by the commander, the Duke of York, approached the city."
  **Today:** The Duke of York (later James II) owned the grant but stayed in England. The fleet was led by Colonel Richard Nicolls.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In 1663, the Carolinas were founded, land of cotton and landed aristocrats."
  **Today:** Colonial Carolina's plantations grew rice, indigo and naval stores. Cotton only took over after Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793), more than a century later.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "new york philadelphia boston already added up to 200 000 souls"
  **Today:** In 1760 the three cities together had roughly 55,000–60,000 people (Philadelphia about 23,000, New York about 18,000, Boston about 15,000).
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "colonists were made to pay taxes and income tax"
  **Today:** The disputed taxes were customs duties and stamp duties (Sugar Act 1764, Stamp Act 1765, Townshend duties 1767, Tea Act 1773). No income tax was imposed; Britain itself only introduced one in 1799. (The transcript is garbled here, so this may be a mishearing.)
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "June 11, 1776, a committee is appointed to draw up a declaration of the rights of man."
  **Today:** The Committee of Five (Adams, Franklin, Jefferson, Livingston, Sherman, all listed correctly) drafted the **Declaration of Independence**. The "Declaration of the Rights of Man" is the French document of 1789. This is probably a translation slip.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "On October the 17th, 1775, the English troops are defeated at Saratoga"
  **Today:** Burgoyne surrendered at Saratoga on 17 October **1777**. That victory brought France into the war in 1778.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- The Pilgrims leaving the Dutch Republic, 102 passengers on the Mayflower, and ending up at Cape Cod instead of the Hudson region.
- The 1621 harvest feast with about 90 Wampanoag men under Massasoit. The modern Thanksgiving holiday owes as much to 19th-century campaigners and Lincoln (1863) as to that feast.
- "Columbus discovered America": the episode itself qualifies this (Bahamas landing, the name "Indians", Vespucci giving the continent its name via Waldseemüller's 1507 map).
- Jamestown in 1607 with three ships; Champlain allying with the Huron (Wendat); New France staying small (about 3,000 in the 1660s).
- Wall Street named after the Dutch wall; Stuyvesant surrendering in 1664; Penn's grant in payment of a royal debt (1681); Georgia (chartered 1732) banning slavery and rum at first.
- About 1.5 million colonists by 1760; the Boston Tea Party (1773); the First Continental Congress (1774); Washington appointed on 15 June 1775; Bunker Hill on 17 June 1775; Franklin suggesting bows and arrows; Howe refusing to negotiate unless the Declaration was revoked.
- Yorktown (19 October 1781): O'Hara offered his sword to the French, who pointed him to Washington. (Washington in turn passed him to General Lincoln.) Lafayette served as a Continental general rather than leading French regiments.
- Territorial growth: Louisiana (1803), Florida (1819), Texas (1845), Oregon (1846), the Mexican Cession (1848). Maine in 1820 was a new state, not new territory.
- Gold discovered by James Wilson Marshall in January 1848; about 9 million people in the Confederate states, about 3.5 million of them enslaved; Fort Sumter on 12 April 1861.
- The episode's tone treats Native peoples as obstacles ("not always so easy to handle") and leaves out removals such as the Trail of Tears. That is an omission rather than a false statement.
