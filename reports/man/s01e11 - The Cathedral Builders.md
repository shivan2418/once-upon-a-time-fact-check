# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e11: The Cathedral Builders
The episode follows a runaway serf into a growing medieval town, from the late 11th century to the 13th. Along the way come the First and Third Crusades (Richard the Lionheart, Saladin), the rise of universities and the multi-generation building of a Gothic cathedral. It ends with the fall of Acre in 1291.

## Outdated (since 1978)
- **Quote:** "All of an animal's strength lies in the shoulder and neck. Anything it wears in that area must not choke the beast."
  **Today:** The episode follows the classic thesis of Lefebvre des Noëttes (1931): ancient "throat-and-girth" harness choked horses, and the rigid collar made a medieval traction revolution possible. Jean Spruytte's practical experiments with replica harness (1977; English edition 1983) and later work showed that ancient harness was not nearly that inefficient. Historians now see the collar as an improvement, not a revolution.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "All three, William II of England, Henry IV of Germany, and Philip I of France, had been excommunicated by the irascible Pope Urban II."
  **Today:** Henry IV and Philip I were excommunicated. William II (Rufus) was not. At the Council of Bari in 1098, Anselm talked Urban II out of excommunicating him.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "According to my figures, with flying buttresses it ought to work." (narration sets the scene in "the late 11th century")
  **Today:** Flying buttresses first appear around the 1160s–1180s (for example Notre-Dame de Paris), after Gothic building began at Saint-Denis in the 1140s. In the late 11th century they would be about 80 years too early.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "People sleep without a stitch on, but surprisingly take their baths fully dressed."
  **Today:** Sleeping naked is well attested. Bathing fully dressed is not: medieval pictures of bathhouses ("stews") routinely show bathers naked.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The solution is a stiff shoulder collar. This will certainly be one of the century's great finds."
  **Today:** The rigid horse collar is shown being invented after the First Crusade (12th century). It was already in use in Europe by the 9th–10th century, and it came from China, where it existed centuries earlier.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Theology, canon law, dialectics, the arts and medicine are taught by masters such as Albert the Great of Germany, Thomas Aquinas from Italy, Abelard from Paris and Roger Bacon the Englishman."
  **Today:** These men did not teach at the same time. Abelard died in 1142, before universities formally existed. Albert, Aquinas and Bacon were 13th-century figures. Yet the scene comes before the Third Crusade (1189–92). Abelard was also from Brittany, not Paris (he taught there). The claim that the university was "open to everyone free of charge" is also wrong: students paid masters' fees, and only men, mostly clerics, could attend.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The emperor Barbarossa is dead. He had just laid siege to Korea, capital of Armenia, when he decided to go swimming."
  **Today:** "Korea" is almost certainly Konya (Iconium), which Barbarossa captured in May 1190. It was the capital of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rûm, not of Armenia. He drowned weeks later in the Saleph river in Armenian Cilicia. He also died in June 1190, a year before Richard reached Acre.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "One of them made the mistake of attacking the Catholic city of Constantinople and looting it."
  **Today:** Constantinople (sacked by the Fourth Crusade in 1204) was the centre of **Orthodox** Christianity. Rome and Constantinople had split in 1054. It was Christian, but not Catholic.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- The feudal insecurity after Charlemagne, serfs escaping to towns, and townspeople winning charters of freedom.
- Urban II's call ("God wills it"), Peter the Hermit, and the capture of Jerusalem in 1099.
- Third Crusade: the siege of Acre (1191), Philip Augustus leaving soon after, Richard turning back from Jerusalem, the dawn fight at Jaffa, and the 1192 treaty allowing pilgrims access (signed 2 September, not the 3rd).
- The fall of Acre in 1291 as the end of the crusader states.
- The romantic "house of the people" picture of cathedral building. Workers were mostly paid professionals, but townspeople did contribute.
