# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e06: The Age of Pericles
The episode starts in Minoan Crete, shown as a rich and peaceful society, then moves to 5th-century BC Athens under Pericles: democracy, theatre (Sophocles' *Antigone*), the Delphic oracle, slavery, the rebuilding of the Acropolis and Spartan education. It ends with a long list of Athenian thinkers, the Peloponnesian War, the death of Socrates, Aristotle tutoring Alexander, and the claim that Greek thought then "slept" for 2,000 years.

## Outdated (since 1978)
- **Quote:** "but no bloodthirsty gods no conquering army no war just happiness prosperity and pleasure"
  **Today:** The picture of peaceful Minoans goes back to Arthur Evans. Evidence found from 1979 onward has overturned it: a probable human sacrifice at the Anemospilia temple (Sakellarakis, 1979), children's bones with cut marks at Knossos (Warren, 1980), and a steadily growing record of weapons, warrior graves and fortifications (e.g. Molloy 2012). Minoan Crete was not free of war or violent ritual.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "the great king Darius I was building magnificent palaces in Persopolis, while somewhere in the Far East lived Buddha and Confucius."
  **Today:** Confucius (551–479 BC) does fit. The Buddha was usually dated 563–483 BC in 1978, but since the Göttingen symposium led by Heinz Bechert (1988, published 1991–92), most scholars place his death around 400 BC, after Darius' time. (Also, India is not the "Far East".)
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Greek philosophy, science and art laid dormant for 2,000 years, only to awaken later like sleeping beauty and continue where they'd left off."
  **Today:** Greek science reached new heights after Alexander, in the Hellenistic world: Euclid, Archimedes, Eratosthenes, Hipparchus and the Library of Alexandria. Rome absorbed Greek learning, Byzantium preserved it, and Islamic scholars translated and extended it (8th–13th centuries) before it reached the Latin West in the 12th century and the Renaissance.
  **Why it was believed:** The older "Dark Ages then Renaissance" story taught in Western schools ignored the Hellenistic, Byzantine and Islamic continuations that specialists knew well.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "But wasn't it McAdam who invented tar for road paving 3,000 years later?"
  **Today:** John Loudon McAdam's "macadam" roads (1820s) used compacted layers of crushed stone without tar. Tar-bound "tarmac" was patented by Edgar Purnell Hooley in 1902.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In 450 BC, Pericles spoke these words. A real democratic state must, without exception, serve the greater number. For each and all, equality before the law."
  **Today:** These ideas come from Pericles' Funeral Oration as recorded by Thucydides (2.37). He gave it in winter 431/430 BC, at the start of the Peloponnesian War, not in 450 BC.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "500 years before our time, Athens was a young democracy."
  **Today:** This should be about 500 BC (Cleisthenes' reforms, 508/507 BC). "Before our time" is a recurring dub slip for "before our era". As spoken, it means around AD 1500.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Crete as an early, wealthy Bronze Age civilisation with plumbing and drains, later taken over by the Mycenaean Achaeans: fine.
- Socrates about 20 and Pericles about 45 in 450 BC: correct.
- The peace with Persia (449 BC), the Thirty Years' Peace with Sparta (446/445 BC), the rebuilding of the Acropolis, and Phidias' Athena and Parthenon: fine.
- Athens (Attica) about the size of Luxembourg, with about 40,000 citizens: fine (adult male citizens, roughly 30,000–60,000).
- The Olympic Games every four years from 776 BC, with 432 BC as the 87th Olympiad: arithmetically right. The 776 BC date itself is traditional.
- The Peloponnesian War from 431 BC, Pericles dying of the plague (429 BC), and Socrates sentenced for impiety and corrupting the young (399 BC): fine.
- The thinkers' slogans: "man is the measure" (Protagoras), *nous* (Anaxagoras), "know thyself" (a Delphic saying linked with Socrates), Democritus and the atom (together with Leucippus), and Hippocrates as the father of medicine: fine.
- The list of exiled or condemned Athenians (Miltiades, Themistocles, Anaxagoras, Thucydides, Protagoras, Phidias and others): broadly matches ancient sources.
- Philip of Macedon uniting Greece in 338 BC, and Aristotle as Alexander's tutor (from 343/342 BC): fine.
