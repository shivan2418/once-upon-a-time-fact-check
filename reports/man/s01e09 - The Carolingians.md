# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e09: The Carolingians
The episode covers the barbarian invasions and the fall of the Western Roman Empire, then Clovis and the Franks (baptism, Salic law fines, the vase of Soissons) and Charles Martel at Poitiers. It shows serfdom and military service under Charlemagne, his envoys (*missi dominici*), the stirrup, his push for schooling and his reforms of writing, measures and money. It ends with the break-up of the empire after the Treaty of Verdun and the Viking raid on Nantes in 843.

## Outdated (since 1978)
- **Quote:** "496, meet Clovis, the only Catholic king in the West"
  **Today:** The traditional date of 496 for Clovis' baptism comes from a reading of Gregory of Tours. Many historians now prefer about 508, or at least a date after 500, based on letters by Avitus of Vienne and others (e.g. Danuta Shanzer 1998; Ian Wood). The date is still debated.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Charlemagne died in the year 814 at the age of 72."
  **Today:** 814 is correct. His birth is now usually placed in 747 or 748, not 742 (Werner 1973; Matthias Becher 1992), which makes him about 66 at death. Biographies around 1978 still usually gave 742.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Quaché, the Arabs came face to face with Charles Martel. Martel had just saved Europe from Islam."
  **Today:** "Quaché" is a mishearing of "In 732". Many historians now see Poitiers/Tours (732 or 733) as defeating a large raid, not as the turning point that "saved Europe". Arab expansion was also slowed by internal conflict (including the Berber revolt of 740), and Arab forces held Narbonne until 759.
  **Why it was believed:** Gibbon's famous passage and French national history made Poitiers a defining battle of civilisation. Specialists began questioning this from the mid-20th century.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "The stirrup, my latest invention. … At last, equipped with stirrups, your cavalry will be invincible."
  **Today:** The stirrup was invented in Asia (paired stirrups in China by the 4th century AD) and reached Europe through steppe peoples such as the Avars. Lynn White Jr.'s thesis (1962) that stirrups gave the Franks unstoppable shock cavalry, and with it feudalism, has been widely rejected (Bachrach 1970 and later). Stirrups are rare in Carolingian finds, and Frankish armies did not depend on them.
  **Why it was believed:** White's *Medieval Technology and Social Change* (1962) was hugely influential, and its "great stirrup controversy" took years to reach general books.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "As of this very moment, it will be compulsory for all clerks and for all children to go to school and learn to read."
  **Today:** Charlemagne's *Admonitio generalis* (789) ordered monasteries and cathedrals to keep schools for boys, mainly to train clergy. There was no compulsory schooling for all children. That came in France only with the Ferry laws of 1881–82.
  **Why it was believed:** The French school myth of Charlemagne as the "inventor of school" (as in the song "Sacré Charlemagne") was a staple of French schoolbooks.
  **Confidence:** high

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "From then on, writing characters would be those we know today. numerals would be Arabic."
  **Today:** The writing part is roughly right: Carolingian minuscule is the ancestor of our lowercase letters. The numerals part is wrong. Charlemagne's world used Roman numerals. Hindu-Arabic numerals first appear in Latin Europe in the late 10th century (the Codex Vigilanus, 976, and Gerbert of Aurillac) and spread mainly after Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* (1202).
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "One pound would equal 20 sous, one sous, 12 deniers. In France, this was the rule till 1789, and the same system exists in England today."
  **Today:** The Carolingian system of £1 = 20 shillings and 1 shilling = 12 pence is correctly described. But Britain switched to decimal currency on 15 February 1971, so it no longer "existed in England today" even when the episode was made in 1978. France replaced the livre with the decimal franc in 1795, a few years after 1789.
  **Confidence:** high

## Simplified but fine
- Waves of Germanic peoples and the Huns under Attila, the Vandal sack of Rome (455, Genseric), and the Visigoths under Euric in Spain and Aquitaine: fine. "Bretons" in the list of invaders is probably a slip for Burgundians or Saxons.
- 3,000 warriors baptised with Clovis (Gregory of Tours), and Clovis as the only Catholic, non-Arian king, which won him the Church's support: fine.
- Salic law's scale of fines, with killing a Frank (200 solidi) costing more than killing a Roman (100): fine.
- The vase of Soissons is an anecdote from Gregory of Tours, told as such. Clovis killing off his royal relatives: fine.
- Serfs, lords taking a share of the harvest, military service tied to landholding, and the *missi dominici* inspecting justice: fine as simplifications.
- The elephant Abul-Abbas, a gift from Harun al-Rashid (arrived 802): fine.
- The empire breaking up after Louis the Pious, the Treaty of Verdun (843) splitting it in three, and Vikings sacking Nantes on 24 June 843: correct.
- Louis the Pious choosing Judith in 819 through a "bride show": this is reported in contemporary sources.
