# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e26: The Earth and Tomorrow
The final episode jumps from the space age into a 1979 warning about the future: population growth, consumerism, pollution, garbage and urban sprawl. It imagines rival nations fighting over resources and waste until a nuclear war leaves only a space colony of survivors. They live on solar energy and wait for the Earth to become habitable again. The narrator admits the vision is deliberately pessimistic.

## Outdated (since 1978)
- **Quote:** "Year 2010, 7 billion inhabitants. Year 2040, 14 billion. Year 2070, 28 billion."
  **Today:** (Prediction.) The 2010 figure came true: the world passed 7 billion around 2011. After that the projection failed. Fertility fell fast in most of the world, and growth slowed from about 2.1% a year in the late 1960s to under 1%. The UN's *World Population Prospects 2024* expects about 9.2 billion around 2040 and a peak of about 10.3 billion in the 2080s, not 14 and 28 billion.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "Decades now, more than 60 nations on this planet have the atomic bomb."
  **Today:** (Prediction.) Nuclear weapons spread far less widely. In 2026 nine states have them (US, Russia, UK, France, China, India, Pakistan, Israel, North Korea). South Africa built and then gave up its bombs, and the 1968 Non-Proliferation Treaty held most countries back.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "T30, one continuous city from Marseille to Genoa, from Paris to Le Havre... From Tokyo to Kyoto, one city of 100 million inhabitants. No more forests, no more trees, all cut down."
  **Today:** (Prediction, probably "in 2030".) With four years to go, nothing like this exists. Urban sprawl grew, but the Riviera, the Seine valley, the Rhine and southern England are not single cities. Japan's Tokyo–Osaka belt holds about 80 million people and is shrinking. Forest cover in Europe grew after 1990, though tropical deforestation went on.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "industry spews into our seas two million three hundred thousand metric tons of lead one million six hundred thousand metric tons of manganese six million five hundred thousand tons of phosphorus"
  **Today:** No time period is given, presumably per year. The lead figure is far too high. Later global inventories (Nriagu & Pacyna, *Nature* 1988) put human lead inputs to all waters at roughly 0.1–0.2 million tonnes a year, and total world lead mining in the 1970s was only about 3.5 million tonnes. Lead pollution then fell steeply once leaded petrol was phased out (1970s–2000s).
  **Confidence:** medium

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Three billion souls were dying of hunger in affluent countries."
  **Today:** The line is garbled (probably "while in the affluent countries..."), but 3 billion hungry people out of about 4.2 billion is far too many. The FAO's *Fourth World Food Survey* (1977) estimated about 450 million seriously undernourished people in the 1970s, roughly one in nine.
  **Why it was believed:** The slogan that "two-thirds of humanity goes hungry", from John Boyd Orr and Josué de Castro around 1950, was repeated for decades after nutrition specialists had rejected it.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "And the Rhine each day spills out 60 million tons of waste into the North Sea. That's equal to one ton per person in the area."
  **Today:** The Rhine's total flow is only about 200 million tonnes of **water** a day, so 60 million tonnes of waste a day is impossible. Its dissolved load (salts and other substances, much of it from industry and mines) was a few tens of millions of tonnes a **year** in the 1970s. Its notorious chloride load was about 30,000–40,000 tonnes a day. "Each day" is probably a slip for "each year", which also fits "one ton per person".
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "the volume of garbage multiplies by 10 each decade"
  **Today:** Waste was rising quickly in the 1960s and 1970s, but by a few percent a year, not tenfold per decade. US municipal waste, for example, grew from about 88 to 121 million tons between 1960 and 1970.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- World population more than doubled in the century before 1979 (in fact it roughly tripled, from about 1.4 billion to 4.3 billion).
- Sputnik (1957), Gagarin (1961), Leonov's spacewalk four years later (1965) and the Moon landing (1969).
- In 1970 the population was growing about 2% a year, a doubling time of about 35 years. The Club of Rome's *Limits to Growth* (1972) is the "symposium" sounding the alarm.
- Past population figures: about 250 million in year 1 and about 450 million in 1500, then faster doublings. The "3 million Cro-Magnons 25,000 years ago" matches Deevey's 1960 estimate for the whole world, not Europe, where people numbered only tens of thousands.
- Birth rates falling in rich countries; at the 1974 Bucharest population conference many developing countries rejected population targets ("development is the best contraceptive").
- The Earth's age of about 5 billion years (now 4.54 billion), and the "50 million times less" arithmetic is consistent.
- People knowing "five centuries ago" that the Earth was round. Educated people had known it since ancient Greece.
- Strontium-90 lingering for decades (half-life about 29 years).
