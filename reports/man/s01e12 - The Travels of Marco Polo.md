# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e12: The Travels of Marco Polo
The episode opens with Temüjin's rise to become Genghis Khan and a quick list of Mongol conquests, then moves to Kublai Khan's request to the Pope and the Polos' second journey east from Acre (1271). It follows them across Persia, Central Asia and the Gobi to Kublai's court, Marco's years of service and the return voyage escorting a princess to Persia. It ends with Marco's tales of Cathay, his capture by the Genoese in 1298 and his influence on Columbus.

## Outdated (since 1978)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Seems to me he might try and use a couple of those catapults I was talking about." (the Polos helping the general besiege "Chang Fu")
  **Today:** This is Marco Polo's own claim that the Polos built siege engines for the siege of Saianfu (Xiangyang). That siege ended in 1273, about two years before the Polos reached Kublai's court (c. 1275). Persian and Chinese sources (Rashid al-Din, the *Yuan Shi*) credit two engineers from the Ilkhanate, Ismail and Ala al-Din, with the counterweight trebuchets. "Chang Fu" is probably a mishearing of Saianfu.
  **Why it was believed:** Polo's book tells the story at length, and popular retellings repeated it. Specialists had flagged the date problem since Henry Yule's edition (1871).
  **Confidence:** high

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "He claimed Khan in 1200."
  **Today:** Temüjin was proclaimed Genghis Khan at the kurultai of **1206**. (An earlier election as khan of his own Mongol following is usually placed around 1189, not 1200.) "1200" may be a mishearing of "1206".
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "In 1209, Tibet."
  **Today:** The 1209 campaign was against **Western Xia** (the Tangut state in north-west China), which submitted that year. Tibet first submitted to the Mongols around 1240–47, under Genghis's successors.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "And if the Pope consents to send me six wise men of the Christian faith, knowledgeable in the seven arts..." (and the haggling scene that follows)
  **Today:** In Polo's text Kublai asks for **a hundred** learned Christians versed in the seven arts. Only two friars, Nicholas of Vicenza and William of Tripoli, were sent, and they turned back early, as the episode shows. The French "cent" (hundred) may have become "six" somewhere along the way, but the haggling joke uses six.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "and 13 ships to take them safely home."
  **Today:** Polo says Kublai fitted out **fourteen** four-masted ships for the voyage. Minor slip.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- "1190", with the West crusading (Third Crusade) while the Mongols rose; Temüjin's rise from a small band. In 1190 the Mongols had not yet left Mongolia; their conquests began after 1206.
- 1207 subjugation of the Siberian forest peoples; invasion of Jin China in 1211; Kara-Khitai (roughly Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan) in 1218; war against the Khwarazmian Empire from 1219 ("Khorasan" was one of its provinces, so this may be a mishearing of Khwarazm) after the Otrar massacre of Mongol envoys and traders. The 200,000-horsemen figure is a traditional one; modern estimates for the Khwarazm campaign are often lower (about 100,000–150,000).
- About 1,300 French knights at Bouvines (27 July 1214), so "only 1,000" is a fair round figure.
- The "greatest pleasure is to defeat one's enemies" creed comes from Rashid al-Din, writing about 80 years after Genghis's death, so it is an attribution rather than a recorded saying.
- Genghis dying in 1227 at about 65 (if born c. 1162; other dates are proposed), after a fall from his horse while hunting (*Secret History*). Kublai was about 12 then, and Genghis is said to have praised him and Hülegü after their first hunt (1224); the deathbed blessing is storytelling.
- The Polo brothers returning with Marco (son of Niccolò, nephew of Maffeo); meeting the papal legate Teobaldo of Piacenza ("Plaisance") at Acre, elected Pope Gregory X in 1271; holy oil from the Holy Sepulchre; friars Nicholas of Vicenza and William of Tripoli turning back in fear of the Mamluks ("sent from Cairo").
- The route (Ararat, Mosul, Baghdad, Hormuz, Khorasan, the Pamirs/Hindu Kush, the desert of Lop/Gobi, Kamul): this follows the places Polo describes. Scholars doubt that the Polos actually passed through Baghdad. Polo's "oil" spring was near Baku, not Mosul.
- Mongol law: ninefold restitution for theft, beatings of 7, 17, 27... up to 107 strokes, and execution by cutting in two for horse theft are all as Polo describes.
- Arrival after about three and a half years. Polo actually says they met Kublai at his summer capital Shangdu (Xanadu), not at Peking (Khanbaliq).
- Kublai's appearance, four wives, more than 10,000 white mares whose milk was reserved (Polo says one other tribe was allowed it); the 6,000-seat hall; paper money from mulberry bark; coal ("black stones"); couvade in Zardandan; 15,000 vessels on the Yangtze; 12,000 bridges at Kinsay (Hangzhou). These are Marco's own claims and exaggerations, and the episode frames them as his tales (a listener even says "This fellow exaggerates"). The Yangtze is not the world's longest river and does not flow through Hangzhou; the transcript is garbled here.
- Marco learning "Tartar" languages; Kublai honouring several religions; 17 years of service; the Persian envoys of Arghun seeking a new bride (Kököchin). The embassy is confirmed by a Chinese document identified by Yang Zhijiu in 1941.
- Away for 24 years (1271–1295); the unrecognised homecoming is a later Venetian legend (Ramusio, 1559).
- Venice–Genoa war and the Battle of Curzola ("Krizola", 1298). Marco's capture there comes from Ramusio, and some scholars place it in a 1296 skirmish instead. He then dictated his book in a Genoese prison.
- "First realistic description of the Far East": earlier friars (Carpini 1245–47, Rubruck 1253–55) had reported on the Mongols, but Polo's account of China was the first detailed one. Doubts about whether he reached China (Frances Wood, 1995) have been largely answered, for example by Hans Ulrich Vogel's study of his currency and salt data (2013).
- Columbus searching for Polo's Cathay; he owned and annotated a printed Latin edition.
