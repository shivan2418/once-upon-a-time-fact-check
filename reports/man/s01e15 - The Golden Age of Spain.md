# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e15: The Golden Age of Spain
The episode covers Spain from the fall of Granada and Columbus's first voyage (1492) through the empire of Charles V. It includes the expulsion of Jews and Muslims, the Inquisition, Magellan's voyage, the conquest of Mexico and Peru, and the Reformation. It ends with Philip II, Lepanto, the Armada and Cervantes.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "The 237 he started with, now there's no doubt, the world is round."
  **Today:** Educated Europeans had known the Earth was a sphere since antiquity. Columbus's critics objected to how **small** he thought the Earth was, not to its shape. Magellan's voyage showed how big the world is and that the oceans connect; it did not settle an argument about whether the Earth is round. The flat-earth myth was traced and debunked in Jeffrey Burton Russell's *Inventing the Flat Earth* (1991).
  **Why it was believed:** 19th-century writers such as Washington Irving (1828) and the "science versus religion" polemicists invented and spread the idea that medieval people thought the world was flat. Schoolbooks repeated it well into the 20th century.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "In that year, there were 12 million Indians in the land. A few years later, Cortes had butchered half of them."
  **Today:** Central Mexico's population did collapse, but mainly from epidemics: smallpox in 1520 and the great *cocoliztli* epidemics of 1545 and 1576. Recent ancient-DNA work (Vågene et al., 2018) points to *Salmonella* Paratyphi C as one cause. War, massacres and forced labour were real and brutal, but Cortés did not personally kill half the population within a few years. Estimates for 1519 range from about 5 to 25 million or more.
  **Why it was believed:** The "Black Legend" of Spanish cruelty goes back to Las Casas. It shaped popular French and English histories, even though demographers such as Borah and Cook (1960s) and Crosby (*The Columbian Exchange*, 1972) had shown disease was the main killer.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "One young soldier lost a hand in battle, not his writing hand." / "the one-armed warrior Cervantes"
  **Today:** Cervantes did not lose his hand at Lepanto. A gunshot wound left his left hand permanently crippled and useless, which is why he was nicknamed "el manco de Lepanto" (the maimed one of Lepanto).
  **Why it was believed:** "Manco" is often translated as "one-armed" or "one-handed", so many popular works say he lost the hand.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "In 1487, Malaga fell. In that same year, Bartomeu Deas rounded the Cape of Good Hope."
  **Today:** Bartolomeu Dias sailed in August 1487 but rounded the Cape in early 1488.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "there would be more conquerors. John Cabot, Americo Vespucci, Pedro Cabral, and Vasco da Gama."
  **Today:** Vasco da Gama sailed to India, never to the Americas. Cabot and Vespucci were explorers, not conquerors.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Prince Charles of Ghent, too Flemish to suit his father, would nonetheless succeed."
  **Today:** Ferdinand of Aragon was Charles's **grandfather**. His father, Philip the Handsome, died in 1506. This may be a mishearing or a dub slip.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "1537, Jacques Cartier discovers and explores Canada. The banner of Francis I waves over Mount Royal."
  **Today:** Cartier's first voyage was in 1534 (when he planted a cross at Gaspé). He reached Hochelaga and named Mount Royal in 1535. Nothing like this happened in 1537.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "In 1543, the Frenchman Ambroise Paré invented the ligation of arteries."
  **Today:** Tying off arteries was known in antiquity (Celsus, Galen). Paré **revived** it for amputations on the battlefield, first around 1552, and described it in print in 1564. 1543 is wrong, and so is "invented".
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The Diet of Augsburg recognized the Protestant religion in late 1554."
  **Today:** The Peace of Augsburg dates from 25 September **1555**.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- The marriage of Isabella and Ferdinand (1469), the fall of Málaga (1487) and Granada (1492), Torquemada and the Inquisition, and the 1492 expulsion of the Jews.
- Columbus aiming for Asia by sailing west; landing on 12 October 1492 about 70 days after leaving Palos. The show itself plays down "discovered America" to "one little Caribbean island".
- Muslims forced to convert or leave despite the 1491 treaty; Charles V born at Ghent in 1500 and taking the Spanish throne at 16, elected emperor in 1519 and crowned by Clement VII in 1530.
- Field of the Cloth of Gold (1520); Suleiman's reign starting in 1520 and his alliance with Francis I; Luther banned after Worms.
- Magellan killed in the Philippines in 1521, with only a handful of men finishing the trip in 1522; Tenochtitlan falling in August 1521; Aztec feather work and warriors taking captives.
- Calvin following Farel in Geneva; Vesalius and Copernicus in 1543; the Council of Trent in 1545; Francis I and Henry VIII both dying in 1547; Charles V's abdication and death in 1558; Lepanto in 1571; the Armada in 1588.
- New World foods (potato, tomato, beans, chocolate, turkey, tobacco).
- Deforestation for shipbuilding: a familiar but simplified claim.
