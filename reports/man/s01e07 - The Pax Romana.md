# Once Upon a Time... Man — s01e07: The Pax Romana
The episode follows Julius Caesar from Vercingetorix's surrender to his rule in Rome. It shows grain handouts and "bread and games", building projects, roads, baths and urban renewal, and the Julian calendar. Daily life in Rome appears through patrons and clients, slave markets, night-time traffic and trade along the Tiber. It ends with the birth of Jesus.

## Outdated (since 1978)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Decades later, a child was conceived, whose birth would mark the year one of our modern era."
  **Today:** Jesus was probably born around 6–4 BC, since Herod the Great, who appears in the Gospel nativity story, died in about 4 BC. The AD count was worked out by Dionysius Exiguus in AD 525, and he was a few years off. There is also no "year zero": 1 BC is followed by AD 1.
  **Why it was believed:** Popular culture assumed the calendar was based on Jesus' exact birth year, even though scholars had known about Dionysius' error for centuries.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1978)
- **Quote:** "Caesar had two semicircular theaters built side by side. His signal, the two enormous buildings, were swung around to form one huge circus. His invention would open up a whole new world in the realm of public entertainment."
  **Today:** Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 36.116–120) credits the rotating twin wooden theatres to Gaius Scribonius Curio, who built them for his father's funeral games in about 52 BC. Curio was Caesar's ally, but the invention was his, not Caesar's.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The glory of Rome, we are going to build roads. … The roads will lead from this column. From this hub, they'll run like spokes over the world."
  **Today:** Roman road-building started centuries before Caesar, with the Via Appia in 312 BC. The "golden milestone" (*Milliarium Aureum*) in the Forum, the symbolic point all roads led to, was set up by Augustus in 20 BC.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Vercingetorix surrendering after Alesia (52 BC; "Elysia" is a mishearing) and Caesar dictating his *Commentaries*: fine.
- Free grain for citizens and "bread and games" (Juvenal's later phrase): fine.
- Rome's many fires in crowded, wooden older districts: fine. A state fire brigade (the *vigiles*) came only under Augustus in AD 6.
- How a Roman road was built in layers (sand, gravel, paving stones), and roads running straight regardless of terrain: fine.
- Caesar's building plans (moving the Tiber, developing the Campus Martius, a new forum): basically right. He built the Forum of Caesar; the Roman Forum itself already existed.
- The Julian calendar of 365 days with a leap year, introduced in 46 BC (taking effect in 45 BC): fine. Today we use its Gregorian revision.
- Patrons and clients with the *sportula* (a handout), slave markets, the seven hills, and wheeled traffic allowed only at night (Caesar's law): fine.
- Educated Romans knowing the Earth was a sphere, and imported goods (Egyptian wheat, Spanish oil, metals, ivory, spices, silk): fine.
- The water clock, presented as a character's "invention", is storytelling. Water clocks were much older.
