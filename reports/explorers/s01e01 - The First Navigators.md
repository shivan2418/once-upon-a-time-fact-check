# Once Upon a Time... The Explorers — s01e01: The First Navigators
The episode surveys the earliest seafaring, from the first sailing boats in Egypt and Mesopotamia through Minoan Crete, the Thera eruption and the Phoenician–Mycenaean rivalry. It then follows three ancient voyages: Pharaoh Necho II's Phoenician circumnavigation of Africa, Hanno of Carthage's trip down the West African coast, and Pytheas of Massalia's journey north to Thule. It closes with the Greek discovery that the Earth is a sphere, from Pythagoras to Eratosthenes, and uses this to show how limited ancient geography was.

## Outdated (since 1996)
- **Quote:** "In Egypt, the oldest examples of sailing craft go back 3,500 years before the common era."
  **Today:** Egyptian pictures of sailing boats from about 3500–3200 BCE (Naqada II pottery) were the textbook "first sail" in 1996, though an Ubaid-period model boat from Eridu with a possible mast socket was already known. Excavations at H3 (As-Sabiyah, Kuwait) from 1998 onward, published by Robert Carter in 2002–06, found Ubaid-period boat remains and a painted disc showing a two-masted sailing boat from about 5500–5000 BCE. The oldest evidence for sail is now from the Gulf and Mesopotamia, about 1,500–2,000 years earlier than the date given. The next line, "That was in Mesopotamia", suggests the script pointed that way too.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "The Caramantes, no-bads, who roamed over North Africa."
  **Today:** The Garamantes ("Caramantes" is a mishearing) were not roaming nomads. They ran a settled, urban, irrigated farming state in the Fezzan (Libya), with its capital at Garama (Germa) and underground water channels (foggaras). Charles Daniels' excavations had begun to show this, but David Mattingly's Fezzan Project (1997–2001) and later work firmly overturned the old nomad picture. Herodotus says they hunted the "cave-dwelling Ethiopians" in four-horse chariots, so "fought against another people" is fair.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "This was the end of the great Minoan civilization of Crete."
  **Today:** The Thera eruption did not end Minoan civilization. Archaeologists had known since the 1980s that it happened in Late Minoan IA, while the Cretan palaces carried on and were destroyed about a century or more later (Late Minoan IB, c. 1450 BCE). Mycenaean Greeks then took over Knossos. Since 1996, radiocarbon dates, especially the buried olive branch from Thera (Friedrich et al., 2006), put the eruption around 1600 BCE, which makes the gap even longer. The eruption probably hurt Minoan trade and coastal sites, but it was not the knockout blow.
  **Why it was believed:** Spyridon Marinatos's 1939 theory that the eruption destroyed Minoan Crete, together with the Atlantis link, dominated popular books and TV documentaries for decades.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "We're gonna be Pygmy's lunch, they'll chop us into the little chest."
  **Today:** Hanno's Periplus does not mention Pygmies, and certainly not man-eating ones. It describes hostile people in animal skins who threw stones, and "hairy people" the interpreters called *Gorillai*, three of whose women the Carthaginians killed and skinned. Central African forest peoples (Mbuti, Aka, Baka and others) were never cannibals, and they lived far from the coast Hanno probably reached. The joke recycles the colonial "cannibal savages" cliché. It is a character speaking, not the narrator.
  **Why it was believed:** Adventure stories, comics and colonial-era writing routinely showed Africans in general, and "Pygmies" in particular, as cannibals.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor

## Wrong (even in 1996)
- **Quote:** "The Phoenicians, in what is now Lebanon, and the Mycenaeans, in Greece."
  **Today:** Minoan Crete's naval peak was about 2000–1450 BCE. The Phoenicians, as a distinct seafaring power in Tyre and Sidon, only appeared after the Bronze Age collapse, around 1200–1100 BCE, by which point Minoan Crete had long since passed to the Mycenaeans. Canaanite ports such as Byblos and Ugarit did trade with Crete and Egypt, so "Phoenician" can be read loosely as Bronze Age Canaanite. But showing Phoenicians fighting Crete for the seas "for centuries" is anachronistic.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "It took place on Thera, one of the islands of Crete."
  **Today:** Thera (Santorini) is a separate island in the Cyclades, about 110 km north of Crete. Its town at Akrotiri was heavily influenced by Minoan culture but did not belong to Crete. The wording may be a dub slip for "near Crete".
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "It's those Mycenaeans. They're better armed than we are."
  **Today:** Hanno and Himilco sailed around the 5th century BCE (perhaps late 6th). Mycenaean civilization had disappeared about 600–700 years earlier, around 1100 BCE. Carthage's sea rivals then were the western Greeks (Phocaeans and Massalia, later Syracuse) and the Etruscans.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Only this time, it was to the north. He encountered the Hyperboreans."
  **Today:** The Hyperboreans were a mythical people "beyond the North Wind", linked to Apollo. Pytheas did not claim to have met them. In the fragments that survive, he describes the tin trade of Cornwall, the Britons and their grain and mead, and Thule six days' sail north of Britain. The narrator's explanation treats Hyperboreans as a real people. Herodotus himself doubted they existed (4.36).
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "He says they're a night six months long!"
  **Today:** A six-month night happens only at the poles. Wherever Thule was (Iceland, the Norwegian coast or Shetland, at about 60–66°N), the winter night lasts at most about a day around the solstice, and in the far north of Norway a few weeks. The "six months" claim comes from Pliny (*Natural History* 4.104) and is not from Pytheas. The fragment Geminus quotes has Pytheas saying only that the summer nights there were two or three hours long. The show does explain correctly that the midnight sun is caused by the Earth's shape.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** major
- **Quote:** "The Earth is round and surrounded by oceans, but Athens is here, right at the center."
  **Today:** This turns Herodotus upside down. He mocked the Ionian maps that drew the Earth "round as if by a compass" with Ocean flowing all around it (4.36), and he said nobody knew whether Europe was bounded by sea to the north and east (4.45). He came from Halicarnassus, and Greeks put the world's centre at Delphi (the *omphalos*), not Athens. He also doubted the Hyperboreans and knew nothing of the Urals, though he did mention elephants and small men in Libya.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- The wheel (about 3500 BCE), the sail and writing (about 3200 BCE) did appear in Egypt and Mesopotamia within a few centuries of one another.
- "Crete... rules the seas": the idea of a Minoan thalassocracy goes back to Thucydides. It is still debated but was a mainstream view.
- Ulysses' ten-year voyage matches the *Odyssey*, which the show rightly presents as a story.
- Necho II (reigned 610–595 BCE) sending Phoenicians around Africa from the Red Sea, with the voyage taking three years, follows Herodotus 4.42. He names no single captain, and historians still argue over whether it happened. About 25,000 km is a reasonable figure for the route.
- Hanno of Carthage sailed about a century after Necho (c. 500 BCE, though the date is uncertain). The Periplus gives him 60 ships and colonists, a much bigger venture than the show's small crew. Himilco led a separate voyage north rather than serving as Hanno's captain.
- Carthage sought West African gold (Herodotus describes the "silent trade") and closed the Strait of Gibraltar (the Pillars of Hercules) to Greek shipping.
- The sailors' fears of a flat Earth with an edge, boiling water and sea monsters fit early Greek (Homeric) cosmology and the "torrid zone" idea. These were ancient sailors, not Columbus's crew, so this is not the Columbus flat-Earth myth.
- Pythagoras is traditionally credited with a spherical Earth (Parmenides is the other candidate). "Nobody would believe him" overstates it, since educated Greeks generally accepted the sphere by Plato's and Aristotle's time.
- Zephyros (the west wind) and Euros (the east wind) are named correctly.
- The Sahara was green and pastoral in the African Humid Period (c. 9000–3000 BCE), well before Hanno's time.
- The mutiny against Hanno is invented. In the Periplus the expedition turned back for lack of food. The crocodile-filled river and Black Africans (probably the Senegal region) are in the text.
- Pytheas of Massalia, an astronomer who measured Marseille's latitude accurately with a gnomon, sailed about 325 BCE, closer to 150–200 years after Hanno than "a century later". His latitude work was precise, but his distances (such as Britain's circumference) were rough.
- The midnight sun and polar winter explained by a spherical Earth is correct and well presented.
- Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference about 240 BCE, roughly a century after Pytheas.
- Thera's ash fell over a wide area, reaching Crete, Rhodes, Anatolia and the Nile delta. "Thousands of miles" overstates the distance (it reached about 1,000 km), but the area covered was vast.
