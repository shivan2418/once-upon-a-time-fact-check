# Once Upon a Time... The Explorers — s01e07: Vasco da Gama
The episode follows Vasco da Gama from a boyhood at sea, through Bartolomeu Dias rounding the Cape (1488), Columbus's rejection in Portugal and the Treaty of Tordesillas, to da Gama's 1497–98 voyage. He makes a wide swing through the South Atlantic, lands near the Cape, gets a pilot at Malindi and reaches the Zamorin's port in India. It teaches why spices drove the search for a sea route and how the Portuguese used the Atlantic wind system.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Well, sir, I'm afraid pepper is worth its weight in pure silver."
  **Today:** Spices were expensive, but "worth its weight in silver (or gold)" is a proverb, not a price. In the late 1400s a pound of pepper in Europe cost a few days' wages of a craftsman, far less than its weight in silver. The follow-up line that nutmeg was "weighed in pure gold" is even further off.
  **Why it was believed:** "Worth its weight in gold" was the standard line about spices in popular histories.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Oh, that's a land discovered by one of my captains."
  **Today:** No evidence shows that Portugal knew of Brazil before Pedro Álvares Cabral landed there in April 1500. The idea that John II secretly knew of it and so pushed the Tordesillas line west to 370 leagues is a speculative theory, the "policy of secrecy". The closing claim, "The Spanish didn't even know it existed, but the Portuguese did", rests on the same theory. (Explaining the name Brazil from brazilwood, *brasa* meaning "ember", is correct.)
  **Why it was believed:** Jaime Cortesão and other Portuguese historians of the 20th century promoted the secrecy theory, and it was repeated in popular books.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Name Ibn Majid."
  **Today:** The Malindi pilot is not named in the Portuguese sources (one calls him a "Moor of Gujarat"). Identifying him as Ahmad ibn Majid comes from a 16th-century Ottoman chronicle and Gabriel Ferrand (1922). Most specialists rejected it by the 1970s, for example G. R. Tibbetts (1971). He was probably a Gujarati Muslim pilot.
  **Why it was believed:** Ibn Majid was the most famous Arab navigator, and the identification made a good story in Arab and Western accounts alike.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1996)
- **Quote:** "in his native Portuguese village near Lisbon"
  **Today:** Da Gama was born in Sines on the Alentejo coast, about 160 km south of Lisbon. It was a small seaport, not really "near Lisbon".
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "My astronomer, Abraham Zakutu Ben Samuel, has brought it up to date,"
  **Today:** In this 1488 scene, Abraham Zacuto was still in Salamanca. He came to Portugal only after the Spanish expulsion of 1492, and he did advise da Gama's voyage under King Manuel. The news from Pêro da Covilhã also probably reached Lisbon only around 1490–91 or later.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "You've made up my mind, Vasco, you will command the exposition to the Indies."
  **Today:** The scene shows John II appointing Vasco and telling "Manuel, my cousin" that he will soon be king. John II died in 1495, and it was **Manuel I** who chose Vasco da Gama in 1497. John II may have had da Gama's father, Estêvão, in mind.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Hmm, you see, the midday sun's practically below the horizon, we are near the Tropic of Capricorn."
  **Today:** In October–November near the Tropic of Capricorn, the midday sun is almost overhead, not near the horizon. Navigators found their latitude from the sun's height at noon with an astrolabe. The line may confuse the sun with the Pole Star, but as dubbed it is wrong.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "Ah, he hopes you like peanuts and saffron in your curry."
  **Today:** This is a joke line, but peanuts are American. They reached East Africa and India only in the 16th century, after the Portuguese brought them.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "something like that. Calcutta in India."
  **Today:** Da Gama landed near **Calicut** (Kozhikode) on the Malabar coast in May 1498. Calcutta (Kolkata) is on the opposite side of India and did not yet exist as a city. The two names are often confused.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "12,000 kilometers, almost 8,000 miles, three"
  **Today:** Da Gama sailed out in a wide loop, the *volta do mar*, so his route from Lisbon to Calicut was close to 20,000 km. 12,000 km is also about 7,500 miles, not "almost 8,000". Columbus's first crossing was about 6,000–7,000 km, so "three times" is right only for the longer figure.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Columbus was born in 1451 and da Gama around 1460–69, so "18 years his senior" fits the traditional 1469 birth date.
- John II sent Pêro da Covilhã (1487) to scout the spice route in disguise. Duties at each port of call did push prices up.
- Fernão Gomes's captains first crossed the equator around 1473–74.
- The Pole Star explanation (the pointer stars, the Little Dipper's handle, the star sinking towards the equator because the Earth is round) is sound.
- Dias rounded the Cape in 1488 and his crew forced him to turn back. "Cape of Storms" later became "Good Hope", although the true southern tip is Cape Agulhas. Columbus was in Lisbon when Dias returned.
- 12 October 1492 after 36 days at sea (from La Gomera) is correct. "He discovered it for the Europeans" is a welcome correction to the old framing.
- Alexander VI (a Spaniard) issued the 1493 bulls with a line 100 leagues west of the Azores and Cape Verde. Tordesillas (1494) moved it to 370 leagues. The kings negotiated through envoys, not face to face.
- Paulo da Gama commanded the *São Rafael*. Navigating by the Southern Cross is right.
- The wide swing into the South Atlantic to catch better winds (about three months and 6,000+ km without sight of land) is correct and well explained. Da Gama made landfall at St Helena Bay, north of the Cape. He also had a compass, astrolabes and sun tables, not "only the stars".
- The friendly then tense contact with the Khoikhoi is right, and so is reaching Dias's turning point about ten years later.
- Mombasa was hostile and Malindi friendly. Mistaking Hindu Indian merchants for "Christians" is in the voyage's journal. Taking a hostage to obtain a pilot is close to what happened.
- The Zamorin of Calicut and da Gama's mission from King Manuel are correct. Zheng He had called at Calicut. Marco Polo visited the Malabar coast, but by sea on his way home from China, not "after crossing the Asian landmass".
- The return voyage was disastrous because of scurvy: roughly a third of the crew came home.
- The young Magellan's cameo is invented, but he did serve Portugal in the East from 1505.
