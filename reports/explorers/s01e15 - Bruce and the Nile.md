# Once Upon a Time... The Explorers — s01e15: Bruce and the Nile
The Scottish laird James Bruce sets out from Cairo for Abyssinia (Ethiopia) to find the source of the Nile. He goes by the Red Sea, Massawa, Adwa and Axum to Gondar, where he treats the royal family and wins favour at the court of Ras Mikael. He then reaches Lake Tana and the Blue Nile. The show explains that he had found only the source of the Blue Nile and that Europe disbelieved his book, and it ends by pointing to the 19th-century explorers of the White Nile.

## Outdated (since 1996)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Then, Master, Bruce discovered the source of the Nile."
  **Today:** The Spanish Jesuit Pedro Páez visited the springs of the Blue Nile (Gish Abay) in 1618, and Jerónimo Lobo described them soon after. Bruce, who reached the springs in November 1770, wrongly played down Páez's claim to have been first. The springs were also a well-known holy site to local Ethiopians. To its credit the episode mentions "the Jesuit P[áez]" at the start and says Bruce found only the Blue Nile source, but it still presents Bruce as the discoverer.
  **Why it was believed:** Bruce's own *Travels* (1790) and British popular histories called him the discoverer of the Blue Nile's source.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** major

## Wrong (even in 1996)
- **Quote:** "I found it. I found it. The waterfall of Tezat,"
  **Today:** The Tis Issat (Blue Nile) Falls are about 30 km downstream of the river's outlet from Lake Tana, so they are not its source. Bruce did see the falls in 1770 with Ras Mikael's army, but the place he claimed as the "source of the Nile" was the Gish Abay springs south of the lake, which he reached in November 1770.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "My son is still fighting."
  **Today:** The "queen mother" (*itege*, "I am Iteg") is the dowager Empress Mentewab. She was the mother of Iyasu II and grandmother of Iyoas, but not the mother of the reigning emperor, Tekle Haymanot II, who was the son of Yohannes II. The smallpox patients Bruce treated belonged to the household of her daughter Weizero Aster ("Esther"), Ras Mikael's wife. "Emperor Amonud" is probably a mishearing.
  **Confidence:** medium
  **Severity:** minor
- **Quote:** "Barden and Speak would reach the source"
  **Today:** Burton and Speke reached Lake Tanganyika together in 1858. Speke then went on **alone** to Lake Victoria while Burton stayed behind ill, and Burton disputed Speke's claim that it was the Nile's source. Speke confirmed the outlet at Ripon Falls in 1862, with Grant.
  **Confidence:** high
  **Severity:** minor

## Simplified but fine
- Cairo ("Pyro") was a cosmopolitan trading city where caravans set out for Mecca, Syria and the Nile route south.
- "Egypt is the gift of the Nile" comes from Herodotus, who visited Egypt around 450 BC, and he took it from Hecataeus. The cataracts above Aswan blocked navigation.
- Bruce was a Scottish landowner (the laird of Kinnaird) rather than a nobleman. Calling the Blue and White Nile "two sources" is a fair simplification.
- Route: Bruce went up the Nile, crossed to Kosseir on the Red Sea, sailed by way of Jidda to Massawa ("Masabar"), and was held up and squeezed for money by the Naib there. The pirate fight appears to be invented.
- His assistant was the Bolognese artist Luigi Balugani ("Bellagani"). Bruce carried a firman from the Sultan and letters from Cairo and the Sharif of Mecca, and he did practise as a doctor.
- Massawa to Adwa took about three weeks, followed by Axum's stelae and Gondar on 14 February 1770, about 91 days after leaving Massawa ("95 days").
- Eating raw meat is real Ethiopian practice (*brundo*, *kitfo*). The line about Abyssinians inventing steak tartare and carpaccio is a joke.
- Ras Mikael Sehul was the real power behind the emperor and fought the Galla (Oromo) and rebel lords. Bruce did impress the court with his marksmanship.
- Bruce came back down the Blue Nile by way of Sennar to its meeting with the White Nile, but Khartoum was not founded until 1821. Speke's identification of the White Nile source came about 90 years later.
- Bruce got home in 1774, but his *Travels* came out only in 1790. They were widely ridiculed, especially the story of cutting steaks from a live cow.
- A French translation came out in 1790–91, and Napoleon's Egyptian campaign (1798) used Bruce's work. The idea that he paid special attention to it is plausible but anecdotal.
- Barth explored the Sahel "Sudan" (Bilad al-Sudan: Timbuktu, Lake Chad) in 1850–55, not the modern country of Sudan. Livingstone came later.
