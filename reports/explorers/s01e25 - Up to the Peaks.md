# Once Upon a Time... The Explorers — s01e25: Up to the Peaks
This episode is in French. It opens with Antoine de Ville's ascent of Mont Aiguille in June 1492, sent by Charles VIII with ladder-makers, stonemasons and clergy. It then skips to Herzog and Lachenal on Annapurna (1950) and spends most of its time on the 1953 British Everest expedition under John Hunt: Kathmandu, the monastery below the mountain, the camps up the Khumbu, oxygen, the failed first attempt and Hillary and Tenzing's summit. Running through it is a yeti subplot (a "scalp" bought in a bazaar, footprints, a lama's tales) that ends with the "yeti" turning out to be a Himalayan bear cub.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Une peur irraisonnée de cet inconnu, de ces monts qui formaient une barrière infranchissable qui divisait l'Europe." [An unreasoning fear of this unknown, of these mountains that formed an impassable barrier dividing Europe.]
  **Today:** The Alps were never impassable. People crossed them from prehistory: Ötzi died on a high pass about 5,300 years ago (found in 1991). Hannibal and the Roman armies crossed them, and medieval merchants, pilgrims and armies used the Great St Bernard, Mont Cenis, Brenner and other passes all the time. Fear of high summits and their legends (dragons, demons) was real, but people did not see the range as a wall.
  **Why it was believed:** "Mountain gloom" was the standard way to introduce the history of mountaineering, as if no one went into mountains before the alpinists. Marjorie Hope Nicolson's *Mountain Gloom and Mountain Glory* (1959) had already shown it was about attitudes to summits, not to travel.

## Simplified but fine
- "L'histoire de l'alpinisme commence véritablement en 1492" [The history of mountaineering really begins in 1492] is the usual French convention. Earlier recorded ascents include the Rocciamelone (3,538 m) in 1358 and Petrarch's Mont Ventoux in 1336.
- Charles VIII's order, the captain Antoine de Ville (lord of Domjulien), and the party with a royal ladder-maker, stonemasons and priests all match the sources. Mont Aiguille (2,085 m, in the Vercors in the Dauphiné) was climbed on 26 June 1492 with ladders.
- The comic notary who stays at the bottom ("Je vais instrumenter ici" [I'll draw up the deed here]) is not far from the record. The Parlement of Grenoble sent a bailiff (huissier) to verify, and the written account was by de Ville's chaplain. There is some doubt about whether the bailiff climbed. ("Mont-Éduille" and "Loisan" are probably mishearings of Mont Aiguille and the Trièves/Oisans area.)
- Maurice Herzog and Louis Lachenal summited Annapurna on 3 June 1950, the first 8,000 m peak to be climbed.
- Everest at 8,848 m was the accepted height in 1996. The 2020 China–Nepal survey gives 8,848.86 m.
- The 1953 expedition gathered in Kathmandu in March and was led by Colonel John Hunt, an army officer. "Signoké" is probably a mishearing of "Tenzing Norgay", who was the expedition's sirdar rather than simply its guide. The episode does not say Hillary was British (he was a New Zealander).
- The "yeti scalps" kept at Himalayan monasteries (Pangboche, Khumjung) existed. The Khumjung scalp was examined in 1960 and turned out to be made from serow hide. "Bédigne" is probably Pangboche or Tengboche.
- The footprint photo "à 6000 mètres" [at 6,000 metres] matches Eric Shipton's 1951 photograph on the Menlung glacier. Ending the yeti hunt with a Himalayan bear cub was ahead of its time: DNA studies of "yeti" samples (2014–2017) mostly identified bears.
- "Chomolungma" is usually translated "Goddess Mother of the World" rather than "of the snows". The translation is debated, so this is fine.
- The monastery camp at about 4,000 m matches Tengboche (about 3,870 m), where the expedition acclimatised.
- The camps match 1953 closely: base camp at about 5,450 m, Camp IV (advanced base) at about 6,460 m, Camp VII on the Lhotse face at about 7,300 m, Camp VIII on the South Col at about 7,900 m, and the last camp at about 8,500 m.
- Finding food left by the 1952 Swiss expeditions, and the Swiss reaching about 8,600 m that year, both fit the record.
- Oxygen: the first assault pair (Bourdillon and Evans) turned back exhausted. The figures of about 1 litre/minute for sleeping, 3 litres/minute for climbing, and 800 litres lasting about 4.5 hours match Hillary's own sums.
- Many earlier attempts had failed with deaths, including Mallory and Irvine in 1924 and the seven porters killed in 1922.
