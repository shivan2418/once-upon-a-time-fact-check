# Once Upon a Time... The Explorers — s01e22: Amundsen, Scott and the South Pole
The episode follows Roald Amundsen from a boy dreaming of the North Pole, through the Belgica's winter trapped in Antarctic ice, to his Northwest Passage voyage in the *Gjøa*, where Inuit teach him dog-driving and fur clothing. It sketches the race for the North Pole (Nansen, Andrée, the Duke of the Abruzzi, Peary), then Amundsen's secret switch to the South Pole in the *Fram* and his victory over Scott, whose party arrived a month later and died on the way back. Along the way it explains why "every direction is south" at the North Pole.

## Outdated (since 1996)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "Robert Perry's been to the North Pole, right?"
  **Today:** Peary's claim to have reached the North Pole on 6 April 1909 has been disputed since 1909 and is now widely doubted. He took no independent navigational witnesses, and his claimed daily distances on the final dash were implausible. Dennis Rawlins (1973) and Wally Herbert (*The Noose of Laurel*, 1989) made strong cases that he fell short, though a 1989 Navigation Foundation study for the National Geographic Society backed him. Frederick Cook's rival 1908 claim is rejected even more widely. Ironically, Amundsen himself was on the airship *Norge* in 1926, the first undisputed sighting of the North Pole.
  **Why it was believed:** Peary's claim was officially recognised by the US Congress and the National Geographic Society, and popular histories repeated it as fact.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1996)
- **Quote:** "1897, the first attempt to explore Antarctica, the South Pole."
  **Today:** The Belgica (1897–99) was the first expedition to *winter* in the Antarctic, by accident, when it became trapped in the ice of the Bellingshausen Sea. It was not the first attempt to explore Antarctica: Cook (1772–75), Bellingshausen (1820), Dumont d'Urville and James Clark Ross (1838–43) and others came first, and Bull and Borchgrevink landed on the continent in 1895. The Belgica was not trying to reach the South Pole. Amundsen was the first mate, not second-in-command (that was Georges Lecointe), and he had already sailed on an Arctic sealer in 1894.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "In 1896, the Swedish explorer Solomon André made another attempt in a hot air balloon and died on the way."
  **Today:** Salomon August Andrée's first try in 1896 was called off when the wind did not cooperate. He took off from Svalbard in July 1897 in the hydrogen balloon *Örnen* (not a hot-air balloon). The crew died on Kvitøya, and their remains and photographs were found in 1930.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "In 1890, Louis Amadei of Savoy came to within 400 kilometers of the pole and disappeared."
  **Today:** Luigi Amedeo, Duke of the Abruzzi, led the 1899–1900 expedition. His officer Umberto Cagni (not the Duke, who had lost fingers to frostbite) reached 86°34′N in April 1900, about 380 km from the Pole. The Duke did not disappear: he lived until 1933. Three men of a support party were lost. "1890" may be a mishearing of 1900.
  **Confidence:** high
- **Quote:** "But Robert Perry's on his way, and Frederick Scott as well."
  **Today:** Peary's rival in the race for the North Pole was the American Frederick Cook. Robert Falcon Scott was preparing for the South Pole. The dub or the transcript has mixed up the two names.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "There's 6,000 meters in altitude."
  **Today:** Amundsen climbed the Axel Heiberg Glacier to the polar plateau at about 3,000 m, and the South Pole stands at about 2,835 m. No Antarctic peak reaches 6,000 m (the highest, Mount Vinson, is 4,892 m). Amundsen himself estimated the nearby peaks at about 4,500 m.
  **Confidence:** high

## Simplified but fine
- At the North Pole every direction is south: correct and nicely explained.
- Nansen crossed the Greenland ice cap in 1888 and then let the *Fram* drift north (1893–96).
- On the Belgica, scurvy, madness among the crew, about 70 sunless days, and seal and penguin meat and fat match the accounts. Dr Frederick Cook and Amundsen effectively took charge.
- The *Gjøa* was a small herring boat bought in 1901 and carried five years of supplies for a crew of seven. Amundsen did slip out of Christiania by night in June 1903 to escape a creditor. Helmer Hanssen sailed with him.
- At Gjøa Haven the Netsilik Inuit taught Amundsen dog-driving and fur clothing, which was key to his later success. Inuit memories of Franklin's men are real, though an English-speaking Inuk is invented.
- The Northeast Passage (Nordenskiöld, 1878–79) and the Northwest Passage (Amundsen, 1903–06) had both been sailed. The Northwest Passage was a route from the Atlantic to the Pacific (Europe to Asia), not "Europe to the Americas".
- Nansen and Johansen left the *Fram* in 1895 and reached 86°14′N, about 420 km from the Pole, on skis with dog sledges. They used kayaks only on open water. The 450 km was their farthest north, not where they left the ship.
- Peary led about eight Arctic expeditions. He was a Navy civil engineer, made rear admiral in 1911.
- The *Fram* did get a new diesel engine for 1910. Amundsen announced a Bering Strait drift, carried about 97 dogs and a prefabricated hut, and told his crew about the switch to the South at Madeira, before (not after) crossing the equator.
- The *Fram* went to the Bay of Whales ("Whale Bay"). Scott used Manchurian ponies, motor sledges and some dogs. Depot-laying took place before the four-month polar night.
- The dogs killed at the "Butcher's Shop" were used to feed the rest, which the show softens.
- Amundsen reached the Pole on 14 December 1911, and Scott's party arrived about 34 days later. All five of Scott's polar party died, though other members of his expedition survived. Amundsen's remark that he had ended up at the opposite pole from the one he dreamed of is authentic.
- The Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station (1956) is named for both.
