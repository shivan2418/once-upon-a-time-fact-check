# Once Upon a Time... The Explorers — s01e09: The Pinzon Brothers
The episode tells Columbus's first voyage from the side of the Pinzón brothers of Palos. Martín Alonso sees a westward map in Rome, Columbus hears a dying pilot's secret on Madeira, and Columbus is turned down by Portugal, England and France and then by a Spanish commission. With the friar of La Rábida's help, the voyage is approved, the Pinzóns recruit the crews, and quarrels and near-mutiny follow before the landfall of October 1492. It teaches that Columbus's discovery depended heavily on the Pinzóns, whom history has partly forgotten.

## Outdated (since 1996)
None found.

## Common belief at the time
- **Quote:** "My name is Alonzo Sanchez. I piloted a ship. I sailed far away."
  **Today:** The "unknown pilot" (*prenauta*) who reached the Caribbean by storm and told Columbus before dying is a legend. Oviedo first reported it in 1535 as an unfounded rumour, and Garcilaso de la Vega gave the pilot the name Alonso Sánchez de Huelva in 1609. No contemporary evidence supports it, and most historians reject it. The show presents it as fact.
  **Why it was believed:** Juan Manzano Manzano's *Colón y su secreto* (1976/1989) revived the story in Spain, and it fitted the long-standing wish to explain Columbus's confidence.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "inquisition they are conversos jews promised to the fire"
  **Today:** The royal offer of pardon to criminals who signed on (April 1492) was taken up by only **four** men: Bartolomé de Torres, convicted of homicide, and three friends who had helped him escape. They were not Inquisition prisoners, and the show's "they're all cutthroats and thieves" is wrong. Most of the crew of about 90 were experienced sailors from Palos, Moguer and Huelva, recruited largely through the Pinzóns, as the episode itself shows.
  **Why it was believed:** "Columbus sailed with a crew of convicts" was a staple of popular accounts.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Wrong (even in 1996)
- **Quote:** "I've seen the architectural plans done by Bramanti and Buenodorti, oh yes, and Michelangelo."
  **Today:** This is anachronistic. Bramante came to Rome only around 1499, the rebuilding of St Peter's began in 1506, and Michelangelo was a teenager around 1490. The Vatican Library itself was founded under Sixtus IV in 1475, so that part fits. (The tradition that Martín Alonso Pinzón saw a document in the papal library comes from his son's testimony in the *pleitos colombinos*.)
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "well this one was drawn by behaim martin behaim"
  **Today:** Martin Behaim's globe was made in Nuremberg in 1492–93, so it could not have been shown in Palos before the voyage left. Toscanelli's map and Pierre d'Ailly's *Imago Mundi* are period-correct.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "This is Louis Torres. The man's a bandit, but he's often very useful."
  **Today:** Luis de Torres was a converted Jew who had served the governor of Murcia. He went as interpreter because he knew Hebrew, Aramaic ("Chaldean") and some Arabic. He was not a bandit. The show seems to mix him up with the convict Bartolomé de Torres.
  **Confidence:** medium
- **Quote:** "to the first man who cites land, I'll give a prize amounted to 10,000 doubloons."
  **Today:** The reward was a lifetime annuity of 10,000 **maravedís** from the Crown, to which Columbus added a silk doublet. Doubloons were later coins, and 10,000 gold coins would have been an enormous sum.
  **Confidence:** medium

## Simplified but fine
- Toscanelli's 1474 letter and map put Asia only a few thousand kilometres west. Columbus figured about 4,400 km from the Canaries to Japan, while the real distance is about 19,000 km.
- Columbus lived on Madeira and Porto Santo around 1479–85 and married Filipa Moniz Perestrelo.
- "No one had any idea that America even existed" leaves out the Norse Vinland voyages (around 1000), which were known from the sagas. The show handles "were people stupid?" fairly.
- John II of Portugal turned him down. Bartholomew Columbus pitched to Henry VII of England and then stayed in France with Anne of Beaujeu, the sister of Charles VIII.
- The commission headed by Hernando de Talavera, Bishop of Ávila, met partly in Salamanca. Its objections closely follow Ferdinand Columbus's account: the ocean too wide, Augustine on the Antipodes, no unknown lands left. The show avoids the "flat Earth" myth.
- Fray Juan Pérez of La Rábida, a former confessor of the queen, helped revive the project.
- Palos was ordered to provide two caravels within ten days (30 April 1492) as a penalty owed to the Crown.
- D'Ailly cited Aristotle on a narrow ocean. Alfraganus's 56⅔ miles per degree gave Columbus a globe of about 20,400 miles, about 25% too small.
- Martín Alonso's promise to share profits, his key role in recruiting crews, and his disputes at sea fit the sources.
- Columbus kept two reckonings of distance, a shorter one shown to the crew.
- The crews grumbled and nearly mutinied (24 September and 10 October). On 6–7 October Pinzón urged steering west-southwest, and Columbus did change course on 7 October, following birds.
- Columbus claimed the reward for a light he saw on 11 October, over Rodrigo de Triana, who sighted land from the *Pinta* at 2 a.m. on 12 October.
- After 1508 the long *pleitos colombinos* lawsuits heard Pinzón-side testimony against Columbus's claims. Palos does have a statue of Martín Alonso Pinzón (1929, moved there in 1945), although Columbus also appears on the town's Departure obelisk.
